 

   The circumstances that led to this telegraphic message about Phileas Fogg were as follows:

The steamer Mongolia, belonging to the Peninsular and Oriental Company, built of iron, with a capacity of two thousand eight hundred tons and five hundred horsepower, was expected at eleven o'clock in the morning on Wednesday, the 9th of October, at Suez. The Mongolia sailed regularly between Brindisi and Bombay via the Suez Canal, and was one of the fastest steamers belonging to the company, always travelling at more than ten knots per hour between Brindisi and Suez, and nine and a half knots between Suez and Bombay.

Two men were walking back and forth on the docks, among the crowd of local people and visitors who were staying in this once small village — now, thanks to the work of M. Lesseps, a fast-growing town. One was the British consul at Suez, who, in spite of the predictions of the English Government and the negative forecasts of Stephenson, was used to watching, from his office window, English ships passing daily back and forth through the great canal that had cut by at least half the long old route from England to India around the Cape of Good Hope. The other was a small, thin-built person, with a nervous, intelligent face, and bright eyes looking out from under eyebrows that he was constantly moving. He was showing clear signs of impatience at that moment, walking nervously up and down, unable to stand still for even a moment. This was Fix, one of the detectives who had been sent from England to find the bank robber. His job was to watch closely every passenger who arrived at Suez, and to follow anyone who seemed suspicious or matched the description of the criminal that he had received two days earlier from police headquarters in London. The detective was clearly driven by the hope of winning the generous reward that would come with success, and he waited with an understandable, feverish impatience for the arrival of the steamer Mongolia.

"So you say, consul," he asked for the twentieth time, "that this steamer is never late?"

"No, Mr. Fix," replied the consul. "She was confirmed at Port Said yesterday, and the rest of the way is nothing to a ship like her. I repeat that the Mongolia has always arrived ahead of the time required by the company's rules, and has won the prize awarded for exceeding the required speed."

"Does she come directly from Brindisi?"

"Directly from Brindisi. She takes on the Indian mail there, and she left there on Saturday at five in the afternoon. Be patient, Mr. Fix. She will not be late. But honestly, I don't see how, from the description you have, you will be able to recognise your man, even if he is on board the Mongolia."

"A man rather feels the presence of these fellows, consul, than recognises them. You have to have a sense for them — a sense that is like a sixth one, combining hearing, sight, and smell. I've arrested more than one of these men in my time, and if my thief is on board, I promise you he will not slip through my fingers."

"I hope so, Mr. Fix. It was a serious robbery."

"A magnificent robbery, consul — fifty-five thousand pounds! We don't often have such lucky cases! Thieves are becoming so small-time nowadays. A man gets hanged for stealing a few coins!"

"Mr. Fix," said the consul, "I like how you talk, and I hope you'll succeed. But I'm afraid you will find it far from easy. Don't you see that the description you have looks very much like that of an honest man?"

"Consul," replied the detective with confidence, "great robbers always look like honest people. Men with dishonest faces have only one choice, and that is to stay honest. Otherwise they would be arrested immediately. The real skill is in seeing through a respectable appearance. It's not an easy task, I admit, but it is a real art."

Mr. Fix was clearly not without a sense of his own importance.

Little by little the scene on the dock became more lively. Sailors of various nationalities, merchants, shipping agents, workers, and locals hurried about as if the steamer were expected to arrive at any moment. The weather was clear and slightly cool. The tall towers of the town's mosques rose above the houses in the pale rays of the sun. A long dock stretched about two thousand yards out into the harbour. A number of fishing boats and coastal vessels, some keeping the old style of ancient galleys, could be seen on the Red Sea.

As he moved among the busy crowd, Fix, as was his habit, examined the passing faces with a sharp, quick glance.

It was now half past ten.

"The steamer doesn't come!" he exclaimed, as the port clock struck.

"She can't be far off now," replied his companion.

"How long will she stop at Suez?"

"Four hours. Long enough to load coal. It is thirteen hundred and ten miles from Suez to Aden, at the other end of the Red Sea, and she needs to take on a fresh supply of coal."

"And does she go from Suez directly to Bombay?"

"Without stopping anywhere."

"Good!" said Fix. "If the robber is on board, he will no doubt get off at Suez, to reach the Dutch or French colonies in Asia by some other route. He should know that he would not be safe for an hour in India, which is English territory."

"Unless," said the consul, "he is exceptionally clever. An English criminal, you know, is always better hidden in London than anywhere else."

This thought gave the detective something to consider. Meanwhile the consul went back to his office. Fix, left alone, was more impatient than ever, feeling a strong sense that the robber was on board the Mongolia. If he had indeed left London intending to reach the New World, he would naturally take the route through India, which was less watched and harder to watch than the Atlantic route. But Fix's thoughts were soon interrupted by a series of sharp whistle blasts, announcing the arrival of the Mongolia. The workers and local people rushed down the dock, and a dozen small boats pushed off from the shore to go and meet the steamer. Soon her enormous body appeared, moving between the harbour banks, and eleven o'clock struck as she anchored in the harbour. She had brought an unusually large number of passengers. Some stayed on deck to look at the attractive view of the town, while most got into the small boats and came ashore onto the dock.

Fix took up a position and carefully examined each face and figure that appeared. Soon one of the passengers, after pushing his way energetically through the crowd of persistent workers, came up to him and politely asked if he could show him the English consulate, at the same time holding out a passport that he wanted to have officially stamped. Fix instinctively took the passport and with a quick glance read the description of its holder. An involuntary movement of surprise almost escaped him, because the description in the passport was identical to the description of the bank robber that he had received from Scotland Yard.

"Is this your passport?" he asked.

"No, it's my master's."

"And your master is—"

"He stayed on board."

"But he must go to the consul's in person in order to prove his identity."

"Oh, is that necessary?"

"Absolutely necessary."

"And where is the consulate?"

"There, on the corner of the square," said Fix, pointing to a building about two hundred steps away.

"I'll go and fetch my master, though he won't be very pleased to be disturbed."

The passenger bowed to Fix and went back to the steamer.
