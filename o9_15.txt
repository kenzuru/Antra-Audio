

The train entered the station, and Passepartout jumped out first, followed by Mr. Fogg, who helped his companion step down. Phileas Fogg intended to go straight to the Hong Kong steamer in order to get Aouda comfortably settled for the voyage. He was unwilling to leave her while they were still in a place that might be dangerous for her.

Just as he was leaving the station, a police officer came up to him and said, "Mr. Phileas Fogg?"

"I am he."

"Is this man your servant?" added the officer, pointing to Passepartout.

"Yes."

"Please, both of you, come with me."

Mr. Fogg showed no surprise at all. The police officer represented the law, and the law is something an Englishman respects. Passepartout tried to argue, but the officer touched him with his stick, and Mr. Fogg signalled to him to obey.

"May this young lady come with us?" he asked.

"She may," replied the officer.

Mr. Fogg, Aouda, and Passepartout were taken to a four-wheeled carriage drawn by two horses, where they took their seats and were driven away. No one spoke during the twenty minutes before they reached their destination. They first passed through the poorer part of the city, with its narrow streets, its dirty, run-down huts, and its poor population. Then they passed through the European part of the city, which looked much better, with its bright brick houses shaded by coconut trees and full of ships' masts. Although it was still early in the morning, well-dressed people on horseback and in fine carriages were already moving back and forth.

The carriage stopped in front of a simple-looking building which did not appear to be a private home. The officer asked his prisoners — for that is truly what they were — to step out, and led them into a room with barred windows, saying: "You will appear before Judge Obadiah at half past eight."

He then left and closed the door.

"Why, we are prisoners!" exclaimed Passepartout, falling into a chair.

Aouda, trying to hide her distress, said to Mr. Fogg: "Sir, you must leave me to my fate! It is because of me that you are treated this way — because you saved me!"

Phileas Fogg simply replied that this was out of the question. It was very unlikely that he would be arrested for stopping a suttee. The people making the complaint would not dare to bring such a charge. There must be some mistake. Besides, he would not in any case leave Aouda, and would escort her to Hong Kong.

"But the steamer leaves at noon!" said Passepartout, nervously.

"We will be on board by noon," replied his master, calmly.

He said it so certainly that Passepartout could not help muttering to himself, "Of course! Before noon we will be on board." But he was far from feeling at ease.

At half past eight the door opened, the officer appeared and, asking them to follow, led the way into a room next door. It was clearly a courtroom, and a crowd of Europeans and local people already filled the back of the room.

Mr. Fogg and his two companions sat on a bench opposite the desks of the judge and his clerk. Shortly after, Judge Obadiah, a fat, round man, entered followed by his clerk. He went to take down a wig that was hanging on a hook, and put it quickly on his head.

"The first case," he said. Then, putting his hand to his head, he cried, "Wait! This is not my wig!"

"No, sir," returned the clerk. "It is mine."

"My dear Mr. Oysterpuff, how can a judge make a wise decision while wearing a clerk's wig?"

The wigs were exchanged.

Passepartout was growing nervous, as the hands of the large clock above the judge seemed to be moving with terrible speed.

"The first case," repeated Judge Obadiah.

"Phileas Fogg?" asked Oysterpuff.

"I am here," replied Mr. Fogg.

"Passepartout?"

"Present," answered Passepartout.

"Good," said the judge. "You have been looked for, prisoners, for two days on the trains from Bombay."

"But what are we accused of?" asked Passepartout, impatiently.

"You are about to be told."

"I am an English subject, sir," said Mr. Fogg, "and I have the right—"

"Have you been badly treated?"

"Not at all."

"Very well. Let the complainants come in."

A door was opened by order of the judge, and three Indian priests entered.

"That's it," muttered Passepartout. "These are the men who were going to burn our young lady."

The priests took their places in front of the judge, and the clerk began to read loudly a complaint of disrespect for a holy place, made against Phileas Fogg and his servant, who were accused of having entered a place sacred to the Brahmin religion.

"You understand the charge?" asked the judge.

"Yes, sir," replied Mr. Fogg, looking at his watch, "and I admit it."

"You admit it?"

"I admit it, and I would like to hear these priests admit, in their turn, what they were about to do at the temple of Pillaji."

The priests looked at each other. They did not seem to understand what had been said.

"Yes!" cried Passepartout warmly. "At the temple of Pillaji, where they were about to burn their victim!"

The judge looked shocked, and the priests were amazed.

"What victim?" said Judge Obadiah. "Burn whom? In Bombay itself?"

"Bombay?" cried Passepartout.

"Certainly. We are not talking about the temple of Pillaji, but the temple of Malabar Hill, in Bombay."

"And as proof," added the clerk, "here are the very shoes left behind by the person who entered without permission."

He then placed a pair of shoes on his desk.

"My shoes!" cried Passepartout, surprised enough to say these words out loud without thinking.

One can imagine the confusion of both master and servant, who had completely forgotten the incident at Bombay — the very reason they were now being held in Calcutta.

Fix the detective had seen the advantage given to him by Passepartout's careless entry into the temple. Delaying his departure by twelve hours, he had gone to see the priests of Malabar Hill. Knowing that the English authorities dealt very harshly with this kind of offence, he promised them a good sum in compensation, and sent them ahead to Calcutta on the next train. Because of the delay caused by the rescue of the young widow, Fix and the priests arrived in the Indian capital before Mr. Fogg and his servant, and the judges had already been warned by telegraph to arrest them if they arrived. Fix's disappointment when he learned that Phileas Fogg had not yet appeared in Calcutta can be imagined. He decided that the man he was chasing must have stopped somewhere along the route and taken shelter in the southern provinces. For twenty-four hours Fix watched the station with great anxiety. At last he was rewarded when he saw Mr. Fogg and Passepartout arrive, accompanied by a young woman whose presence he could not explain at all. He quickly called for a police officer, and this is how the group came to be arrested and brought before Judge Obadiah.

Had Passepartout been less worried about other things, he would have noticed the detective sitting in a corner of the courtroom, watching the events with easily understood interest. The warrant he had been waiting for had not arrived at Calcutta, just as it had not arrived at Bombay or Suez.

Judge Obadiah had unfortunately heard Passepartout's careless exclamation, which the poor fellow would have given anything to take back.

"The facts are admitted?" asked the judge.

"Admitted," replied Mr. Fogg, calmly.

"Since," continued the judge, "English law protects equally and firmly the religions of the Indian people, and since the man Passepartout has admitted that he entered without permission the sacred temple of Malabar Hill in Bombay on October 20th, I sentence the said Passepartout to fifteen days in prison and a fine of three hundred pounds."

"Three hundred pounds!" cried Passepartout, shocked by the large amount.

"Silence!" shouted the officer.

"And since," the judge continued, "it has not been proved that the act was not done with the knowledge of the master, and since in any case a master must be held responsible for the acts of his paid servant, I sentence Phileas Fogg to one week in prison and a fine of one hundred and fifty pounds."

Fix quietly rubbed his hands with satisfaction. If Phileas Fogg could be kept in Calcutta for a week, there would be more than enough time for the warrant to arrive. Passepartout was stunned. This sentence would ruin his master. A wager of twenty thousand pounds lost, because he, like a complete fool, had gone into that terrible temple!

Phileas Fogg, as composed as if the judgement had nothing to do with him, did not even raise his eyebrows when it was announced. Just as the clerk was calling the next case, he stood up and said, "I offer bail."

"You have that right," returned the judge.

Fix's blood ran cold, but he recovered his calm when he heard the judge announce that bail would be set at one thousand pounds for each prisoner.

"I will pay it immediately," said Mr. Fogg, taking a roll of banknotes from the carpet bag that Passepartout had with him, and placing them on the clerk's desk.

"This sum will be returned to you when you are released from prison," said the judge. "For now, you are free on bail."

"Come!" said Phileas Fogg to his servant.

"But at least give me back my shoes!" cried Passepartout angrily.

"These are very expensive shoes!" he muttered, as they were handed to him. "More than a thousand pounds each — and they pinch my feet!"

Mr. Fogg, offering his arm to Aouda, then left, followed by the disappointed Passepartout. Fix still hoped that the man he was chasing would not, in the end, leave the two thousand pounds behind and would decide instead to serve his week in prison. He followed Mr. Fogg. That gentleman called a carriage, and the group were soon at one of the harbour docks.

The "Rangoon" was anchored half a mile away in the harbour, its signal of departure already raised at the top of the mast. Eleven o'clock was sounding. Mr. Fogg was an hour ahead of time. Fix watched them leave the carriage and get into a boat heading for the steamer, and stamped his foot with frustration.

"The man is leaving after all!" he exclaimed. "Two thousand pounds wasted! He spends money as freely as a thief! I will follow him to the end of the world if necessary. But at the rate he is spending, the stolen money will soon be all gone."

The detective was not far wrong in thinking this. Since leaving London, between travel costs, bribes, the purchase of the elephant, bail, and fines, Mr. Fogg had already spent more than five thousand pounds along the way, and the share of the recovered bank robbery money promised to the detectives was getting smaller and smaller.
