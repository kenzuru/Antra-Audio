

  Like sunshine after a storm were the peaceful weeks that followed. The sick ones got better quickly, and Mr. March began to talk about coming home early in the new year. Beth was soon able to lie on the sofa in the study all day, keeping herself busy with her beloved cats at first, and later with doll's sewing, which had fallen far behind. Her legs had been so weak and stiff that Jo carried her around the house in her strong arms every day for some fresh air. Meg cheerfully burned and dirtied her white hands cooking special food for 'the dear', while Amy, a loyal servant of her ring, celebrated Beth's recovery by giving away as many of her own things as she could get her sisters to accept.

As Christmas came closer, the usual secrets began to fill the house, and Jo often made the family laugh by suggesting completely impossible or wonderfully silly ceremonies in honor of this particularly happy Christmas. Laurie was equally impractical, and would have had bonfires, fireworks, and celebration arches, if he had had his way. After many arguments and being told no, the two eager planners were thought to have finally calmed down and went around with sad faces, which were proven wrong by bursts of laughter whenever the two of them were together.

Several days of unusually mild weather perfectly introduced a beautiful Christmas Day. Hannah felt in her heart that it was going to be an unusually good day, and she proved herself right, because everybody and everything seemed to work together to make a great success. To begin with, Mr. March wrote that he would be home soon. Then Beth felt especially well that morning, and, dressed in her mother's gift — a soft dark red warm robe — was carried with great celebration to the window to see the gift from Jo and Laurie. The two of them had worked their hardest to deserve their nickname, because like little elves they had worked at night and created a funny surprise. In the garden stood a tall snow figure, crowned with holly, holding a basket of fruit and flowers in one hand, a large roll of music in the other, a colorful blanket around her cold shoulders, and a Christmas song coming from her lips on a pink paper ribbon.

THE SNOW MAIDEN TO BETH

God bless you, dear Queen Bess!
May nothing you dismay,
But health and peace and happiness
Be yours, this Christmas day.

Here's fruit to feed our busy bee,
And flowers for her nose.
Here's music for her pianee,
An afghan for her toes,

A portrait of Joanna, see,
By Raphael No. 2,
Who laboured with great industry
To make it fair and true.

Accept a ribbon red, I beg,
For Madam Purrer's tail,
And ice cream made by lovely Peg,
A Mont Blanc in a pail.

Their dearest love my makers laid
Within my breast of snow.
Accept it, and the Alpine maid,
From Laurie and from Jo.

How Beth laughed when she saw it, how Laurie ran up and down to bring in the presents, and what funny speeches Jo made as she presented them.

"I am so full of happiness, that if Father were only here, I could not hold one drop more," said Beth, sighing contentedly as Jo carried her to the study to rest after all the excitement, and to enjoy some of the delicious grapes the snow maiden had sent her.

"So am I," added Jo, touching the pocket where her long-wanted books were kept.

"I am sure I am," echoed Amy, looking closely at the beautiful printed copy of the Madonna and Child, which her mother had given her in a pretty frame.

"Of course I am!" cried Meg, smoothing the shiny folds of her first silk dress, because Mr. Laurence had insisted on giving it. "How could I feel otherwise?" said Mrs. March gratefully, as her eyes moved from her husband's letter to Beth's smiling face, and her hand touched the brooch made of their different hair colors, which the girls had just fastened on her chest.

Sometimes, in this ordinary world, things do happen in the wonderful way they do in stories, and what a comfort that is. Half an hour after everyone had said they were so happy they could not hold one drop more, the drop came. Laurie opened the sitting room door and put his head in very quietly. He might just as well have turned a somersault and shouted, because his face was so full of excitement he could not hide, and his voice was so joyfully unsteady, that everyone jumped up, even though he only said, in a strange, breathless voice, "Here is another Christmas present for the March family."

Before the words were fully out of his mouth, he was somehow moved aside, and in his place appeared a tall man, wrapped up to his eyes, leaning on the arm of another tall man, who tried to say something and could not. Of course everyone rushed forward, and for several minutes everybody seemed to lose their senses, because the strangest things were done, and no one said a word.

Mr. March disappeared into the arms of four loving pairs. Jo embarrassed herself by nearly fainting, and had to be helped by Laurie in the other room. Mr. Brooke kissed Meg completely by accident, as he tried to explain. And Amy, who was usually so dignified, tripped over a stool, and not stopping to get up, hugged and cried over her father's boots in the most touching way. Mrs. March was the first to collect herself, and held up her hand with a warning, "Quiet! Remember Beth."

But it was too late. The study door flew open, the little red robe appeared in the doorway, joy gave strength to the weak legs, and Beth ran straight into her father's arms. Never mind what happened just after that, because the full hearts overflowed, washing away the pain of the past and leaving only the sweetness of the present.

It was not romantic at all, but a big laugh set everyone right again, because Hannah was found behind the door, crying over the large turkey, which she had forgotten to put down when she ran up from the kitchen. As the laughter died down, Mrs. March began to thank Mr. Brooke for taking such good care of her husband, at which Mr. Brooke suddenly remembered that Mr. March needed rest, and taking Laurie, he quickly left. Then the two sick ones were told to rest, which they did, by both sitting in one big chair and talking happily.

Mr. March told how much he had wanted to surprise them, and how, when the good weather came, his doctor had allowed him to take advantage of it, how devoted Brooke had been, and how he was truly a wonderful and honest young man. Mr. March paused for a moment just there, and after looking at Meg, who was busily poking the fire, looked at his wife with a questioning raise of his eyebrows — I will let you guess what that meant. Also why Mrs. March gently nodded her head and asked, rather quickly, if he would not like something to eat. Jo saw and understood the look, and she walked away stiffly to get wine and soup, saying to herself as she closed the door firmly, "I hate admirable young men with brown eyes!"

There was never such a Christmas dinner as they had that day. The large turkey was wonderful to see, when Hannah sent it up, stuffed, browned, and decorated. So was the plum pudding, which melted in the mouth, and so were the jellies, in which Amy enjoyed herself like a fly in a jar of honey. Everything went well, which was a relief, Hannah said, "Because my mind was so confused, madam, that it is a miracle I did not roast the pudding, and stuff the turkey with raisins, let alone boil it in a cloth."

Mr. Laurence and his grandson had dinner with them, and so did Mr. Brooke, at whom Jo frowned darkly, to Laurie's great amusement. Two comfortable chairs stood side by side at the head of the table, in which Beth and her father sat, eating modestly on chicken and a little fruit. They drank to each other's health, told stories, sang songs, remembered old times, as older people say, and had a wonderfully good time. A sleigh ride had been planned, but the girls would not leave their father, so the guests left early, and as the evening came, the happy family sat together around the fire.

"Just a year ago we were complaining about the sad Christmas we expected to have. Do you remember?" asked Jo, breaking a short silence that had followed a long conversation about many things.

"Rather a pleasant year on the whole!" said Meg, smiling at the fire, and feeling glad she had treated Mr. Brooke with dignity.

"I think it has been quite a hard one," said Amy, watching the light shine on her ring with thoughtful eyes.

"I am glad it is over, because we have got you back," whispered Beth, who was sitting on her father's knee.

"It has been quite a difficult road for you to travel, my little pilgrims, especially the second half. But you have managed well, and I think the heavy loads are close to falling off," said Mr. March, looking with fatherly satisfaction at the four young faces around him.

"How do you know? Did Mother tell you?" asked Jo.

"Not much. Small signs show which way things are going, and I have made several discoveries today."

"Oh, tell us what they are!" cried Meg, who was sitting next to him.

"Here is one." And taking up the hand that lay on the arm of his chair, he pointed to the rough finger, a burn on the back, and two or three small hard spots on the palm. "I remember a time when this hand was white and smooth, and your first concern was to keep it that way. It was very pretty then, but to me it is much prettier now, because in these small marks I read a little story. Something has been given up for the sake of appearance, this hardened palm has earned something better than blisters, and I am sure the sewing done by these little pricked fingers will last a long time, so much love went into the stitches. Meg, my dear, I value the practical skill that keeps a home happy more than pretty hands or fashionable talents. I am proud to shake this good, hardworking little hand, and I hope I shall not soon be asked to give it away."

If Meg had wanted a reward for hours of patient work, she received it in the warm squeeze of her father's hand and the approving smile he gave her.

"What about Jo? Please say something nice, because she has tried so hard and been so very, very good to me," said Beth in her father's ear.

He laughed and looked across at the tall girl who sat opposite, with an unusually gentle expression on her face.

"Despite the short hair, I do not see the 'son Jo' I left a year ago," said Mr. March. "I see a young lady who fixes her collar straight, ties her boots neatly, and neither whistles, uses slang, nor lies on the rug as she used to do. Her face is a little thin and pale just now, from watching and worrying, but I like to look at it, because it has become gentler, and her voice is softer. She does not move about loudly, but moves quietly, and takes care of a certain little person in a motherly way that delights me. I miss my wild girl a little, but if I am getting a strong, helpful, warm-hearted woman in her place, I shall be very satisfied. I do not know whether the haircut calmed our wild one down, but I do know that in all of Washington I could not find anything beautiful enough to equal the twenty-five dollars my good girl sent me."

Jo's bright eyes were a little wet for a moment, and her thin face went pink in the firelight as she received her father's praise, feeling that she did deserve some of it.

"Now, Beth," said Amy, wanting her turn, but ready to wait.

"There is so little of her, I am afraid to say too much, for fear she will disappear altogether, though she is not as shy as she used to be," began their father cheerfully. But remembering how close he had come to losing her, he held her close, saying tenderly, with her cheek against his own, "I have got you safe, my Beth, and I will keep you so, please God."

After a moment's silence, he looked down at Amy, who sat on a small stool at his feet, and said, gently touching her shining hair,

"I noticed that Amy took the less popular pieces at dinner, ran errands for her mother all afternoon, gave Meg her place tonight, and has looked after everyone with patience and good humor. I also noticed that she does not complain much or look in the mirror, and has not even mentioned a very pretty ring which she wears, so I understand that she has learned to think of other people more and of herself less, and has decided to try and shape her character as carefully as she shapes her little clay figures. I am glad of this, because though I should be very proud of a beautiful statue she makes, I shall be even more proud of a lovable daughter with a talent for making life better for herself and others."

"What are you thinking of, Beth?" asked Jo, when Amy had thanked her father and talked about her ring.

"I read in Pilgrim's Progress today how, after many difficulties, Christian and Hopeful came to a pleasant green field where flowers bloomed all year round, and there they rested happily, as we do now, before they went on to the end of their journey," answered Beth, adding, as she got up from her father's arms and went to the piano, "It is time for singing now, and I want to be in my old place. I will try to sing the song of the shepherd boy which the Pilgrims heard. I made the music for Father, because he likes the words."

So, sitting at the dear little piano, Beth softly touched the keys, and in the sweet voice they had never expected to hear again, sang to her own playing the old hymn, which was a perfectly suitable song for her.

He that is down need fear no fall,
He that is low no pride.
He that is humble ever shall
Have God to be his guide.

I am content with what I have,
Little be it, or much.
And, Lord! Contentment still I crave,
Because Thou savest such.

Fulness to them a burden is,
That go on pilgrimage.
Here little, and hereafter bliss,
Is best from age to age!
