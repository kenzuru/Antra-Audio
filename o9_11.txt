

The train had left on time. Among the passengers were several officers, government workers, and merchants who sold opium and indigo, and who needed to travel to the eastern coast. Passepartout sat in the same carriage as his master, and a third passenger sat across from them. This was Sir Francis Cromarty, one of Mr. Fogg's whist partners from the "Mongolia," who was now travelling to join his army unit at Benares. Sir Francis was a tall, fair man of fifty who had become very well known during the last Sepoy revolt. He had made India his home and only visited England rarely for short periods. He knew the customs, history, and character of India and its people almost as well as someone born there. But Phileas Fogg, who was not really travelling to see the world but only moving along a fixed path around it, made no effort to learn about these things. He was like a solid object moving in a circle around the Earth, following the rules of science. At that moment he was calculating in his mind how many hours had passed since he left London, and if it had been his nature to show his feelings, he would have rubbed his hands together with satisfaction. Sir Francis Cromarty had noticed how unusual his travel companion was — though he had only had the chance to observe him while playing cards — and asked himself whether a human heart really beat inside this cold man, and whether Phileas Fogg could feel the beauty of nature. The general could only admit to himself that, of all the strange people he had ever met, none was equal to this man who lived by exact science.

Phileas Fogg had not hidden from Sir Francis his plan to travel around the world, or the reason he had started the journey. The general saw the bet as nothing more than a useless and foolish idea. The way this strange man was going about things, he would finish his journey without having done any good for himself or anyone else.

An hour after leaving Bombay, the train had crossed the bridges and the Island of Salcette, and had reached open land. At Callyan they came to the junction of a branch line that goes south-east through Kandallah and Pounah. After passing Pauwell, they entered the mountain passes, with their dark stone bases and tops covered in thick green forests. Phileas Fogg and Sir Francis Cromarty spoke a few words from time to time, and now Sir Francis, returning to the conversation, said, "Some years ago, Mr. Fogg, you would have faced a delay at this point that would probably have cost you your wager."

"How so, Sir Francis?"

"Because the railway used to stop at the bottom of these mountains. Passengers had to cross to Kandallah on the other side by small covered chairs carried by people, or on ponies."

"Such a delay would not have changed my plans at all," said Mr. Fogg. "I have always expected that certain problems might arise."

"But, Mr. Fogg," Sir Francis continued, "you risk having some trouble because of what your servant did at the temple." Passepartout, his feet warmly wrapped in his travelling blanket, was fast asleep and had no idea that anyone was talking about him. "The Government is very strict about this kind of offence. It is very careful to make sure that the religious customs of the Indians are respected, and if your servant were caught—"

"Very well, Sir Francis," replied Mr. Fogg. "If he had been caught, he would have been found guilty and punished, and then he would have quietly returned to Europe. I don't see how that would have caused any delay for his master."

The conversation stopped again. During the night the train left the mountains behind and passed Nassik, and the next day it continued across the flat, well-farmed land of Khandeish, with its scattered villages above which rose the towers of temples. This rich land is watered by many small rivers and clear streams, most of which flow into the Godavery River.

Passepartout, waking up and looking out of the window, could not believe that he was really crossing India on a train. The engine, driven by an English engineer and powered by English coal, sent smoke over fields of cotton, coffee, nutmeg, cloves, and pepper, while the steam curled in circles around groups of palm trees. Among the trees could be seen pretty bungalows, viharis — a kind of old abandoned monastery — and wonderful temples decorated with the rich designs of Indian architecture. Then came large areas of land that stretched to the horizon, with forests full of snakes and tigers that ran away from the noise of the train. More forests followed, through which the railway had been built, and in which elephants stood watching the train pass with calm, thoughtful eyes. The travellers crossed, beyond Milligaum, a dangerous area that had often been stained with blood by followers of the goddess Kali. Not far away was Ellora, with its graceful temples, and the famous Aurungabad, once the capital of the cruel Aureng-Zeb, and now the main town of one of the separated parts of the kingdom of Nizam. It was in this area that Feringhea, the leader of the Thuggees, the king of the stranglers, had once ruled. These criminals, connected by a secret bond, killed people of all ages in honour of the goddess of Death, without ever using blood. There was a time when it was almost impossible to travel through this part of the country without finding dead bodies in every direction. The English Government had managed to greatly reduce these killings, though the Thuggees still existed and continued their terrible practices.

At half past twelve the train stopped at Burhampoor, where Passepartout was able to buy some Indian slippers decorated with false pearls. He put them on his feet with obvious pride. The travellers quickly ate breakfast and then set off for Assurghur, after briefly following the bank of the small river Tapty, which flows into the Gulf of Cambray near Surat.

Passepartout was now deep in thought. Until he arrived in Bombay, he had hoped that their journey would end there. But now that they were clearly racing across India at full speed, his thinking had suddenly changed. His old love of wandering returned to him, and the exciting ideas of his younger years came back. He began to believe that his master's plan was a serious one, that the bet was real, and therefore that the journey around the world was real and had to be completed without fail within the set time. He was already starting to worry about possible delays and accidents that might happen on the way. He felt personally connected to the bet and trembled at the thought that he might have been the cause of losing it because of his foolish behaviour the night before. Being much less calm than Mr. Fogg, he was much more restless. He counted and recounted the days that had passed, complained loudly when the train stopped, accused it of being too slow, and in his mind blamed Mr. Fogg for not having paid the engineer to go faster. The good man did not know that while it might be possible to speed up a ship this way, it could not be done on a railway.

The train entered the passes of the Sutpour Mountains, which separate Khandeish from Bundelcund, towards evening. The next day Sir Francis Cromarty asked Passepartout what time it was. After looking at his watch, Passepartout replied that it was three in the morning. This well-known watch, always set to Greenwich time, was now about seventy-seven degrees to the west, and was at least four hours slow. Sir Francis corrected Passepartout's time, and Passepartout replied in the same way he had replied to Fix. When the general insisted that the watch should be adjusted at each new place, since they were always moving east — that is, towards the sun — and so each day was four minutes shorter for each degree travelled, Passepartout firmly refused to change his watch, which he kept on London time. It was a harmless mistake that hurt no one.

The train stopped at eight o'clock in the middle of a clearing about fifteen miles past Rothal, where there were several bungalows and workers' huts. The conductor walked along the carriages and shouted, "Passengers must get out here!"

Phileas Fogg looked at Sir Francis Cromarty for an explanation, but the general could not explain why they were stopping in the middle of this forest of date and acacia trees.

Passepartout, equally surprised, rushed out and quickly came back, shouting: "Sir, there is no more railway!"

"What do you mean?" asked Sir Francis.

"I mean that the train is not going any further."

The general immediately stepped out, while Phileas Fogg calmly followed him, and they both went to speak to the conductor.

"Where are we?" asked Sir Francis.

"At the village of Kholby."

"Do we stop here?"

"Yes. The railway is not finished."

"What! Not finished?"

"No. There are still about fifty miles to be built before Allahabad, where the line starts again."

"But the newspapers said the railway was open all the way through."

"What can I say, officer? The newspapers were wrong."

"Yet you sell tickets from Bombay to Calcutta," replied Sir Francis, who was becoming angry.

"Of course," replied the conductor. "But the passengers know they must find their own way from Kholby to Allahabad."

Sir Francis was furious. Passepartout would gladly have hit the conductor, and he did not dare to look at his master.

"Sir Francis," said Mr. Fogg quietly, "shall we look for some way to get to Allahabad?"

"Mr. Fogg, this delay is very bad for you."

"No, Sir Francis. It was expected."

"What! You knew that the railway—"

"Not at all. But I knew that some problem would eventually appear along the way. Nothing is lost. I had gained two days, which I can now use. A steamer leaves Calcutta for Hong Kong at noon on the 25th. Today is the 22nd, and we will reach Calcutta in time."

There was nothing to say to such a confident reply.

It was unfortunately true that the railway ended at this point. The newspapers were like some watches that run too fast and had too quickly announced that the line was complete. Most of the travellers already knew about this gap, and leaving the train, they began to find whatever transport the village could offer: four-wheeled carriages, wagons pulled by zebus, vehicles that looked like moving temples, covered chairs carried by people, ponies, and anything else available.

Mr. Fogg and Sir Francis Cromarty searched the village from one end to the other, but came back with nothing.

"I will walk," said Phileas Fogg.

Passepartout, who had now rejoined his master, made a face at the thought of his wonderful but delicate Indian shoes. Luckily he had also been looking around, and after a moment said, "Sir, I think I have found a way to travel."

"What?"

"An elephant! An elephant that belongs to an Indian man who lives just a hundred steps from here."

"Let's go and see the elephant," replied Mr. Fogg.

They soon reached a small hut, next to which, behind some tall wooden fences, was the animal. An Indian man came out of the hut and, when they asked, took them inside the fence. The elephant had been raised by its owner not as a working animal but for fighting. The Indian had begun to make it more aggressive, by irritating it often and feeding it sugar and butter every three months — a method often used by those who train Indian elephants for battle. Fortunately for Mr. Fogg, the animal had not been trained very far in this direction, and the elephant still had its natural gentleness. Kiouni — this was the animal's name — could clearly travel fast for a long time, and since there was no other option, Mr. Fogg decided to hire him. But elephants are far from cheap in India, where they are becoming rare. Male elephants, which are the only ones suitable for shows, are much wanted, especially since very few are kept in captivity. When Mr. Fogg offered to hire Kiouni from the Indian, the man immediately refused. Mr. Fogg kept trying, offering the very high price of ten pounds an hour for the use of the animal as far as Allahabad. Refused. Twenty pounds? Refused again. Forty pounds? Still refused. Passepartout reacted to each new offer with surprise, but the Indian refused to be tempted. Yet the offer was very attractive, because if the elephant took fifteen hours to reach Allahabad, the owner would receive no less than six hundred pounds.

Phileas Fogg, without becoming the least bit anxious, then offered to buy the animal outright, offering at first one thousand pounds. The Indian, perhaps thinking he could get even more, still refused.

Sir Francis Cromarty took Mr. Fogg aside and asked him to think carefully before going any further. Mr. Fogg replied that he did not usually act without thinking, that twenty thousand pounds were at stake, that the elephant was absolutely necessary, and that he would get him even if he had to pay twenty times his value. Returning to the Indian, whose small, sharp eyes shone with greed and showed that for him it was only a question of how much he could get, Mr. Fogg offered first twelve hundred, then fifteen hundred, eighteen hundred, two thousand pounds. Passepartout, usually red-faced, had gone completely white with tension.

At two thousand pounds the Indian agreed.

"What a price, for goodness' sake!" cried Passepartout. "For an elephant!"

Now they only needed to find a guide, which was much easier. A young Parsee man with an intelligent face offered his services, which Mr. Fogg accepted, promising a reward generous enough to encourage his best efforts. The elephant was brought out and prepared. The Parsee, who was an experienced elephant driver, covered the animal's back with a kind of saddle cloth and attached some rather uncomfortable seats to each of its sides. Phileas Fogg paid the Indian with some banknotes taken from the famous carpet bag, which seemed to cause poor Passepartout great pain. He then offered to take Sir Francis to Allahabad, which the general gratefully accepted, since one more traveller was unlikely to tire such a large animal. Food was bought at Kholby, and while Sir Francis and Mr. Fogg sat in the seats on either side, Passepartout climbed on the saddle cloth between them. The Parsee sat on the elephant's neck, and at nine o'clock they left the village, with the animal moving quickly through the thick palm forest by the shortest path.
