

The weather was bad during the last days of the voyage. The wind, stubbornly staying in the north-west, blew strongly and slowed the steamer. The "Rangoon" rocked heavily, and the passengers grew tired of the long, enormous waves that rose up in the ship's path. A kind of storm began on November 3rd. The violent wind threw the vessel around, and high waves crashed against it. The "Rangoon" brought in all her sails, and even the ropes shook and whistled in the wind. The steamer was forced to move slowly, and the captain estimated that she would arrive at Hong Kong twenty hours late — or more if the storm continued.

Phileas Fogg looked at the rough sea, which seemed to be working especially hard to delay him, with his usual calm. His expression did not change for a moment, even though a delay of twenty hours — by making him miss the Yokohama boat — would almost certainly mean losing the wager. But this man of steady nerves showed neither impatience nor frustration. It seemed as though the storm was part of his plan and had been expected all along. Aouda was amazed to find him as calm as he had been from the first moment she saw him.

Fix did not see the situation in the same way. The storm pleased him greatly. His satisfaction would have been complete if the "Rangoon" had been forced to turn back in the face of the wind and waves. Every delay filled him with hope, since it was becoming more and more likely that Fogg would have to stay several days in Hong Kong. And now the sky itself seemed to be helping him, with its gusts and storms. It did not matter that they made him seasick — he paid no attention to this discomfort. While his body suffered, his spirit leapt with hopeful joy.

Passepartout was furious beyond words at the bad weather. Everything had gone so well until now! The land and sea had seemed to be at his master's service. Steamers and trains had obeyed him. Wind and steam had come together to speed his journey. Had the time of trouble now arrived? Passepartout was as upset as if the twenty thousand pounds were going to come from his own pocket. The storm made him angry, the gale drove him mad, and he longed to force the stubborn sea to behave. Poor fellow! Fix carefully hid his own satisfaction from him, for if he had let it show, Passepartout would have found it very hard not to attack him physically.

Passepartout stayed on deck as long as the storm lasted, unable to keep still below. He decided to help the ship move faster by lending a hand to the crew. He asked the captain, officers, and sailors — who could not help laughing at his desperation — every kind of question. He wanted to know exactly how long the storm would last, and was pointed towards the barometer, which showed no sign of rising. Passepartout shook it, but without any clear result. Neither shaking it nor complaining could make it change.

On November 4th, however, the sea became calmer, and the storm began to die down. The wind changed to the south and became helpful again. Passepartout's spirits improved along with the weather. Some of the sails were put up again, and the "Rangoon" returned to its fastest speed. The lost time could not be recovered, however. Land was not spotted until five o'clock in the morning on the 6th, when the steamer was due on the 5th. Phileas Fogg was twenty-four hours behind schedule, and the Yokohama steamer would, of course, be missed.

The pilot came on board at six o'clock and took his place on the bridge to guide the "Rangoon" through the channels to the port of Hong Kong. Passepartout wanted desperately to ask the pilot whether the steamer for Yokohama had left. But he did not dare, as he wanted to keep alive the small hope that remained until the very last moment. He had told Fix about his worries, and that clever man tried to comfort him by saying that Mr. Fogg would be in time if he took the next boat. But this only made Passepartout more angry.

Mr. Fogg, bolder than his servant, did not hesitate to approach the pilot and calmly ask him whether he knew when a steamer would leave Hong Kong for Yokohama.

"At high tide tomorrow morning," answered the pilot.

"Ah!" said Mr. Fogg, without showing any surprise.

Passepartout, who heard this, would gladly have hugged the pilot, while Fix would have liked to wring his neck.

"What is the steamer's name?" asked Mr. Fogg.

"The 'Carnatic.'"

"Was she not supposed to leave yesterday?"

"Yes, sir. But they had to repair one of her boilers, and so her departure was put off until tomorrow."

"Thank you," returned Mr. Fogg, going calmly back down to the cabin.

Passepartout grabbed the pilot's hand and shook it energetically, exclaiming, "Pilot, you are the finest fellow in the world!"

The pilot probably never knew to this day why his answer had earned him such an enthusiastic welcome. He went back up to the bridge and guided the steamer through the many small boats and fishing vessels that crowded Hong Kong harbour.

At one o'clock the "Rangoon" was at the dock, and the passengers were going ashore.

Chance had worked strongly in Phileas Fogg's favour. If the "Carnatic" had not been forced to wait for boiler repairs, she would have left on November 6th, and the passengers travelling to Japan would have had to wait a whole week for the next ship. Mr. Fogg was indeed twenty-four hours late, but this could not seriously put the rest of his journey at risk.

The steamer crossing the Pacific from Yokohama to San Francisco was directly connected to the one from Hong Kong, and could not sail until the Hong Kong steamer had arrived at Yokohama. If Mr. Fogg was twenty-four hours late reaching Yokohama, this time could easily be made up during the twenty-two-day Pacific crossing. He was therefore about twenty-four hours behind, thirty-five days after leaving London.

The "Carnatic" was scheduled to leave Hong Kong at five o'clock the next morning. Mr. Fogg had sixteen hours to take care of his business there, which was to safely place Aouda with her wealthy relative.

After landing, he took her to a covered carriage, and they went to the Club Hotel. A room was arranged for the young woman, and Mr. Fogg, after making sure she had everything she needed, went out to look for her cousin Jeejeeh. He asked Passepartout to stay at the hotel until he returned, so that Aouda would not be left completely alone.

Mr. Fogg went to the financial district, where he had no doubt that a wealthy and well-known person like the Parsee merchant would be known to everyone. Meeting a broker, he made his enquiry, only to learn that Jeejeeh had left China two years earlier. Having retired from business with a great fortune, he had gone to live in Europe — in Holland, the broker thought, since that was where most of his trade contacts were. Phileas Fogg returned to the hotel, asked for a moment to speak with Aouda, and without further delay told her that Jeejeeh was no longer in Hong Kong, but probably in Holland.

Aouda said nothing at first. She put her hand to her forehead and thought for a few moments. Then, in her sweet, soft voice, she said: "What should I do, Mr. Fogg?"

"It is very simple," replied the gentleman. "Come with us to Europe."

"But I cannot impose—"

"You are not imposing, and you do not in any way get in the way of my plans. Passepartout!"

"Sir."

"Go to the 'Carnatic' and book three cabins."

Passepartout, happy that the young woman — who was always very kind to him — would continue the journey with them, went off at a quick pace to carry out his master's instruction.
