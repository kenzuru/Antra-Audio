

  Everyone knows that the great triangle-shaped piece of land, broad in the north and coming to a point in the south, which is called India, covers fourteen hundred thousand square miles and has a population of one hundred and eighty million people spread unevenly across it. The British Crown holds real and absolute power over the larger part of this vast country. It has a governor-general stationed at Calcutta, governors at Madras, Bombay, and Bengal, and a lieutenant-governor at Agra.

But British India, properly speaking, only covers seven hundred thousand square miles with a population of between one hundred and one hundred and ten million people. A large part of India is still free from British authority, and there are certain powerful local rulers in the interior who are completely independent. The famous East India Company was all-powerful from 1756, when the English first gained a position on the spot where the city of Madras now stands, right up to the time of the great Sepoy rebellion. It gradually took over province after province, buying them from the local rulers, whom it rarely paid fairly, and it appointed the governor-general and his civil and military assistants. But the East India Company has now ceased to exist, leaving the British territories in India directly under the control of the Crown. The appearance of the country, as well as the customs and racial differences, is changing every day.

In the old days one was forced to travel in India using the old slow methods — on foot, on horseback, in small covered coaches, or in large awkward carriages. Now fast steamboats travel the Indus and the Ganges rivers, and a great railway, with branch lines joining the main line at many points, crosses the whole country from Bombay to Calcutta in three days. This railway does not go in a straight line across India. The distance between Bombay and Calcutta, as the crow flies, is only about a thousand to eleven hundred miles. But the curves and bends in the route increase this distance by more than a third.

The general route of the Great Indian Peninsula Railway is as follows: leaving Bombay, it passes through Salcette, crosses to the mainland opposite Tannah, goes over the chain of the Western Ghats mountain range, then runs north-east as far as Burhampoor, passes near the almost independent territory of Bundelcund, goes up to Allahabad, then turns east, meets the Ganges at Benares, then moves away from the river again, and travelling south-east through Burdivan and the French town of Chandernagor, ends at Calcutta.

The passengers of the Mongolia went ashore at half past four in the afternoon. At exactly eight o'clock the train would depart for Calcutta.

Mr. Fogg, after saying goodbye to his whist partners, left the steamer, gave his servant several tasks to do, told him firmly to be at the station by eight, and with his regular step — as precise as an astronomical clock — made his way to the passport office. As for the wonders of Bombay — its famous city hall, its splendid library, its forts and docks, its markets, mosques, synagogues, its Armenian churches, and the fine pagoda on Malabar Hill with its two many-sided towers — he did not care in the slightest to see any of them. He would not even glance at the masterpieces of Elephanta, or the mysterious ancient underground temples hidden south-east of the docks, or those fine remains of Buddhist architecture, the Kanherian cave temples on the island of Salcette.

Having completed his business at the passport office, Phileas Fogg went quietly to the railway station, where he ordered dinner. Among the dishes served to him, the restaurant owner particularly recommended a certain dish of "jungle rabbit" of which he was very proud.

Mr. Fogg accordingly tried the dish, but despite its spiced sauce, found it far from tasty. He called for the restaurant owner, and when he appeared, said, looking at him steadily with clear eyes, "Is this rabbit, sir?"

"Yes, my lord," the man replied boldly. "Rabbit from the jungles."

"And this rabbit did not make a sound when it was killed?"

"Make a sound, my lord! What, a rabbit make a sound! I swear to you—"

"Please do not swear, landlord, but remember this: cats were once considered sacred animals in India. That was a good time."

"For the cats, my lord?"

"Perhaps for the travellers as well!"

After which Mr. Fogg quietly continued his dinner. Fix had gone ashore shortly after Mr. Fogg, and his first stop was the headquarters of the Bombay police. He introduced himself as a London detective, explained his purpose in Bombay and the situation regarding the suspected robber, and nervously asked whether a warrant had arrived from London. It had not reached the office. Indeed, there had not yet been enough time for it to arrive. Fix was deeply disappointed, and tried to get an order of arrest from the director of the Bombay police. The director refused, because the matter concerned the London office, which was the only one with the legal authority to issue the warrant. Fix did not argue, and had to accept that he would have to wait for this important document to arrive. But he was determined not to lose sight of the mysterious suspect for as long as he stayed in Bombay. He had no doubt at all, any more than Passepartout did, that Phileas Fogg would remain there at least until it was time for the warrant to arrive.

Passepartout, however, had no sooner heard his master's instructions on leaving the Mongolia than he realised at once that they were going to leave Bombay just as they had left Suez and Paris, and that the journey would continue at least as far as Calcutta, and perhaps even further. He began to wonder if this bet that Mr. Fogg talked about was not actually serious, and whether his own fate was not truly forcing him, despite his love of peace and quiet, around the world in eighty days!

Having bought the usual amount of shirts and shoes, he took a leisurely walk through the streets, where crowds of people of many different nationalities gathered — Europeans, Persians wearing pointed caps, Banyans in round turbans, Sindes in square bonnets, Parsees in black hats, and Armenians in long robes. It happened to be the day of a Parsee festival. These people, who are descendants of the followers of Zoroaster — the most careful, civilised, intelligent, and serious of all East Indians, and among whom are found the wealthiest local merchants of Bombay — were celebrating a sort of religious festival, with processions and performances. In the middle of it all, Indian dancing girls dressed in rose-coloured see-through fabric, decorated with gold and silver, danced lightly but with perfect modesty to the sound of string instruments and the beating of tambourines. It is needless to say that Passepartout watched these unusual ceremonies with wide eyes and open mouth, and that his face had the expression of the most completely amazed person imaginable.

Unfortunately for both his master and himself, his curiosity led him unknowingly further than he intended to go. At last, having watched the Parsee procession disappear into the distance, he was turning back towards the station when he happened to see the splendid pagoda on Malabar Hill and felt an irresistible desire to see the inside of it. He was completely unaware that Christians are forbidden from entering certain Indian temples, and that even the faithful must not enter without first leaving their shoes outside the door. It should be mentioned here that the wise policy of the British Government strongly punishes those who fail to respect the practices of the local religions.

Passepartout, however, thinking no harm would come of it, went in like a simple tourist, and was soon lost in admiration of the splendid Hindu decoration that met his eyes everywhere. Suddenly he found himself falling and lying flat on the sacred stone floor. He looked up to see three very angry priests, who immediately threw themselves on him, tore off his shoes, and began to beat him, shouting loudly and fiercely. The quick-moving Frenchman was soon back on his feet, and wasted no time in knocking down two of his long-robed attackers with his fists and a well-aimed kick. Then, running out of the pagoda as fast as his legs could carry him, he quickly escaped the third priest by disappearing into the crowd in the streets.

At five minutes before eight, Passepartout, without a hat, without shoes, and having lost his package of shirts and shoes in the fight, rushed breathlessly into the station.

Fix, who had followed Mr. Fogg to the station and seen that he was truly going to leave Bombay, was already there on the platform. He had decided to follow the supposed robber to Calcutta, and further if necessary. Passepartout did not notice the detective, who was standing in a dark corner. But Fix heard him tell Mr. Fogg about his adventure in a few words.

"I hope this will not happen again," said Phileas Fogg coldly, as he got into the train. Poor Passepartout, feeling quite ashamed, followed his master without a word. Fix was about to get into another carriage when a thought came to him that made him change his plan.

"No, I'll stay," he muttered. "A crime has been committed on Indian soil. I've got my man."

Just then the engine gave a sharp whistle, and the train moved out into the darkness of the night.
