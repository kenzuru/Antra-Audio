

  "Hans, move these tables into the smoking room, and bring a brush to clean these marks off the carpet and — one moment, Hans —" Jose loved giving orders to the servants, and they loved following them. She always made them feel they were part of something exciting. "Tell mother and Miss Laura to come here at once."

"Very good, Miss Jose."

She turned to Meg. "I want to hear how the piano sounds, just in case I am asked to sing this afternoon. Let's try 'This Life is Weary.'"

Pom! Ta-ta-ta Tee-ta! The piano started so powerfully that Jose's face changed. She held her hands together. She looked sadly and mysteriously at her mother and Laura as they came in.

This Life is Wee-ary,
A Tear — a Sigh.
A Love that Chan-ges,
This Life is Wee-ary,
A Tear — a Sigh.
A Love that Chan-ges,
And then... Good-bye!

But at the word "Good-bye," even though the piano sounded more emotional than ever, her face broke into a bright, strangely unsympathetic smile.

"Am I not in good voice, mummy?" she said happily.

This Life is Wee-ary,
Hope comes to Die.
A Dream — a Wa-kening.

But then Sadie interrupted them. "What is it, Sadie?"

"If you please, ma'am, cook asks whether you have the little labels for the sandwiches."

"The labels for the sandwiches, Sadie?" Mrs. Sheridan repeated, as if far away in thought. And the children could tell by her face that she had forgotten them. "Let me see." And she said firmly to Sadie, "Tell cook I will give them to her in ten minutes."

Sadie left.

"Now, Laura," said her mother quickly, "come with me into the smoking room. I wrote the names somewhere on the back of an envelope. You will have to write them out for me. Meg, go upstairs right now and take that wet thing off your head. Jose, go and finish getting dressed this minute. Do you hear me, children, or should I tell your father when he comes home tonight? And — and, Jose, calm down cook if you go into the kitchen, will you? I am frightened of her this morning."

The envelope was finally found behind the dining room clock, though Mrs. Sheridan could not imagine how it got there.

"One of you children must have taken it from my bag, because I clearly remember — cream cheese and lemon curd. Have you done that?"

"Yes."

"Egg and —" Mrs. Sheridan held the envelope further away from her eyes. "It looks like mice. It cannot be mice, can it?"

"Olive, I think," said Laura, looking over her shoulder.

"Yes, of course, olive. What a strange combination. Egg and olive."

They finished at last, and Laura took the list to the kitchen. She found Jose there calming the cook, who did not look frightening at all.

"I have never seen such beautiful sandwiches," said Jose in a delighted voice. "How many types did you say there were, cook? Fifteen?"

"Fifteen, Miss Jose."

"Well, cook, I congratulate you."

Cook swept up the crusts with the long sandwich knife and smiled broadly.

"Godber's has arrived," announced Sadie, coming out of the pantry. She had seen the man walk past the window.

That meant the cream puffs had come. Godber's was famous for its cream puffs. Nobody ever thought of making them at home.

"Bring them in and put them on the table, my girl," cook ordered.

Sadie brought them in and went back to the door. Of course Laura and Jose were far too grown-up to really care about such things. All the same, they could not help agreeing that the cream puffs looked very attractive. Very. Cook began arranging them, shaking off the extra icing sugar.

"Don't they take you back to all the childhood parties?" said Laura.

"I suppose they do," said practical Jose, who never liked being taken back. "They do look beautifully light and soft, I must say."

"Have one each, my dears," said cook in her comfortable voice. "Your mother won't know."

Oh, impossible. Cream puffs so soon after breakfast. Just the thought made you feel ill. All the same, two minutes later Jose and Laura were licking their fingers with that focused, dreamy expression that only comes from eating whipped cream.
