

   The distance between Suez and Aden is exactly thirteen hundred and ten miles, and the company's regulations allow the steamers one hundred and thirty-eight hours to cross it. The Mongolia, thanks to the strong efforts of the engineer, appeared likely, given her great speed, to reach her destination well within that time. Most of the passengers from Brindisi were travelling to India — some to Bombay, others to Calcutta via Bombay, which was now the nearest route since a railway crossed the Indian peninsula. Among the passengers were a number of government officials and military officers of various ranks. The military men were either attached to the regular British forces or commanding the Sepoy troops and receiving high salaries, ever since the central government took over the powers of the East India Company. Sub-lieutenants received £280 a year, brigadiers £2,400, and major-generals £4,000. With the military men, a number of wealthy young Englishmen travelling abroad, and the friendly efforts of the purser, time passed quickly on the Mongolia. The best food was placed on the cabin tables at breakfast, lunch, dinner, and the eight o'clock supper. The ladies carefully changed their clothes twice a day, and the hours flew by, when the sea was calm, with music, dancing, and games.

But the Red Sea is unpredictable and often rough, like most long and narrow bodies of water. When the wind came from the African or Asian coast, the Mongolia, with her long hull, rolled terribly. Then the ladies quickly disappeared below deck. The pianos went silent, and singing and dancing suddenly stopped. But the good ship pushed straight on, not slowed by wind or wave, towards the Strait of Bab-el-Mandeb. What was Phileas Fogg doing all this time? One might think that, being worried, he would constantly be watching the changing wind and the wild waves — every chance, in short, that might force the Mongolia to slow down and so interrupt his journey. But if he thought about these possibilities, he gave no outward sign of it.

Always the same untroubled member of the Reform Club, whom no event could surprise, as steady as the ship's clocks, and rarely even feeling curious enough to go up on deck, he passed through the notable scenes of the Red Sea with cold indifference. He did not care to look at the historic towns and villages that, along its shores, raised their picturesque outlines against the sky. He showed no fear of the dangers of the Arabian Gulf, which old writers always described with horror and upon which ancient sailors never ventured without first praying to their gods. How did this unusual person pass his time on the Mongolia? He ate his four hearty meals every day, no matter how much the steamer rolled and swayed. And he played whist without tiring, having found partners who were as enthusiastic about the game as himself. A tax collector on his way to his post at Goa, the Reverend Decimus Smith returning to his parish in Bombay, and a brigadier-general of the English army about to rejoin his troops at Benares made up the group, and together with Mr. Fogg they played whist by the hour in focused silence.

As for Passepartout, he too had avoided seasickness and ate his meals properly in the front cabin. He rather enjoyed the voyage. He was well fed and well looked after, took a great interest in the scenes passing by, and comforted himself with the belief that his master's strange idea would end at Bombay. He was pleased, the day after leaving Suez, to find on deck the friendly man he had walked and chatted with on the docks.

"If I'm not mistaken," he said, going up to this person with his most friendly smile, "you are the gentleman who so kindly offered to guide me at Suez?"

"Ah! I recognise you perfectly. You are the servant of that unusual Englishman—"

"That's right, sir—"

"Fix."

"Monsieur Fix," continued Passepartout, "I'm very happy to find you on board. Where are you headed?"

"Like you, to Bombay."

"That's excellent! Have you made this trip before?"

"Several times. I am one of the agents of the Peninsular Company."

"So you know India?"

"Why yes," replied Fix, who spoke carefully.

"A curious place, this India?"

"Oh, very curious. Mosques, tall towers, temples, holy men, pagodas, tigers, snakes, elephants! I hope you will have plenty of time to see the sights."

"I hope so, Monsieur Fix. You see, a sensible man should not spend his life jumping from a steamer onto a railway train and from a railway train onto a steamer again, pretending to make a tour of the world in eighty days! No. All this rushing about will certainly stop at Bombay."

"And Mr. Fogg is keeping well?" asked Fix, in the most natural tone he could manage.

"Very well, and so am I. I eat like a starving man. It must be the sea air."

"But I never see your master on deck."

"Never. He has no curiosity at all."

"Do you know, Mr. Passepartout, that this supposed tour of eighty days might be hiding some secret purpose — perhaps a diplomatic mission?"

"Honestly, Monsieur Fix, I know nothing about it, and I wouldn't give half a penny to find out."

After this meeting, Passepartout and Fix got into the habit of talking together. Fix made it his aim to gain the good man's trust. He often offered him a glass of whisky or pale ale in the ship's bar, which Passepartout never failed to accept with happy willingness, privately thinking Fix was one of the finest men he had ever met.

Meanwhile the Mongolia was moving forward quickly. On the 13th, Mocha, surrounded by its broken walls on which date trees were growing, came into view, and on the mountains beyond, vast coffee fields could be seen. Passepartout was delighted to see this famous place, and thought that with its round walls and broken-down fort, it looked like an enormous coffee cup and saucer. The following night they passed through the Strait of Bab-el-Mandeb, which means in Arabic "The Bridge of Tears," and the next day they stopped at Steamer Point, north-west of Aden harbour, to load coal. The matter of fuelling steamers is a serious one at such great distances from the coal mines. It costs the Peninsular Company about eight hundred thousand pounds a year. In these far-off seas, coal costs three or four pounds per ton.

The Mongolia still had sixteen hundred and fifty miles to travel before reaching Bombay, and needed to stay four hours at Steamer Point to load coal. But this delay, having been planned for, did not affect Phileas Fogg's schedule. Besides, the Mongolia, instead of arriving at Aden on the morning of the 15th as planned, arrived there on the evening of the 14th — a gain of fifteen hours.

Mr. Fogg and his servant went ashore at Aden to have the passport stamped again. Fix, without being noticed, followed them. After the passport was stamped, Mr. Fogg returned on board to continue his usual habits, while Passepartout, as was his way, wandered about among the mixed population of Somalis, Banyans, Parsees, Jews, Arabs, and Europeans who made up the twenty-five thousand inhabitants of Aden. He looked with wonder at the fortifications that make this place the Gibraltar of the Indian Ocean, and the vast water tanks where English engineers were still at work, two thousand years after the engineers of Solomon.

"Very curious, very curious," said Passepartout to himself, on returning to the steamer. "I can see that travelling is by no means useless, if a man wants to see something new." At six in the evening the Mongolia slowly moved out of the harbour, and was soon once more on the Indian Ocean. She had a hundred and sixty-eight hours in which to reach Bombay, and the sea was calm, with the wind coming from the north-west and all sails helping the engine. The steamer rocked very little. The ladies, in fresh outfits, reappeared on deck, and the singing and dancing started again. The trip was going extremely well, and Passepartout was delighted with the pleasant companion that chance had found for him in the agreeable Fix. On Sunday, the 20th of October, towards noon, they came in sight of the Indian coast. Two hours later a harbour pilot came on board. A range of hills appeared on the horizon against the sky, and soon the rows of palm trees that decorate Bombay came clearly into view. The steamer entered the passage formed by the islands in the bay, and at half past four she docked at the quays of Bombay.

Phileas Fogg was just finishing the thirty-third round of cards during the voyage. His partner and he, by a bold move, had taken all thirteen tricks, ending this fine campaign with a brilliant victory.

The Mongolia was due at Bombay on the 22nd. She arrived on the 20th. This meant a gain of two days for Phileas Fogg since his departure from London, and he calmly wrote this down in his travel record, in the column for time gained.
