
  "From ocean to ocean" — so say the Americans. These four words make up the general description of the "great main line" which crosses the entire width of the United States. The Pacific Railroad is, however, really divided into two separate lines: the Central Pacific, between San Francisco and Ogden, and the Union Pacific, between Ogden and Omaha. Five main lines connect Omaha with New York.

New York and San Francisco are therefore connected by an unbroken metal track which is no less than three thousand seven hundred and eighty-six miles long. Between Omaha and the Pacific the railway crosses a territory which is still home to Native Americans and wild animals, and a large area which the Mormons, after they were forced out of Illinois in 1845, began to settle.

The journey from New York to San Francisco used to take, in the best conditions, at least six months. It is now done in seven days.

It was in 1862 that, despite the Southern Members of Congress who wanted a more southern route, it was decided to build the road between the forty-first and forty-second parallels. President Lincoln himself fixed the end of the line at Omaha, in Nebraska. The work began at once and was carried out with true American energy. The speed of the work did not, however, reduce its quality. The road grew across the flat lands at a mile and a half a day. A train, running on the rails laid the night before, brought the rails to be laid the next morning and moved forward as fast as they were put in place.

The Pacific Railroad connects with several branch lines in Iowa, Kansas, Colorado, and Oregon. Starting from Omaha, it runs along the left bank of the Platte River to the point where its northern branch joins, follows the southern branch, crosses the Laramie territory and the Wahsatch Mountains, passes around the Great Salt Lake, arrives at Salt Lake City, the Mormon capital, goes through the Tuilla Valley, crosses the American Desert, the Cedar and Humboldt Mountains, the Sierra Nevada, and comes down, through Sacramento, to the Pacific. Its slope, even in the Rocky Mountains, never exceeds one hundred and twelve feet per mile.

This was the route to be covered in seven days, which would allow Phileas Fogg — or so he hoped — to catch the Atlantic steamer at New York on the 11th for Liverpool.

The carriage he occupied was a kind of long bus on eight wheels, with no separate compartments inside. It had two rows of seats, one on each side of a central aisle that led to the front and rear platforms. These platforms could be found throughout the train, and passengers were able to move from one end to the other. The train was equipped with sitting rooms, balcony cars, restaurants, and smoking carriages. Only theatre carriages were missing, but these would probably come one day.

Book and news sellers, sellers of food, drinks, and cigars, who seemed to have plenty of customers, were constantly moving through the aisles.

The train left Oakland station at six o'clock. It was already night — cold and without comfort. The sky was covered with clouds that seemed to promise snow. The train did not move quickly. Counting the stops, it did not go faster than twenty miles per hour, which was still fast enough to reach Omaha within its planned time.

There was very little conversation in the carriage, and soon many of the passengers fell asleep. Passepartout found himself sitting next to the detective, but he did not speak to him. After recent events, their relationship had become somewhat distant. There could no longer be real friendship or closeness between them. Fix's manner had not changed, but Passepartout was very guarded and ready to grab his former friend by the throat at the smallest provocation.

Snow began to fall an hour after they set off — a fine snow, which luckily could not block the train. Nothing could be seen through the windows except a vast white surface, against which the smoke from the engine looked grey.

At eight o'clock a train worker entered the carriage and announced that it was time for bed. In a few minutes the carriage was turned into a sleeping room. The backs of the seats were pushed back, carefully packed beds were rolled out using a clever system, sleeping places appeared suddenly, and each traveller soon had a comfortable bed, protected from curious eyes by thick curtains. The sheets were clean and the pillows soft. All that remained was to lie down and sleep — which everyone did — while the train moved on across the State of California.

The land between San Francisco and Sacramento is not very hilly. The Central Pacific, starting from Sacramento, goes east to meet the road from Omaha. The line from San Francisco to Sacramento runs in a north-easterly direction, along the American River, which flows into San Pablo Bay. The one hundred and twenty miles between these cities were covered in six hours, and around midnight, while fast asleep, the travellers passed through Sacramento. So they saw nothing of that important place — the seat of the State government, with its fine docks, broad streets, fine hotels, squares, and churches.

The train, leaving Sacramento and passing through the towns of Roclin, Auburn, and Colfax, entered the Sierra Nevada mountain range. The town of Cisco was reached at seven in the morning, and an hour later the sleeping area had been turned back into a normal carriage. The travellers could now see the beautiful mountain scenery through which they were moving. The railway track wound in and out among the mountain passes, sometimes coming close to the sides of mountains, sometimes hanging above cliffs, avoiding sharp bends by making bold curves, going into narrow valleys that seemed to have no way out. The engine, its large chimney giving out a strange light, with its loud bell and its front guard shaped like a pointed boot, mixed its noise with the sounds of streams and waterfalls, and sent its smoke among the branches of the enormous pine trees.

There were few bridges or tunnels on the route. The railway went around the sides of the mountains and did not try to take shortcuts through them.

The train entered the State of Nevada through the Carson Valley around nine o'clock, still going generally north-east. At midday it reached Reno, where there was a twenty-minute stop for breakfast.

From that point the road ran along the Humboldt River, going north for several miles along its banks. Then it turned east and followed the river until it reached the Humboldt mountain range, near the far eastern edge of Nevada.

After breakfast, Mr. Fogg and his companions returned to their seats in the carriage and watched the changing scenery — vast flat lands, mountains on the horizon, and streams with white, foaming water. Sometimes a great herd of buffalo, gathered together in the distance, looked like a moving dam. These countless numbers of grazing animals often make it impossible for the trains to pass. Thousands of them have been seen crossing the track for hours at a time, in tight groups. The train is then forced to stop and wait until the road is clear again.

This is exactly what happened to the train in which Mr. Fogg was travelling. Around midday, a group of ten or twelve thousand buffalo blocked the track. The engine, slowing down, tried to push through with its front guard. But the mass of animals was too large. The buffalo walked along at a calm pace, making loud bellowing sounds from time to time. There was no point in trying to stop them. Once they had chosen a direction, nothing could slow or change their course. It was a river of living flesh that no wall could hold back.

The travellers watched this unusual sight from the platforms. But Phileas Fogg, who had the most reason of all to be in a hurry, stayed in his seat and waited patiently until it pleased the buffalo to move out of the way.

Passepartout was furious at the delay they were causing, and wanted to fire all his revolvers at them.

"What a country!" he cried. "Just animals blocking the trains, going past in a parade as if they were not slowing anyone down! I'd like to know if Mr. Fogg predicted this problem in his plan! And here's an engineer who doesn't dare drive the train into this herd of animals!"

The engineer was right not to try to force his way through. He would have crushed the first buffalo, no doubt, with the front guard. But the locomotive, however powerful, would soon have been stopped. The train would certainly have left the track and become completely helpless.

The best thing to do was to wait patiently and make up the lost time by going faster once the obstacle was removed. The parade of buffalo lasted three full hours, and night had come before the track was clear. The last rows of the herd were now passing over the rails, while the first had already disappeared below the southern horizon.

It was eight o'clock when the train passed through the openings of the Humboldt mountain range, and half past nine when it entered Utah — the land of the Great Salt Lake, home of the unusual Mormon community.
