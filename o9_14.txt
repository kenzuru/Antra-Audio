

The brave and reckless action had been completed, and for an hour Passepartout laughed happily at his success. Sir Francis shook the good fellow's hand warmly, and his master said "Well done!" — which, coming from him, was great praise. Passepartout replied that all the credit for the matter belonged to Mr. Fogg. As for himself, he said, he had only had a "strange" idea. And he laughed to think that for a few moments he, Passepartout, the former gymnast and former fireman, had played the role of the husband of a charming woman — a respected, preserved old rajah! As for the young Indian woman, she had been unconscious throughout everything that had happened, and now, wrapped in a travelling blanket, was resting in one of the seats on the elephant.

The elephant, thanks to the skilled guidance of the Parsee, was moving quickly through the still-dark forest, and an hour after leaving the temple had crossed a wide plain. They stopped at seven o'clock, with the young woman still completely without strength. The guide gave her a little brandy and water to drink, but the heavy sleep that had come over her could not yet be shaken off. Sir Francis, who knew well the effects of the drugs made from hemp fumes, reassured his companions about her condition. But he was more troubled about what her future might hold. He told Phileas Fogg that if Aouda remained in India, she would certainly fall again into the hands of those who wanted to kill her. These fanatics were spread throughout the country, and would, despite the English police, find their victim again in Madras, Bombay, or Calcutta. She would only be safe if she left India forever.

Phileas Fogg replied that he would think carefully about the matter.

The station at Allahabad was reached at about ten o'clock, and since the railway line continued from there, they would be able to reach Calcutta in less than twenty-four hours. Phileas Fogg would therefore arrive in time to take the steamer leaving Calcutta the next day, October 25th at noon, for Hong Kong.

The young woman was placed in one of the waiting rooms at the station, while Passepartout was given the job of buying her various personal items — a dress, a shawl, and some warm furs — for which his master gave him unlimited money to spend. Passepartout went off right away, finding himself in the streets of Allahabad, the City of God, one of the most respected cities in India, built at the meeting point of the two sacred rivers, the Ganges and the Jumna, whose waters draw pilgrims from every part of the country. According to the stories of the Ramayana, the Ganges begins in heaven, and through the power of Brahma, comes down to the Earth.

Passepartout made a point, while buying what he needed, of having a good look at the city. It was once protected by a fine fort, which had since become a state prison. Its trade had greatly decreased, and Passepartout looked without success for a market like those he used to visit in Regent Street. At last he found an old, unfriendly Jewish man who sold second-hand items, from whom he bought a dress in Scottish fabric, a large coat, and a fine otter-skin fur coat, for which he paid seventy-five pounds without hesitation. He then returned proudly to the station.

The effects of the drugs that the priests of Pillaji had used on Aouda were gradually wearing off, and she was becoming more herself. Her beautiful eyes recovered all their soft Indian expression.

When the poet-king Ucaf Uddaul praised the beauty of the queen of Ahmehnagara, he wrote:

"Her shining hair, divided in two parts, frames the smooth shape of her white and delicate cheeks, bright and fresh. Her dark eyebrows have the form and beauty of the bow of Kama, the god of love, and beneath her long soft lashes, the purest light swims, like a reflection in the sacred lakes of the Himalaya, in the dark pupils of her large clear eyes. Her teeth, fine, even, and white, shine between her smiling lips like dewdrops inside a half-open flower. Her delicately shaped ears, her red hands, her small feet, curved and soft as a lotus bud, shine with the brightness of the loveliest pearls of Ceylon and the most brilliant diamonds of Golconda. Her slim and flexible waist, which one hand could go around, shows the shape of her rounded figure and the beauty of her chest, where youth displays the wealth of its treasures. And beneath the silken folds of her tunic she seems to have been shaped in pure silver by the godlike hands of Vicvarcarma, the immortal sculptor."

It is enough to say, without applying this poetic description to Aouda, that she was a charming woman in every sense of the word. She spoke English with great clarity, and the guide had not exaggerated when he said that the young Parsee woman had been shaped by her upbringing.

The train was about to leave Allahabad, and Mr. Fogg paid the guide exactly the amount they had agreed upon, not a penny more. This surprised Passepartout, who remembered everything that his master owed to the guide's loyalty. The guide had indeed risked his life in the adventure at Pillaji, and if he were later caught by the Indians, he would have great difficulty escaping their revenge. Then there was also Kiouni to deal with. What should be done with the elephant that had been bought at such a high price? Phileas Fogg had already made his decision about this.

"Parsee," he said to the guide, "you have been helpful and loyal. I have paid for your service, but not for your loyalty. Would you like to have this elephant? He is yours."

The guide's eyes lit up.

"Your honour is giving me a fortune!" he cried.

"Take him, guide," returned Mr. Fogg, "and I will still be in your debt."

"Wonderful!" exclaimed Passepartout. "Take him, friend. Kiouni is a brave and faithful animal." And going up to the elephant, he gave him several pieces of sugar, saying, "Here, Kiouni, here, here."

The elephant made a happy sound, and wrapping his trunk around Passepartout's waist, lifted him up as high as his head. Passepartout, not at all frightened, patted the animal, which then gently put him back on the ground.

Soon after, Phileas Fogg, Sir Francis Cromarty, and Passepartout, seated in a carriage with Aouda, who had the best seat, were moving at full speed towards Benares. It was an eighty-mile journey, completed in two hours. During the trip, the young woman completely recovered her senses. She was amazed to find herself in this carriage, on the railway, dressed in European clothes, and with travellers who were total strangers to her. Her companions first gave her a little drink to help her fully recover, and then Sir Francis told her everything that had happened, speaking of the courage with which Phileas Fogg had not hesitated to risk his life to save her, and telling the happy outcome of the rescue, which had been the result of Passepartout's brave idea. Mr. Fogg said nothing, while Passepartout, embarrassed, kept saying that "it wasn't worth mentioning."

Aouda deeply thanked her rescuers, more with tears than with words. Her beautiful eyes showed her gratitude better than her lips could. Then, as her thoughts returned to the scene of the sacrifice and she remembered the dangers that still threatened her, she trembled with fear.

Phileas Fogg understood what was going through Aouda's mind, and offered, in order to calm her fears, to take her to Hong Kong, where she could stay safely until the matter had been forgotten. She accepted this offer eagerly and with great thankfulness. She had, it seemed, a Parsee relative who was one of the leading merchants in Hong Kong, which is entirely an English city, though it is on an island off the Chinese coast.

At half past twelve the train stopped at Benares. The ancient Brahmin stories say that this city is built on the site of the old Casi, which, like the tomb of Mohammed, was once said to float between heaven and earth. But today's Benares, which scholars call the Athens of India, stands very much on solid ground. Passepartout caught glimpses of its brick houses and clay huts, which gave the place a sad and empty appearance, as the train passed through.

Benares was Sir Francis Cromarty's destination, as the troops he was going to join were camped some miles north of the city. He said goodbye to Phileas Fogg, wishing him every success and expressing hope that he would come this way again in a less unusual but more profitable way. Mr. Fogg lightly pressed his hand. The goodbye of Aouda, who had not forgotten what she owed to Sir Francis, was much warmer. And as for Passepartout, he received a firm and friendly handshake from the brave general.

As the railway left Benares, it passed for a while through the valley of the Ganges. Through the windows of their carriage the travellers saw the varied landscape of Behar, with its green mountains, its fields of barley, wheat, and corn, its forests with green crocodiles, its tidy villages, and its still thick forests. Elephants were bathing in the water of the sacred river, and groups of Indians, despite the late season and the cold air, were performing their religious washing rituals with great seriousness. These were devoted Brahmins, the greatest enemies of Buddhism, whose gods were Vishnu, the sun god; Shiva, the divine power of nature; and Brahma, the highest ruler of priests and lawmakers. What would these gods think of India as it is today, with steamers whistling and rushing along the Ganges, frightening the birds floating on its surface, disturbing the turtles crowding its banks, and pushing away the faithful who live along its shores?

The landscape passed before their eyes like a flash, except when steam hid it briefly from view. The travellers could barely make out the fort of Chupenie, twenty miles south-west of Benares; or Ghazipur with its famous rose water factories; or the tomb of Lord Cornwallis, rising on the left bank of the Ganges; the fortified town of Buxar; or Patna, a large manufacturing and trading city, where the most important opium market in India is held; or Monghir, a city more European than European, as English as Manchester or Birmingham, with its iron works, tool factories, and tall chimneys sending clouds of black smoke into the sky.

Night came. The train continued at full speed, while tigers, bears, and wolves that ran from the locomotive could be heard in the darkness. The wonders of Bengal — the ruined Golconda and Gour, Murshedabad the ancient capital, Burdwan, Hugly, and the French town of Chandernagor where Passepartout would have been proud to see his country's flag flying — were all hidden from view by the darkness.

Calcutta was reached at seven in the morning, and the ship left for Hong Kong at noon. So Phileas Fogg had five hours to spare.

According to his plans, he was due at Calcutta on October 25th, and that was the exact date he had arrived. He was neither behind nor ahead of schedule. The two days gained between London and Bombay had been lost, as we have seen, during the journey across India. But it should not be thought that Phileas Fogg regretted this.
