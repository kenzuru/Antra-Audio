

   "November is the most unpleasant month of the whole year," said Margaret, standing at the window one dull afternoon, looking out at the garden where everything had been killed by frost.

"That's probably why I was born in it," said Jo sadly, with a ink stain on her nose that she didn't know about.

"If something very nice were to happen now, we would think it a wonderful month," said Beth, who always looked on the bright side of things, even November.

"Maybe, but nothing pleasant ever happens to this family," said Meg, who was in a bad mood. "We just go along day after day without any change and very little enjoyment. We might as well be in a prison."

"Oh, how gloomy we are!" cried Jo. "I'm not surprised, poor thing, because you see other girls having wonderful times while you work hard, year in and year out. How I wish I could arrange things for you the way I do for the characters in my stories! You're already pretty enough and good enough, so I'd have some wealthy relative leave you a fortune unexpectedly. Then you'd appear as an heiress, show everyone who had ever looked down on you, go abroad, and come home as a titled lady in a blaze of splendor."

"People don't have fortunes left to them like that anymore. Men have to work and women marry for money. It's a terribly unfair world," said Meg bitterly.

"Jo and I are going to make fortunes for all of you. Just wait ten years and see if we don't," said Amy, who was sitting in a corner making small clay models of birds, fruit, and faces—what Hannah called her mud pies.

"I can't wait that long, and I don't have much faith in writing and clay, though I'm grateful for your good intentions."

Meg sighed and turned back to the frost-damaged garden. Jo groaned and leaned on both elbows on the table in a discouraged pose, but Amy continued to work energetically, and Beth, who sat at the other window, said with a smile, "Two pleasant things are about to happen right away. Marmee is coming down the street, and Laurie is walking through the garden as if he has something good to share."

They both came in—Mrs. March with her usual question, "Any letter from Father, girls?" and Laurie to say in his friendly way, "Won't some of you come for a drive? I've been working on mathematics until my head is spinning, and I'm going out to clear my mind. It's a dull day, but the air is pleasant, and I'm going to take Brooke home, so it will be cheerful inside the carriage even if it isn't outside. Come, Jo, you and Beth will come, won't you?"

"Of course we will."

"Many thanks, but I'm busy." And Meg quickly took out her sewing basket, because she had agreed with her mother that it was best, for her at least, not to go driving too often with the young man.

"We three will be ready in a minute," cried Amy, running to wash her hands.

"Can I do anything for you, dear Mrs. March?" asked Laurie, leaning over Mrs. March's chair with the warm and affectionate look and tone he always gave her.

"No, thank you, except stop at the post office, if you don't mind, dear. It's our day for a letter, and the postman hasn't come yet. Father is as reliable as the sun, but perhaps there's been some delay along the way."

A sharp ring at the door interrupted her, and a minute later Hannah came in with a letter.

"It's one of those terrible telegraph things, ma'am," she said, handling it as if she were afraid it would explode.

At the word 'telegraph', Mrs. March grabbed it, read the two lines it contained, and fell back in her chair as white as if the little piece of paper had shot her through the heart. Laurie ran downstairs for water, while Meg and Hannah supported her, and Jo read aloud in a frightened voice:

Mrs. March:
Your husband is very ill. Come at once.
S. HALE
Blank Hospital, Washington.

How silent the room was as they listened, hardly breathing. How strangely the light faded outside, and how suddenly the whole world seemed to change as the girls gathered around their mother, feeling as if all the happiness and support in their lives was about to be taken away.

Mrs. March quickly composed herself, read the message again, and stretched out her arms to her daughters, saying in a voice they would never forget, "I shall go at once, but it may be too late. Oh, children, children, help me to bear it!"

For several minutes there was nothing but the sound of crying in the room, mixed with broken words of comfort, warm promises of help, and hopeful whispers that faded into tears. Poor Hannah was the first to recover, and with practical wisdom she set all the rest a good example, because for her, work was the best medicine for most troubles.

"May God protect the dear man! I won't waste any time crying, but I'll get your things ready right away, ma'am," she said with feeling, as she wiped her face on her apron, gave her mistress a warm squeeze of the hand, and went away to work with the energy of three women.

"She's right. There's no time for tears now. Be calm, girls, and let me think."

They tried to be calm, poor things, as their mother sat up, looking pale but steady, and put aside her grief to think and plan for them.

"Where's Laurie?" she asked shortly, when she had gathered her thoughts and decided on the first things to be done.

"Here, ma'am. Oh, let me do something!" cried the boy, hurrying in from the next room, where he had gone, feeling that their first great sorrow was too private even for his friendly eyes to see.

"Send a telegram saying I will come immediately. The next train leaves early in the morning. I'll take that."

"What else? The horses are ready. I can go anywhere, do anything," he said, looking ready to run to the ends of the earth.

"Leave a note at Aunt March's. Jo, bring me the pen and paper."

Tearing off the blank side of one of her freshly copied pages, Jo set the table in front of her mother, knowing that money for the long, sad journey had to be borrowed, and feeling as if she would do anything to add a little more to the amount needed for her father.

"Now go, dear, but don't drive dangerously fast. There is no need for that."

Mrs. March's warning was clearly ignored, because five minutes later Laurie flew past the window on his own fast horse, riding as if his life depended on it.

"Jo, run to Mrs. King's and tell her I can't come. On your way, get these things. I've written them down—they'll be needed, and I must go prepared for nursing. Hospital supplies are not always of good quality. Beth, go and ask Mr. Laurence for a couple of bottles of his good wine. I'm not too proud to ask for Father's sake. He shall have the best of everything. Amy, tell Hannah to get down the black trunk. Meg, come and help me find my things, because I'm quite overwhelmed."

Writing, thinking, and giving instructions all at the same time might well overwhelm the poor lady, and Meg begged her to sit quietly in her room for a little while and let them work. Everyone scattered like leaves in a strong wind, and the quiet, happy household was turned upside down as suddenly as if the telegram had been an evil spell.

Mr. Laurence came hurrying back with Beth, bringing every comfort the kind old man could think of for the patient, and the warmest promises of protection for the girls during their mother's absence, which comforted her very much. There was nothing he didn't offer, from his own dressing gown to himself as escort. But the last was impossible. Mrs. March would not hear of the old gentleman making the long journey, yet a look of relief was visible when he mentioned it, because worry is not a good preparation for traveling. He noticed the look, knit his heavy eyebrows, rubbed his hands, and walked away quickly, saying he'd be back immediately. No one had time to think about him again until, as Meg hurried through the hallway with a pair of boots in one hand and a cup of tea in the other, she came suddenly face to face with Mr. Brooke.

"I'm very sorry to hear about this, Miss March," he said in the kind, quiet voice that was very pleasant to her troubled spirit. "I've come to offer to escort your mother. Mr. Laurence has some business for me in Washington, and it would give me real satisfaction to be of service to her there."

The boots fell to the floor, and the tea nearly followed, as Meg held out her hand with a face so full of gratitude that Mr. Brooke would have felt well rewarded for a much greater sacrifice than the small one of time and convenience he was about to make.

"How kind you all are! Mother will accept, I'm sure, and it will be such a relief to know that she has someone to look after her. Thank you, so very much!"

Meg spoke sincerely, and forgot herself completely until something in the brown eyes looking down at her made her remember the cooling tea, and she led the way into the living room, saying she would call her mother.

Everything had been arranged by the time Laurie returned with a letter from Aunt March, which contained the money needed and a few lines repeating what she had often said before—that she had always said it was foolish for Mr. March to go into the army, had always predicted no good would come of it, and she hoped they would follow her advice in future. Mrs. March put the letter in the fire, the money in her bag, and went on with her preparations, pressing her lips together firmly in a way that Jo would have recognized.

The short afternoon went by. All other tasks were done, and Meg and her mother were busy with some necessary sewing, while Beth and Amy prepared tea and Hannah finished her ironing with great energy. But Jo still hadn't come. They began to worry, and Laurie went to find her, because no one knew what sudden idea Jo might have had. He missed her, however, and she came walking in with a very unusual expression on her face—a mixture of humor and worry, satisfaction and regret—which confused the family as much as did the roll of banknotes she placed before her mother, saying with a slight catch in her voice, "That's my contribution toward making Father comfortable and bringing him home!"

"My dear, where did you get it? Twenty-five dollars! Jo, I hope you haven't done anything foolish?"

"No, it's honestly mine. I didn't beg, borrow, or steal it. I earned it, and I don't think you'll blame me, because I only sold something that was my own."

As she spoke, Jo took off her hat, and everyone cried out at once, because all her thick hair had been cut short.

"Your hair! Your beautiful hair!" "Oh, Jo, how could you? Your one beauty!" "My dear girl, there was no need for this." "She doesn't look like my Jo anymore, but I love her dearly for it!"

As everyone exclaimed and Beth gently hugged the short head, Jo pretended to be unconcerned, which didn't fool anyone at all, and said, ruffling up the short brown hair and trying to look as if she liked it, "It doesn't change anything important, so don't be upset, Beth. It will be good for my vanity—I was becoming too proud of my long hair. Having that mass removed will be good for me. My head feels wonderfully light and cool, and the barber said I'd soon have a curly style, which will be boyish, attractive, and easy to look after. I'm satisfied, so please take the money and let's have supper."

"Tell me everything, Jo. I'm not entirely satisfied, but I can't criticize you, because I know how willingly you gave up your pride, as you call it, out of love for your father. But, my dear, it wasn't necessary, and I'm afraid you'll regret it someday," said Mrs. March.

"No, I won't!" returned Jo firmly, feeling much relieved that her action hadn't been entirely condemned.

"What made you do it?" asked Amy, who would just as soon have thought of cutting off her head as her pretty hair.

"Well, I was desperate to do something for Father," replied Jo, as they gathered around the table, because healthy young people can eat even in the middle of trouble. "I hate borrowing as much as Mother does, and I knew Aunt March would complain, as she always does if you ask her for anything. Meg gave all of her quarterly salary toward the rent, and I only bought some clothes with mine, so I felt guilty, and was determined to have some money, even if I had to sell my nose to get it."

"You shouldn't feel guilty, my dear! You had no winter clothes and got the simplest things with your own hard-earned money," said Mrs. March with a look that warmed Jo's heart.

"I had no idea at all of selling my hair at first, but as I walked along I kept thinking about what I could do, and feeling as if I'd like to walk into one of the wealthy shops and help myself. In a barber's window I saw cut hair with the prices marked, and one black piece, not as thick as mine, was forty dollars. It suddenly struck me that I had one thing I could turn into money, and without stopping to think, I walked in, asked if they bought hair, and what they would give for mine."

"I don't know how you had the courage to do it," said Beth in wonder.

"Oh, he was a small man who looked as if his one purpose in life was to keep his hair perfectly oiled. He looked rather surprised at first, as if he wasn't used to having girls walk boldly into his shop and ask him to buy their hair. He said he wasn't really interested in mine—it wasn't the fashionable color, and he never paid much for it in the first place, and so on. It was getting late, and I was afraid that if I didn't get it done right away I wouldn't do it at all, and you know how once I've decided to do something, I hate to give up. So I begged him to take it and told him why I was in such a hurry. It was probably silly, I suppose, but it changed his mind, because I got quite emotional and told the story in my mixed-up way, and his wife overheard and said very kindly, 'Take it, Thomas, and help the young lady. I'd do the same for our Jimmy any day if I had hair worth selling.'"

"Who was Jimmy?" asked Amy, who liked to have all the details explained.

"Her son, she said, who was in the army. How much kinder such things make strangers feel toward each other, don't they? She talked away the whole time the man was cutting, and kept my mind pleasantly busy."

"Didn't you feel terrible when the first cut was made?" asked Meg, shuddering.

"I took one last look at my hair while the man prepared his tools, and then it was done. I don't usually get emotional over small things like that. I will admit, though, that I felt strange when I saw my dear old hair laid out on the table and felt only the short rough ends on my head. It almost felt as if I'd lost an arm or a leg. The woman saw me look at it and picked out a long piece for me to keep. I'll give it to you, Marmee, just to remember the old days, because a short style is so comfortable I don't think I'll ever want long hair again."

Mrs. March folded the wavy brown piece of hair and put it away with a short gray one in her desk. She only said, "Thank you, dear," but something in her face made the girls change the subject and talk as cheerfully as they could about Mr. Brooke's kindness, the hope for good weather tomorrow, and the happy times they would have when Father came home to be cared for.

No one wanted to go to bed when at ten o'clock Mrs. March put away her last finished work and said, "Come, girls." Beth went to the piano and played their father's favorite hymn. They all began bravely but fell silent one by one until Beth was left alone, singing with all her heart, because for her, music was always a sweet comfort.

"Go to bed and don't talk, because we must be up early and will need all the sleep we can get. Good night, my darlings," said Mrs. March, when the hymn ended, as no one felt like trying another.

They kissed her quietly and went to bed as silently as if their dear father were already lying ill in the next room. Beth and Amy soon fell asleep despite the great worry, but Meg lay awake, thinking the most serious thoughts she had ever had in her young life. Jo lay still, and her sister thought she was asleep, until a quiet sob made her say softly as she touched a wet cheek:

"Jo, dear, what is it? Are you crying about Father?"

"No, not now."

"What then?"

"My... My hair!" burst out poor Jo, trying unsuccessfully to hold back her feelings in the pillow.

It didn't seem at all funny to Meg, who kissed and comforted her troubled sister as tenderly as she could.

"I'm not sorry," insisted Jo, with a catch in her voice. "I'd do it again tomorrow if I could. It's only the vain part of me that cries in this silly way. Don't tell anyone—it's all over now. I thought you were asleep, so I just had a small private cry over my one beauty. How did you come to be awake?"

"I can't sleep, I'm so worried," said Meg.

"Think about something pleasant and you'll soon fall asleep."

"I tried that, but felt more wide awake than ever."

"What did you think about?"

"Attractive faces—eyes in particular," answered Meg, smiling to herself in the darkness.

"What color do you like best?"

"Brown, that is, sometimes. Blue are very lovely."

Jo laughed, and Meg firmly told her to stop talking, then kindly offered to curl her hair, and fell asleep to dream of living in her dream home.

The clocks were striking midnight and the rooms were very still as a figure moved quietly from bed to bed, straightening a cover here, settling a pillow there, and pausing to look long and tenderly at each sleeping face, to kiss each with lips that silently gave a blessing, and to say the heartfelt prayers that only mothers say. As she lifted the curtain to look out into the dark night, the moon broke suddenly from behind the clouds and shone on her like a bright, kind face, which seemed to whisper in the silence, "Be comforted, dear soul! There is always light behind the clouds."
