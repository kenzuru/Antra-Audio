AFTER dinner all the group went out to hunt for turtle eggs on the sandbar. They went about poking sticks into the sand, and when they found a soft spot they went down on their knees and dug with their hands. Sometimes they would find fifty or sixty eggs in one hole. They were perfectly round white things a little smaller than an English walnut. They had a great fried egg feast that night, and another on Friday morning.

After breakfast they went running and jumping out on the sandbar, and chased each other around, taking off clothes as they went, until they were naked, and then continued playing far away up the shallow water of the sandbar, against the strong current, which tripped their legs from under them from time to time and greatly increased the fun. And now and then they stopped in a group and splashed water in each other's faces with their hands, gradually getting closer to each other, with faces turned away to avoid the water, and finally grabbing and struggling until the strongest one pushed his neighbor underwater, and then they all went under in a tangle of white legs and arms and came up blowing, sputtering, laughing, and gasping for breath all at the same time.

When they were well tired out, they would run out and lie on the dry, hot sand, and rest there and cover themselves with it, and after a while run back into the water again and go through the same things once more. Finally it occurred to them that their bare skin looked very much like flesh-colored tight clothes; so they drew a circle in the sand and had a circus -- with three clowns in it, for none would give up this most important role to another.

Next they got their marbles and played "knucks" and "ring-taw" and "keeps" until that entertainment grew boring. Then Joe and Huck had another swim, but Tom would not risk it, because he found that in taking off his trousers he had lost his string of rattlesnake rattles from his ankle, and he was worried about how he had avoided cramps so long without the protection of this mysterious charm. He did not dare go back in until he had found it, and by that time the other boys were tired and ready to rest. They gradually moved away from each other, fell into sad moods, and began looking longingly across the wide river to where the village lay quietly in the sun. Tom found himself writing "BECKY" in the sand with his big toe; he scratched it out, and was angry with himself for his weakness. But he wrote it again anyway; he could not help it. He erased it once more and then took himself away from temptation by gathering the other boys together and joining them.

But Joe's mood had gone down almost too far to recover. He was so homesick that he could hardly stand the misery of it. He was very close to tears. Huck was sad too. Tom was sad but tried hard not to show it. He had a secret which he was not ready to share yet, but if this sad mood was not broken up soon, he would have to bring it out. He said, with a big show of happiness:

"I bet there were pirates on this island before, boys. We'll explore it again. They hid treasure here somewhere. How would you feel finding a rotten chest full of gold and silver -- hey?"

But it raised only faint interest, which quickly died, with no reply. Tom tried one or two other exciting ideas; but they failed too. It was discouraging work. Joe sat poking the sand with a stick and looking very gloomy. Finally he said:

"Oh, boys, let's give it up. I want to go home. It's so lonely."

"Oh no, Joe, you'll feel better soon," said Tom. "Just think of the fishing that's here."

"I don't care for fishing. I want to go home."

"But, Joe, there isn't a better swimming place anywhere."

"Swimming isn't good. I don't seem to care for it, somehow, when there isn't anybody to say I can't go in. I want to go home."

"Oh, come on! Baby! You want to see your mother, I suppose."

"Yes, I DO want to see my mother -- and you would too, if you had one. I'm no more of a baby than you are." And Joe cried softly a little.

"Well, we'll let the crybaby go home to his mother, won't we, Huck? Poor thing -- does it want to see its mother? Then it shall. You like it here, don't you, Huck? We'll stay, won't we?"

Huck said, "Y-e-s" -- without any feeling in it.

"I'll never speak to you again as long as I live," said Joe, standing up. "There now!" And he moved sadly away and began to get dressed.

"Who cares!" said Tom. "Nobody wants you to. Go home and get laughed at. Oh, you're a fine pirate. Huck and me aren't crybabies. We'll stay, won't we, Huck? Let him go if he wants to. I reckon we can get along without him, maybe."

But Tom was worried, and was troubled to see Joe continue getting dressed. And then it was upsetting to see Huck watching Joe's preparations sadly, keeping quiet. Presently, without a parting word, Joe began to wade off toward the Illinois shore. Tom's heart began to sink. He looked at Huck. Huck could not bear the look, and dropped his eyes. Then he said:

"I want to go too, Tom. It was getting so lonely anyway, and now it'll be worse. Let's go too, Tom."

"I won't! You can all go, if you want to. I mean to stay."

"Tom, I better go."

"Well, go then -- who's stopping you."

Huck began to pick up his scattered clothes. He said:

"Tom, I wish you'd come too. Think it over. We'll wait for you when we get to shore."

"Well, you'll wait a long time, that's all."

Huck started away sadly, and Tom stood looking after him, with a strong desire pulling at his heart to forget his pride and go along too. He hoped the boys would stop, but they kept wading slowly on. It suddenly became clear to Tom that it had become very lonely and quiet. He made one final struggle with his pride, and then ran after his friends, yelling:

"Wait! Wait! I want to tell you something!"

They stopped and turned around. When he reached them, he began to share his secret, and they listened without much interest until at last they understood what he was saying, and then they let out a loud yell of approval and said it was "great!" and said if he had told them at first, they would not have started to leave. He made a good excuse; but his real reason had been the fear that not even the secret would keep them with him for very long, and so he had meant to save it as a last attraction.

The boys happily returned and went back to their activities with energy, talking all the time about Tom's great plan and admiring how clever it was. After a delicious egg and fish dinner, Tom said he wanted to learn to smoke now. Joe liked the idea and said he would like to try too. So Huck made pipes and filled them. These beginners had never smoked anything before but cigars made of grape vine, and these burned the tongue and were not considered manly anyway.

Now they lay on their elbows and began to puff, carefully, and without much confidence. The smoke had an unpleasant taste, and they coughed a little, but Tom said:

"Why, it's just as easy! If I'd known this was all, I'd have learned long ago."

"So would I," said Joe. "It's nothing at all."

"Why, many times I've watched people smoking, and thought well, I wish I could do that; but I never thought I could," said Tom.

"That's just the way with me, hasn't it, Huck? You've heard me talk just that way -- haven't you, Huck? I'll leave it to Huck if I haven't."

"Yes -- lots of times," said Huck.

"Well, I have too," said Tom; "oh, hundreds of times. Once down by the slaughterhouse. Don't you remember, Huck? Bob Tanner was there, and Johnny Miller, and Jeff Thatcher, when I said it. Don't you remember, Huck, about me saying that?"

"Yes, that's so," said Huck. "That was the day after I lost a white marble. No, it was the day before."

"There -- I told you so," said Tom. "Huck remembers it."

"I think I could smoke this pipe all day," said Joe. "I don't feel sick."

"Neither do I," said Tom. "I could smoke it all day. But I bet you Jeff Thatcher couldn't."

"Jeff Thatcher! Why, he'd pass out just with two puffs. Just let him try it once. HE'D see!"

"I bet he would. And Johnny Miller -- I wish I could see Johnny Miller try it once."

"Oh, I do too!" said Joe. "Why, I bet you Johnny Miller couldn't any more do this than nothing. Just one little puff would get HIM."

"It certainly would, Joe. Say -- I wish the boys could see us now."

"So do I."

"Say -- boys, don't say anything about it, and some time when they're around, I'll come up to you and say, 'Joe, got a pipe? I want a smoke.' And you'll say, kind of casually, as if it wasn't anything, you'll say, 'Yes, I got my OLD pipe, and another one, but my tobacco isn't very good.' And I'll say, 'Oh, that's all right, if it's STRONG enough.' And then you'll take out the pipes, and we'll light up just as calm, and then just see them look!"

"Wow, that'll be great, Tom! I wish it was NOW!"

"So do I! And when we tell them we learned when we were off pirating, won't they wish they'd been along?"

"Oh, I think so! I'll just BET they will!"

So the talk went on. But soon it began to slow down a little, and become disconnected. The pauses grew longer; the spitting greatly increased. Every part of the inside of the boys' mouths became a fountain of saliva; they could scarcely spit fast enough to keep up; small amounts went down their throats in spite of everything they could do, and sudden sickness followed each time. Both boys were looking very pale and miserable now. Joe's pipe dropped from his weak fingers. Tom's followed. Both were feeling very ill, and both pretended something else was wrong.

Joe said weakly:

"I've lost my knife. I think I better go and find it."

Tom said, with shaking lips:

"I'll help you. You go that way and I'll look around by the spring. No, you don't need to come, Huck -- we can find it."

So Huck sat down again, and waited an hour. Then he felt lonely, and went to find his friends. They were far apart in the woods, both very pale, both fast asleep. But something told him that if they had been feeling bad, they had got rid of it.

They were not very talkative at supper that night. They looked humble, and when Huck prepared his pipe after the meal and was going to prepare theirs, they said no, they were not feeling very well -- something they ate at dinner had made them sick.

Around midnight Joe woke up, and called the boys. There was a heavy feeling in the air that seemed to predict something. The boys moved together and looked for the comfort of the fire, though the dead heat of the still air was stifling. They sat still, focused and waiting. The deep quiet continued. Beyond the light of the fire everything was swallowed up in darkness. Soon there was a flickering light that vaguely lit up the leaves for a moment and then disappeared. After a while another came, a little brighter. Then another. Then a faint sound came sighing through the branches of the forest and the boys felt a brief touch of wind on their cheeks, and shuddered with the feeling that the Spirit of the Night had passed by. There was a pause. Now a strange flash turned night into day and showed every little grass blade, separate and clear, growing around their feet. And it showed three white, frightened faces too. A deep loud sound of thunder went rolling and rumbling down the sky and died away in low rumbles in the distance. A wave of cold air passed by, rustling all the leaves and blowing the light ashes all around the fire. Another fierce flash lit up the forest and an instant crash followed that seemed to split the tree tops right over the boys' heads. They held on to each other in terror, in the thick darkness that followed. A few large rain drops fell pattering upon the leaves.

"Quick! boys, go for the tent!" exclaimed Tom.

They ran, stumbling over roots and vines in the dark, each going in a different direction. A furious strong wind roared through the trees, making everything shake as it went. One blinding flash after another came, and loud crashes of deafening thunder. And now a heavy rain poured down and the rising storm drove it in sheets along the ground. The boys called out to each other, but the roaring wind and the thunder drowned their voices completely. However, one by one they came together at last and took shelter under the tent, cold, scared, and soaking wet; but having company in misery felt like something to be grateful for. They could not talk, the old sail flapped so hard, even if the other noises would have allowed them. The storm grew higher and higher, and soon the sail tore free from its fastenings and flew away in the blast. The boys grabbed each other's hands and ran, falling and getting hurt, to the shelter of a great oak that stood on the riverbank. Now the storm was at its worst. Under the endless lightning that lit up the sky, everything below stood out sharp and clear: the bending trees, the choppy river, white with foam, the driving spray, the faint outlines of the high cliffs on the other side, seen quickly through the drifting clouds and the slanted rain. Every little while some large tree gave up and fell crashing through the smaller trees; and the continuous thunder came now in ear-splitting bursts, sharp and terrifying. The storm reached its peak in one great effort that seemed about to tear the island to pieces, burn it, drown it, blow it away, and make everyone on it deaf, all at the same moment. It was a wild night for homeless young people to be out in.

But at last the storm was over, and the winds left with weaker and weaker threats and rumbles, and peace returned. The boys went back to camp, quite scared, but they found there was still something to be thankful for, because the great sycamore tree, which sheltered their beds, was now a wreck, struck by lightning, and they were not under it when it happened.

Everything in camp was soaked, including the camp fire; for they were careless boys, like most of their age, and had made no preparation against rain. This was a reason for worry, for they were soaked through and cold. They expressed their unhappiness loudly; but they soon discovered that the fire had burned far enough under the great log it had been built against that a small part of it had escaped getting wet; so they patiently worked until, with scraps and bark gathered from the undersides of sheltered logs, they carefully got the fire going again. Then they piled on large dead branches until they had a roaring fire, and felt cheerful again. They warmed their ham and had a feast, and after that they sat by the fire and talked excitedly about their midnight adventure until morning, for there was not a dry spot to sleep anywhere around.

As the sun began to appear, sleepiness came over them, and they went out on the sandbar and lay down to sleep. They got too hot after a while, and sadly began to make breakfast. After the meal they felt uncomfortable, stiff, and a little homesick again. Tom saw the signs, and tried to cheer up the pirates as well as he could. But they cared nothing for marbles, or circus, or swimming, or anything. He reminded them of the important secret, and raised a small amount of cheer. While it lasted, he got them interested in a new idea. This was to stop being pirates for a while, and be Native Americans for a change. They liked this idea; so it was not long before they were undressed, and painted from head to foot with black mud, like zebras -- all of them chiefs, of course -- and then they went running through the woods to attack an English settlement.

After a while they split into three unfriendly tribes, and jumped on each other from hiding with terrible war cries, and pretended to kill and scalp each other many times. It was a violent day. And therefore it was an extremely enjoyable one.

They came together in camp toward supper time, hungry and happy; but now a problem arose -- unfriendly tribes could not eat together without first making peace, and this was impossible without smoking a peace pipe. There was no other way they had ever heard of. Two of the boys almost wished they had remained pirates. However, there was no other choice; so with as much happiness as they could manage, they called for the pipe and took their puff as it passed, in the right way.

And look, they were glad they had become warriors, for they had gained something; they found that they could now smoke a little without needing to go and hunt for a lost knife; they did not feel sick enough to be seriously uncomfortable. They were not likely to waste this new ability for lack of effort. No, they practiced carefully after supper, with pretty good success, and so they spent a joyful evening. They were prouder and happier in their new skill than they would have been in any of their earlier adventures. We will leave them to smoke and chat and boast, since we have no further need of them at present.
