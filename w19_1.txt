Alice had been sitting by the riverbank next to her older sister for a long time, and she was growing extremely tired. She had absolutely nothing to occupy her, and the whole day felt heavy and boring. To pass the time, she looked into the book her sister was reading, but she was quickly disappointed. The book contained only pages of text; there were no drawings, no pictures, and no quoted conversations. "What good is a book," Alice thought, sighing, "if it doesn't have fun pictures or people talking to each other?"

The day was very hot, which made Alice feel sleepy and a little slow-witted, so she tried to decide on a simple activity. She was thinking about whether the enjoyment she would get from making a pretty daisy-chain would be worth the small amount of effort it would take to stand up and gather the flowers. She was nearly dozing off when, without any warning, a **White Rabbit** with nervous pink eyes suddenly ran right past her.

At first, Alice didn't think this was strange at all; a rabbit running across a field is common. She didn't even find it very unusual to hear the Rabbit speak to itself, sounding stressed and worried.

“Oh dear! Oh dear! I shall be late!” the Rabbit squeaked as it hurried on its way. (When Alice thought about this later, she realized she should have been shocked to hear a rabbit talk, but at the moment, it all seemed perfectly normal.)

However, the next thing the Rabbit did made Alice jump up immediately. The Rabbit actually pulled a **watch** out of a little pocket in its jacket—a *waistcoat-pocket*—looked at the time, and then rushed away. A sudden, bright idea flashed through Alice’s mind: she had never, ever seen a rabbit that wore a waistcoat, and certainly not one that carried a pocket watch! Filled with a fierce, burning curiosity, she ran across the field as fast as she could after the strange creature. She was just in time to see it disappear, or "pop," down a large, dark rabbit-hole hidden under a thick hedge.

Without pausing for a second to think about what she was doing or how she would escape, Alice went down the hole right after it.

The rabbit-hole started out like a straight tunnel, running for a distance, but then it dropped suddenly and sharply downwards. The change was so unexpected that Alice didn't even have a moment to try and stop herself before she found herself falling down what seemed like a well of enormous depth.

Alice fell either very slowly or the well was truly deep, because she had plenty of time on the way down to look around and wonder what would happen next. She tried to look down to see the bottom, but it was too dark to see anything clearly. Then she looked at the walls of the well as they rushed past. She saw that they were lined with wooden cupboards and shelves stacked with books. Maps and pictures were hanging here and there on small pegs. She reached out and took a jar from one of the shelves as she floated by. It had a label that said “ORANGE MARMALADE,” but to her great disappointment, it was empty. She decided she shouldn't just drop the empty jar, in case it fell and hurt someone below, so she carefully tucked it into one of the cupboards as she passed.

“Well!” Alice thought to herself, trying to sound brave. “After falling this far, I will never worry about tumbling down the stairs again! Everyone at home will think I am so courageous! I wouldn’t even bother mentioning it if I fell off the roof of our house!” (Which, in her current state of mind, was probably true.)

Down, down, down she went. Would this fall never end? “I wonder how many miles I’ve fallen by now?” she said aloud. “I must be getting close to the center of the earth. Let me calculate: that would be four thousand miles down, I think.” (Alice had learned a few facts like this in her school lessons, and even though there was no one listening, it was good practice to repeat her knowledge.) “Yes, that sounds about right. But then, I wonder what Latitude or Longitude I've reached?” (Alice had no real understanding of what Latitude or Longitude meant, but they were certainly impressive words to say.)

Soon, she started talking again. “I wonder if I will fall completely *through* the earth! How funny it will be to come out among the people who walk upside-down! The Antipathies, I believe they are called.” (She was secretly relieved that no one was listening this time, as "Antipathies" didn't sound like the right word.) “But I will have to ask them the country’s name. I'll say, 'Excuse me, Ma'am, is this New Zealand or Australia?'” (And she attempted a little formal bow, or **curtsey**, as she was still falling through the air—a very difficult thing to manage!) “And then she’ll think I’m such an ignorant little girl for asking! No, I absolutely mustn't ask. Perhaps I will see the name written on a sign somewhere.”

Down, down, down. Since she had nothing else to do, Alice kept up her conversation. “Dinah will miss me terribly tonight, I’m sure!” (Dinah was her beloved cat.) “I hope they remember to put out her saucer of milk at teatime. Dinah, my dear! I wish you could be here with me! There are no mice flying around, I'm afraid, but you might catch a bat, and a bat is quite similar to a mouse, you know. But do cats eat bats? I wonder.” At this point, Alice was growing very sleepy and kept saying to herself, in a slow, dreamy way, “Do cats eat bats? Do cats eat bats?” and sometimes, in her confusion, “Do bats eat cats?” Since she couldn't answer the question either way, it didn't really matter how she phrased it. She was just starting to drift off, beginning a dream where she was walking hand-in-hand with Dinah, asking her very seriously, “Now, Dinah, you must tell me the truth: did you ever actually eat a bat?” when suddenly, **thump! thump!** She landed heavily on a large pile of sticks and dry leaves, and the fall was finally over.

Alice was completely unharmed. She jumped instantly to her feet. She looked up, but it was pitch black overhead. Before her was another long passage, and she could still see the White Rabbit hurrying down it. There was no time to waste. Alice ran like the wind and was just close enough to hear the Rabbit say, as it quickly turned a corner, “Oh my ears and whiskers, how dreadfully late it’s getting!” She rounded the corner just after him, but the Rabbit had vanished. She found herself in a wide, low hall, lit by a row of glass lamps hanging from the roof.

There were doors all around the hall, but every single one of them was locked. Alice walked all the way down one side and back up the other, desperately trying every door. Finally, she walked slowly down the center of the hall, feeling very sad, wondering how she would ever manage to escape this strange place.

Suddenly, she saw a small table with three legs, made entirely of solid, clear glass. The only thing resting on it was a tiny golden key. Alice immediately thought it must belong to one of the doors. But sadly, either the key was much too small, or the door locks were far too large, because the key would not open any of them. However, on her second time around the room, she noticed a low curtain she hadn't seen before. Behind the curtain was a very little door, only about fifteen inches high. She tried the small golden key in this lock, and to her great joy, it fit perfectly!

Alice opened the door. It led into a small passage, no bigger than a rat-hole. She knelt down and looked through the passage into the most beautiful garden she had ever imagined. She desperately wanted to get out of the dark hall and wander among the bright flower beds and cool fountains, but she couldn't even fit her head through the small doorway. "Even if my head would go through," poor Alice thought, "it would be absolutely useless without my shoulders. Oh, how I wish I could shut up and shrink like a telescope! I really think I could, if only I knew how to begin." So many strange and unusual things had happened to her lately that Alice was starting to believe that almost nothing was actually impossible.

There was no point in waiting by the little door, so she went back to the glass table, half-hoping to find another key, or maybe a book of instructions for shrinking people like telescopes. This time, she noticed a little bottle on the table. (“That definitely was not here a moment ago,” said Alice). Around the bottle’s neck was a paper label with the words “DRINK ME” clearly and beautifully printed in large letters.

It was very easy to suggest "Drink me," but Alice was sensible and decided not to rush. “No, I must look first,” she said, “and see if the bottle is marked ‘poison’ or not.” She had read many stories about careless children who were burned, eaten by wild animals, or met other unpleasant ends, all because they ignored the simple rules they were taught. These rules included basic facts: a red-hot poker will burn you if you hold it too long; if you cut your finger deeply with a knife, it will bleed; and she never forgot that drinking from a bottle marked “poison” will certainly make you ill, sooner or later.

However, the bottle was *not* marked “poison.” So, Alice dared to take a taste. She found the liquid to be incredibly delicious. It tasted like a mixture of cherry-tart, creamy custard, sweet pine-apple, savory roast turkey, chewy toffee, and hot buttered toast—all at once. It was so wonderful that she quickly drank every last drop.

“What a very strange feeling!” Alice remarked. “I must be shrinking down like a telescope closing.”

And indeed, she was. She was now only ten inches high. Her face brightened up immediately with the thought that she was finally the perfect size to go through the little door and into the lovely garden. However, she waited for a few minutes to make sure she wouldn't shrink any further. She felt a little nervous about continuing to shrink. “Because it might end,” Alice worried to herself, “with me disappearing completely, like a candle flame going out. I wonder what I would look like then?” She tried to imagine what a candle flame looks like after the fire is put out, but she couldn't remember ever having seen such a thing.

After a while, she realized that she was not shrinking any more, so she decided to go straight into the garden. But alas for poor Alice! When she got to the little door, she remembered that she had left the tiny golden key on the glass table. When she rushed back for it, she realized she was now far too small to reach it. She could see the key clearly through the glass tabletop, and she tried her hardest to climb up one of the table legs, but the glass was too smooth and slippery. After tiring herself out with trying, the poor little thing sat down on the floor and began to cry.

“Now stop this! There is no use in crying like that!” Alice said to herself quite sharply. “I order you to stop right this minute!” Alice was always good at giving herself excellent advice (though she rarely followed it). Sometimes, she would scold herself so fiercely that it brought tears to her own eyes. She even remembered one time trying to hit her own ears because she had cheated herself in a game of croquet she was playing alone—this strange child loved to pretend she was two different people. “But there is no point now,” Alice sadly thought, “in pretending to be two people! Why, there is hardly enough of me left to make *one* respectable person!”

Soon, her eye caught sight of a small glass box lying under the table. She opened it and found a very small cake inside. On the cake, the words “EAT ME” were clearly spelled out in little currants. “Well, I'll eat it,” Alice decided. “If it makes me grow larger, I can reach the key. And if it makes me grow even smaller, I can creep right under the door. So either way, I will definitely get into the garden, and I don't really care which way it happens!”

She took a small bite of the cake and said nervously to herself, “Which way is it? Which way?” She held her hand on top of her head, waiting to feel if she was shrinking or growing. She was quite surprised when she found that she remained exactly the same size. Of course, this is usually what happens when someone eats cake, but Alice had become so used to expecting only the most unusual and impossible things to occur that it seemed completely dull and stupid for life to continue in the common, expected way.

So, she set to work and, without further hesitation, very quickly finished off the whole cake.
