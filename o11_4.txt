
Then all three of them were very happy. The huntsman took the wolf's skin and went home with it; the grandmother ate the cake and drank the wine that Red-Cap had brought, and started to feel better. Red-Cap thought to herself: 'For the rest of my life, I will never leave the path on my own and run into the forest, when my mother has told me not to.'

It is also told that one time when Red-Cap was again taking cakes to the old grandmother, another wolf spoke to her, and tried to get her to leave the path. Red-Cap was careful this time, and kept walking straight ahead, and told her grandmother that she had met the wolf, and that he had said 'good morning' to her, but with such an evil look in his eyes, that if they had not been on the main road she was sure he would have eaten her up. 'Well,' said the grandmother, 'we will close the door, so he cannot come in.' Soon after that the wolf knocked, and called out: 'Open the door, grandmother, I am Little Red-Cap, and I am bringing you some cakes.' But they did not speak, or open the door, so the wolf walked around the house two or three times, and finally climbed onto the roof, planning to wait until Red-Cap went home in the evening, and then to follow her and eat her in the dark. But the grandmother understood what he was planning. In front of the house there was a large stone trough, so she said to the child: 'Take the bucket, Red-Cap; I made some sausages yesterday, so carry the water I cooked them in to the trough.' Red-Cap kept carrying water until the large trough was completely full. Then the smell of the sausages reached the wolf, and he sniffed and looked down, and finally stretched his neck out so far that he lost his balance and started to slip, and slipped down from the roof straight into the big trough, and drowned. But Red-Cap walked happily home, and nobody ever hurt her again.
