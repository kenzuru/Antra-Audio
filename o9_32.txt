
  The "China," by leaving, seemed to have taken away Phileas Fogg's last hope. None of the other steamships could help him. The "Pereire," of the French Transatlantic Company, whose excellent ships were equal to any in speed and comfort, did not leave until the 14th. The Hamburg ships did not go directly to Liverpool or London but to Havre, and the extra journey from Havre to Southampton would make Mr. Fogg's last efforts useless. The Inman steamer did not leave until the next day, and could not cross the Atlantic in time to win the bet.

Mr. Fogg found all this out by looking at his "Bradshaw," which gave him the daily schedules of the Atlantic steamers.

Passepartout felt crushed. Missing the ship by three-quarters of an hour was too much. It was his fault — instead of helping his master, he had done nothing but put obstacles in his way! When he thought back over all the events of the trip, when he counted up all the money spent pointlessly and because of him, when he remembered that the huge bet, added to the heavy costs of this useless journey, would completely ruin Mr. Fogg, he blamed himself bitterly. Mr. Fogg, however, did not blame him at all, and after leaving the Cunard pier, only said: "We will decide what is best tomorrow. Come."

The group crossed the Hudson on the Jersey City ferry, then took a carriage to the St. Nicholas Hotel on Broadway. Rooms were booked, and the night passed — quickly for Phileas Fogg, who slept deeply, but very slowly for Aouda and the others, whose worry would not let them rest.

The next day was the 12th of December. From seven in the morning of the 12th to a quarter before nine in the evening of the 21st, there were nine days, thirteen hours, and forty-five minutes. If Phileas Fogg had left on the "China," one of the fastest steamers on the Atlantic, he would have reached Liverpool and then London within the agreed time.

Mr. Fogg left the hotel alone, after telling Passepartout to wait for him and letting Aouda know to be ready to leave at any moment. He went to the banks of the Hudson and looked at the ships tied up or anchored in the river, searching for any that were about to leave. Several were showing departure signals and preparing to sail on the morning tide — because in this huge and busy port, there is hardly a day when ships do not set out for every part of the world. But most were sailing ships, which were of no use to Phileas Fogg.

He was almost ready to give up all hope when he spotted, anchored near the Battery, at most a short distance away, a cargo ship with a propeller and a good shape, whose chimney was blowing out smoke — a sign that it was getting ready to leave.

Phileas Fogg called a small boat, got in, and was soon on board the "Henrietta," a ship with an iron bottom and a wooden upper part. He went up to the deck and asked for the captain, who came forward immediately. He was a man of about fifty, a real sea creature, with large eyes, a copper-coloured face, red hair and a thick neck, and a rough voice.

"The captain?" asked Mr. Fogg.

"I am the captain."

"I am Phileas Fogg, of London."

"And I am Andrew Speedy, of Cardiff."

"Are you about to sail?"

"In an hour."

"Where are you going?"

"Bordeaux."

"And your cargo?"

"No cargo. Sailing empty."

"Do you have passengers?"

"No passengers. Never take passengers. Too much trouble."

"Is your ship fast?"

"Between eleven and twelve knots. The 'Henrietta' — well known."

"Will you take me and three other people to Liverpool?"

"To Liverpool? Why not to China?"

"I said Liverpool."

"No!"

"No?"

"No. I am leaving for Bordeaux, and I will go to Bordeaux."

"Money is no problem?"

"None."

The captain spoke in a way that allowed no argument.

"But the owners of the 'Henrietta'—" said Phileas Fogg.

"The owners are me," replied the captain. "The ship belongs to me."

"I will pay you for the trip."

"No."

"I will buy it from you."

"No."

Phileas Fogg showed no sign of disappointment, but the situation was serious. This was not like Hong Kong, and the captain of the "Henrietta" was not like the captain of the "Tankadere." Until now, money had removed every obstacle. Now money was not working.

Still, some way had to be found to cross the Atlantic — unless by balloon, which would be too risky and impossible anyway. It seemed that Phileas Fogg had an idea, because he said to the captain: "Well, will you take me to Bordeaux?"

"No, not even if you paid me two hundred dollars."

"I offer you two thousand."

"Each person?"

"Each person."

"And there are four of you?"

"Four."

Captain Speedy began to scratch his head. There were eight thousand dollars to earn, without changing his route — which was worth overcoming his dislike of passengers. Besides, passengers paying two thousand dollars are no longer just passengers — they are valuable goods. "I leave at nine o'clock," said Captain Speedy simply. "Are you and your group ready?"

"We will be on board at nine o'clock," replied Mr. Fogg, just as simply.

It was half past eight. Getting off the "Henrietta," jumping into a carriage, rushing to the St. Nicholas Hotel, and returning with Aouda, Passepartout, and even the constant Fix, was quick work — done by Mr. Fogg with his usual calmness. They were on board when the "Henrietta" was ready to lift its anchor.

When Passepartout heard how much this last voyage would cost, he let out a long "Oh!" that went through every note of his voice.

As for Fix, he thought to himself that the Bank of England would certainly not come out of all this well covered. Even when they reached England, more than seven thousand pounds would have been spent — if Mr. Fogg did not actually throw some bank notes into the sea!
