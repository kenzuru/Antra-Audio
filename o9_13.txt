

The plan was a brave one, full of difficulty, and perhaps impossible to carry out. Mr. Fogg was going to risk his life, or at least his freedom, and therefore the success of his journey. But he did not hesitate, and he found in Sir Francis Cromarty an eager and willing partner.

As for Passepartout, he was ready for anything that might be suggested. His master's idea filled him with joy. He could see a heart and a soul beneath that cold surface. He began to truly like Phileas Fogg.

The guide was another matter. What would he do? Would he side with the Indians? If he would not help them, they needed at least to be sure he would stay neutral.

Sir Francis asked him directly.

"Officers," replied the guide, "I am a Parsee, and this woman is a Parsee. Tell me what to do."

"Excellent!" said Mr. Fogg.

"However," the guide continued, "it is certain that we will not only risk our lives, but also terrible suffering if we are caught."

"That is understood," replied Mr. Fogg. "I think we should wait until night before doing anything."

"I agree," said the guide.

The kind Indian then told them something about the victim. She was, he said, a famously beautiful woman of the Parsee people, and the daughter of a wealthy merchant from Bombay. She had received a fully English education in that city, and from her manners and intelligence, she could easily be mistaken for a European. Her name was Aouda. Left without parents, she had been married against her wishes to the old rajah of Bundelcund. Knowing what fate waited for her, she had tried to escape, but was caught and given by the rajah's relatives — who had a reason to want her dead — to the sacrifice from which she now seemed unable to escape.

The Parsee's story only made Mr. Fogg and his companions more determined to carry out their generous plan. It was decided that the guide would lead the elephant towards the temple of Pillaji, which he approached as quickly as possible. Half an hour later they stopped in a small wooded area, about five hundred feet from the temple, where they were well hidden. But they could clearly hear the groans and cries of the holy men.

They then discussed how to reach the victim. The guide knew the temple of Pillaji well, and said that the young woman was locked inside. Could they enter through one of the doors while all the Indians were deep in a drunken sleep? Or was it safer to try to make a hole in the walls? This could only be decided when they were there at the right moment. But it was clear that the rescue had to happen that night, and not when, at dawn, the victim would be led to the fire. Once that happened, no human help could save her.

As soon as night fell, at about six o'clock, they decided to look around the temple carefully. The cries of the holy men were just ending. The Indians were falling into the deep sleep caused by liquid opium mixed with hemp, and it might be possible to slip between them and reach the temple.

The Parsee, leading the others, moved quietly through the forest. After ten minutes they found themselves on the banks of a small stream. By the light of burning torches they could see a pile of wood, on top of which lay the preserved body of the rajah, which was to be burned with his wife. The temple, its towers rising above the trees in the growing darkness, stood a hundred steps away.

"Come!" whispered the guide.

He moved even more carefully than before through the bushes, followed by his companions. The only sound around them was the soft movement of wind through the branches.

Soon the Parsee stopped at the edge of a clearing lit up by torches. The ground was covered with groups of Indians who lay completely still in their drunken sleep. It looked like a battlefield covered with the dead. Men, women, and children lay together.

In the background, among the trees, the temple of Pillaji could be clearly seen. To the guide's disappointment, the rajah's guards, carrying torches, were watching at the doors and walking back and forth with uncovered swords. The priests were probably also watching inside.

Now convinced that forcing their way into the temple was impossible, the Parsee stopped and led his companions back. Phileas Fogg and Sir Francis Cromarty could also see that nothing could be tried from that direction. They stopped and spoke quietly together.

"It is only eight o'clock now," said the general, "and these guards might also fall asleep."

"It is not impossible," replied the Parsee.

They lay down at the foot of a tree and waited.

The time passed slowly. The guide kept going to the edge of the forest to look, but the guards continued to watch steadily by the light of their torches, and a faint light came through the windows of the temple.

They waited until midnight, but nothing changed among the guards, and it became clear that they were not going to fall asleep. The other plan had to be tried: they must make an opening in the walls of the temple. It still needed to be checked whether the priests were watching their prisoner as carefully as the soldiers were watching the door.

After a final discussion, the guide said he was ready to make the attempt, and moved forward, followed by the others. They took a long, roundabout route so they could reach the back of the temple. They arrived at the walls at about half past twelve without having met anyone. There was no guard there, and no windows or doors either.

The night was dark. The moon, which was growing smaller, barely rose above the horizon and was covered with heavy clouds. The tall trees made the darkness even deeper.

Reaching the walls was not enough. An opening had to be made, and to do this the group only had their pocket knives. Fortunately the temple walls were made of brick and wood, which could be cut through with little difficulty. Once one brick was removed, the rest would come out easily.

They began working quietly. The Parsee on one side and Passepartout on the other began removing bricks to make an opening about two feet wide. They were making good progress when suddenly a cry came from inside the temple, followed almost immediately by more cries from outside. Passepartout and the guide stopped. Had they been heard? Was the alarm being raised? Common sense told them to move back, and they did, followed by Phileas Fogg and Sir Francis. They hid again in the forest and waited for whatever noise it was to end, ready to start again without delay. But unfortunately, the guards now appeared at the back of the temple and set themselves up there, ready to stop any surprise attack.

It is difficult to describe how disappointed the group felt, having been stopped in the middle of their work. They could no longer reach the prisoner. How then could they save her? Sir Francis shook his fists in frustration, Passepartout was beside himself, and the guide ground his teeth with anger. The calm Fogg waited without showing any emotion.

"There is nothing we can do but leave," whispered Sir Francis.

"Nothing but leave," the guide repeated.

"Wait," said Fogg. "I only need to be at Allahabad by noon tomorrow."

"But what do you hope to do?" asked Sir Francis. "In a few hours it will be daylight, and—"

"The chance that seems lost now might appear again at the last moment."

Sir Francis wished he could read Phileas Fogg's thoughts. What was this calm Englishman planning? Was he thinking of rushing forward at the very moment of the sacrifice and boldly pulling the young woman from the hands of those who would kill her?

That would be complete madness, and it was hard to believe that Fogg was such a fool. Sir Francis agreed, however, to stay until the end of this terrible event. The guide led them to the back of the clearing, from where they could watch the sleeping groups.

Meanwhile Passepartout, who had climbed up and was sitting on the lower branches of a tree, was turning over an idea in his mind that had first come to him like a flash of lightning and was now firmly fixed in his head.

He had begun by saying to himself, "What madness!" and then he repeated, "But why not, after all? It's a chance — perhaps the only one. And with people as drunk as these!" Thinking this, he slipped down from the lowest branches with the smoothness of a snake. The ends of the branches nearly touched the ground.

The hours passed, and the sky was beginning to grow lighter, announcing the approach of day, though it was not yet light. This was the moment. The sleeping crowd began to stir. The tambourines sounded, and songs and shouts arose. The hour of the sacrifice had come. The doors of the temple opened wide, and a bright light came from inside. In the middle of it, Mr. Fogg and Sir Francis could see the victim. She seemed to have shaken off some of the effects of the drugs and was trying to escape from her killers. Sir Francis's heart beat quickly, and, grabbing Mr. Fogg's hand tightly, he found an open knife in it. At that moment the crowd began to move. The young woman had fallen into her heavy sleep again because of the hemp smoke, and she was carried past the holy men, who walked beside her with their wild religious shouts.

Phileas Fogg and his companions, moving into the back of the crowd, followed. In two minutes they reached the banks of the stream and stopped fifty steps from the pile of wood on which the rajah's body still lay. In the half-darkness they could see the victim, completely senseless, stretched out beside her husband's body. Then a torch was brought, and the wood, heavily soaked in oil, immediately caught fire.

At this moment Sir Francis and the guide grabbed Phileas Fogg, who, in a moment of brave but unthinking generosity, was about to rush toward the fire. But he had quickly pushed them aside when the whole scene suddenly changed. A cry of terror rose from the crowd. Everyone fell to the ground in terror, their faces down.

The old rajah was not dead after all — he rose suddenly, like a ghost, took his wife in his arms, and came down from the burning pile of wood, surrounded by clouds of smoke that made him look even more like a ghost.

The holy men, soldiers, and priests, instantly filled with terror, lay there, their faces on the ground, not daring to look up and see such a miracle.

The woman who seemed to have no life in her was carried along by strong arms that held her easily. Mr. Fogg and Sir Francis stood upright, the Parsee bowed his head, and Passepartout was no doubt equally amazed.

The apparently dead rajah came up to Sir Francis and Mr. Fogg and said in a sharp voice, "Let's go!"

It was Passepartout himself, who had climbed onto the burning pile in the middle of the smoke and, taking advantage of the darkness that still hung over everything, had saved the young woman from death! It was Passepartout who, acting his part with brave and clever daring, had walked through the crowd while everyone was terrified.

A moment later all four of the group had disappeared into the forest, and the elephant was carrying them away at great speed. But the shouts and noise, and a bullet that passed through Phileas Fogg's hat, told them that their trick had been discovered.

The old rajah's body had indeed now appeared on the burning pile, and the priests, having recovered from their terror, realised that someone had been taken away. They rushed into the forest, followed by soldiers who fired after the fleeing group. But those being chased quickly put more and more distance between themselves and their pursuers, and before long were far beyond the reach of bullets and arrows.
