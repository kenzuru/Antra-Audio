

  Our breakfast table was cleared early, and Holmes waited in his dressing-gown for the promised meeting. Our clients arrived on time, for the clock had just struck ten when Dr. Mortimer was shown up, followed by the young baronet. The latter was a small, alert, dark-eyed man of about thirty years old, very strongly built, with thick black eyebrows and a strong, determined face. He wore a reddish-brown tweed suit and had the weather-beaten look of someone who has spent most of his time outdoors, and yet there was something in his steady gaze and the quiet confidence of his manner which showed he was a gentleman.

"This is Sir Henry Baskerville," said Dr. Mortimer.

"Why, yes," said he, "and the strange thing is, Mr. Sherlock Holmes, that if my friend here had not suggested coming round to you this morning I should have come on my own account. I understand that you solve little puzzles, and I've had one this morning which needs more thinking out than I can give it."

"Please take a seat, Sir Henry. Do I understand you to say that you have yourself had some remarkable experience since you arrived in London?"

"Nothing of much importance, Mr. Holmes. Only a joke, most likely. It was this letter, if you can call it that, which reached me this morning."

He placed an envelope on the table, and we all bent over it. It was made of ordinary quality gray paper. The address, "Sir Henry Baskerville, Northumberland Hotel," was printed in rough letters; the postmark "Charing Cross," and the date of posting the previous evening.

"Who knew that you were going to the Northumberland Hotel?" asked Holmes, looking sharply at our visitor.

"No one could have known. We only decided after I met Dr. Mortimer."

"But Dr. Mortimer was already staying there, no doubt?"

"No, I had been staying with a friend," said the doctor. "There was no way anyone could have known we intended to go to that hotel."

"Hmm! Someone seems to be very deeply interested in your movements." From the envelope he took a half-sheet of paper folded in four. This he opened and spread flat on the table. Across the middle of it a single sentence had been made by pasting printed words on it. It read: "As you value your life or your reason keep away from the moor." The word "moor" only was written in ink.

"Now," said Sir Henry Baskerville, "perhaps you will tell me, Mr. Holmes, what on earth this means, and who it is that takes so much interest in my affairs?"

"What do you make of it, Dr. Mortimer? You must admit that there is nothing supernatural about this, at any rate?"

"No, sir, but it might very well come from someone who believed the business to be supernatural."

"What business?" asked Sir Henry sharply. "It seems to me that all you gentlemen know a great deal more than I do about my own affairs."

"You shall share our knowledge before you leave this room, Sir Henry. I promise you that," said Sherlock Holmes. "For the moment, with your permission, let us stay with this very interesting document, which must have been put together and posted yesterday evening. Have you yesterday's Times, Watson?"

"It is here in the corner."

"Could I trouble you for it — the inside page, please, with the main articles?" He looked quickly over it, running his eyes up and down the columns. "Excellent article this on free trade. Allow me to read you a passage from it. 'You may be persuaded into thinking that your own special trade or your own industry will be helped by a protective tax, but it stands to reason that such a law must in the long run keep wealth away from the country, reduce the value of our imports, and lower the general conditions of life in this island.' What do you think of that, Watson?" cried Holmes with great happiness, rubbing his hands together with satisfaction. "Don't you think that is an admirable point?"

Dr. Mortimer looked at Holmes with an air of professional interest, and Sir Henry Baskerville turned a pair of puzzled dark eyes upon me.

"I don't know much about taxes and things of that kind," said he; "but it seems to me we've got a bit off the trail so far as that note is concerned."

"On the contrary, I think we are very close to the trail, Sir Henry. Watson here knows more about my methods than you do, but I fear that even he has not quite grasped the importance of this sentence."

"No, I confess that I see no connection."

"And yet, my dear Watson, there is such a close connection that one is taken from the other. 'You,' 'your,' 'your,' 'life,' 'reason,' 'value,' 'keep away,' 'from the.' Don't you see now where these words have been taken from?"

"By thunder, you're right! Well, if that isn't clever!" cried Sir Henry.

"If any possible doubt remained it is settled by the fact that 'keep away' and 'from the' were cut out in one piece."

"Well, now — so it is!"

"Really, Mr. Holmes, this is beyond anything I could have imagined," said Dr. Mortimer, staring at my friend in amazement. "I could understand anyone saying that the words were from a newspaper; but that you should name which paper, and add that it came from the main article, is really one of the most remarkable things I have ever seen. How did you do it?"

"I presume, Doctor, that you could tell the skull of one race from another?"

"Most certainly."

"But how?"

"Because that is my special hobby. The differences are obvious. The shape of the forehead, the face angle, the jaw —"

"But this is my special hobby, and the differences are equally obvious. There is as much difference to my eyes between the type used in a Times article and the messy print of a cheap evening paper as there could be between two different skulls. The identification of print types is one of the most basic areas of knowledge for the expert in crime, though I confess that once when I was very young I confused the Leeds Mercury with the Western Morning News. But a Times main article is completely distinctive, and these words could not have come from anything else. As it was done yesterday the strong probability was that we should find the words in yesterday's issue."

"So far as I can follow you, then, Mr. Holmes," said Sir Henry Baskerville, "someone cut out this message with scissors —"

"Nail scissors," said Holmes. "You can see that they were very short-bladed scissors, since the person had to make two cuts over 'keep away.'"

"That is so. Someone, then, cut out the message with a pair of short-bladed scissors, pasted it with paste —"

"Glue," said Holmes.

"With glue onto the paper. But I want to know why the word 'moor' was written by hand?"

"Because he could not find it in print. The other words were all simple and could be found in any issue, but 'moor' would be less common."

"Why, of course, that would explain it. Have you read anything else in this message, Mr. Holmes?"

"There are one or two small points, and yet the greatest care has been taken to remove all clues. The address, you notice, is printed in rough letters. But the Times is a paper which is rarely found in anyone's hands except the highly educated. We may therefore assume that the letter was put together by an educated man who wanted to appear uneducated, and his effort to hide his own handwriting suggests that that handwriting might be known, or come to be known, by you. Again, you will notice that the words are not glued on in a straight line, but that some are much higher than others. 'Life,' for example, is quite out of its proper place. That may point to carelessness or it may point to nervousness and hurry on the part of the person cutting. On the whole I lean towards the second view, since the matter was clearly important, and it is unlikely that the writer of such a letter would be careless. If he was in a hurry it raises the interesting question of why he should be in a hurry, since any letter posted up to early morning would reach Sir Henry before he left his hotel. Was the writer afraid of being interrupted — and by whom?"

"We are coming now rather into the region of guesswork," said Dr. Mortimer.

"Say rather into the region where we consider probabilities and choose the most likely. It is the scientific use of the imagination, but we always have some real starting point for our guesses. Now, you would call it a guess, no doubt, but I am almost certain that this address was written in a hotel."

"How on earth can you say that?"

"If you examine it carefully you will see that both the pen and the ink have given the writer trouble. The pen has left marks twice in a single word, and has run dry three times in a short address, showing that there was very little ink in the bottle. Now, a private pen or ink bottle is seldom allowed to get into such a state, and the combination of the two must be quite rare. But you know the hotel ink and the hotel pen, where it is rare to get anything better. Yes, I have very little hesitation in saying that if we could examine the waste-paper baskets of the hotels around Charing Cross until we found the remains of the cut-up Times article we could find the person who sent this unusual message. Hello! Hello! What's this?"

He was carefully examining the paper on which the words were pasted, holding it only an inch or two from his eyes.

"Well?"

"Nothing," said he, putting it down. "It is a blank half-sheet of paper, without even a watermark on it. I think we have drawn as much as we can from this curious letter; and now, Sir Henry, has anything else of interest happened to you since you have been in London?"

"Why, no, Mr. Holmes. I think not."

"You have not noticed anyone following or watching you?"

"I seem to have walked right into the middle of a cheap thriller," said our visitor. "Why on earth should anyone follow or watch me?"

"We are coming to that. You have nothing else to report to us before we go into this matter?"

"Well, it depends upon what you think worth reporting."

"I think anything outside the ordinary routine of life well worth reporting."

Sir Henry smiled.

"I don't know much of British life yet, for I have spent nearly all my time in the States and in Canada. But I hope that losing one of your boots is not part of the ordinary routine of life over here."

"You have lost one of your boots?"

"My dear sir," cried Dr. Mortimer, "it has only been misplaced. You will find it when you return to the hotel. What is the use of troubling Mr. Holmes with small things like this?"

"Well, he asked me for anything unusual."

"Exactly," said Holmes, "however foolish the incident may seem. You have lost one of your boots, you say?"

"Well, misplaced it, anyway. I put both of them outside my door last night, and there was only one in the morning. I could get no sense out of the man who cleans them. The worst of it is that I only bought the pair last night in the Strand, and I had never worn them."

"If you had never worn them, why did you put them out to be cleaned?"

"They were tan boots and had never been polished. That was why I put them out."

"Then I understand that when you arrived in London yesterday you went out at once and bought a pair of boots?"

"I did a good deal of shopping. Dr. Mortimer here went round with me. You see, if I am to be the man of the house down there I must dress the part, and it may be that I have become a little careless in my ways out West. Among other things I bought these brown boots — paid six dollars for them — and had one stolen before I had even worn them."

"It seems a strangely useless thing to steal," said Sherlock Holmes. "I confess that I share Dr. Mortimer's belief that it will not be long before the missing boot is found."

"And now, gentlemen," said the baronet with decision, "it seems to me that I have spoken quite enough about the little that I know. It is time that you kept your promise and gave me a full account of what we are all talking about."

"Your request is very reasonable," Holmes answered. "Dr. Mortimer, I think you could not do better than to tell your story as you told it to us."

Encouraged in this way, our scientific friend took his papers from his pocket and presented the whole case as he had done the morning before. Sir Henry Baskerville listened with deep attention, and with an occasional exclamation of surprise.

"Well, I seem to have come into an inheritance with a vengeance," said he when the long story was finished. "Of course, I've heard of the hound ever since I was a child. It's the pet story of the family, though I never thought of taking it seriously before. But as to my uncle's death — well, it is all mixing up in my head, and I can't get it clear yet. You don't seem quite to have made up your mind whether it's a case for a policeman or a clergyman."

"Precisely."

"And now there's this affair of the letter to me at the hotel. I suppose that fits into its place."

"It seems to show that someone knows more than we do about what goes on upon the moor," said Dr. Mortimer.

"And also," said Holmes, "that someone does not wish you harm, since they warn you of danger."

"Or it may be that they wish, for their own purposes, to frighten me away."

"Well, of course, that is possible also. I am very much indebted to you, Dr. Mortimer, for introducing me to a problem which presents several interesting possibilities. But the practical point which we now have to decide, Sir Henry, is whether or not it is advisable for you to go to Baskerville Hall."

"Why should I not go?"

"There seems to be danger."

"Do you mean danger from this family monster or do you mean danger from human beings?"

"Well, that is what we have to find out."

"Whichever it is, my answer is fixed. There is no devil in hell, Mr. Holmes, and there is no man on earth who can prevent me from going to the home of my own people, and you may take that to be my final answer." His dark brows came together and his face went a dark red as he spoke. It was clear that the fiery temper of the Baskervilles was not gone in this last member of the family. "Meanwhile," said he, "I have hardly had time to think over all that you have told me. It's a big thing for a man to have to understand and decide at one sitting. I should like to have a quiet hour by myself to make up my mind. Now, look here, Mr. Holmes, it's half past eleven now and I am going back right away to my hotel. Suppose you and your friend, Dr. Watson, come round and have lunch with us at two. I'll be able to tell you more clearly then how this thing strikes me."

"Is that convenient to you, Watson?"

"Perfectly."

"Then you may expect us. Shall I have a cab called?"

"I'd prefer to walk, for this matter has unsettled me rather."

"I'll join you in a walk, with pleasure," said his companion.

"Then we meet again at two o'clock. Goodbye, and good morning!"

We heard the steps of our visitors going down the stairs and the bang of the front door. In an instant Holmes had changed from the relaxed dreamer to the man of action.

"Your hat and boots, Watson, quick! Not a moment to lose!" He rushed into his room in his dressing-gown and was back again in a few seconds in a proper coat. We hurried together down the stairs and into the street. Dr. Mortimer and Baskerville were still visible about two hundred yards ahead of us in the direction of Oxford Street.

"Shall I run and stop them?"

"Not for the world, my dear Watson. I am perfectly happy with your company if you will put up with mine. Our friends are wise, for it is certainly a very fine morning for a walk."

He walked faster until we had reduced the distance between us by about half. Then, still keeping about a hundred yards behind, we followed them into Oxford Street and then down Regent Street. Once our friends stopped and looked in a shop window, and Holmes did the same. An instant later he gave a small cry of satisfaction, and, following the direction of his eager eyes, I saw that a hansom cab with a man inside, which had stopped on the other side of the street, was now moving slowly forward again.

"There's our man, Watson! Come along! We'll have a good look at him, if we can do no more."

At that moment I saw a bushy black beard and a pair of sharp eyes turned upon us through the side window of the cab. Instantly the trapdoor at the top flew open, something was shouted to the driver, and the cab rushed madly off down Regent Street. Holmes looked eagerly around for another cab, but no empty one was in sight. Then he ran wildly in pursuit through the traffic, but the start was too great, and already the cab was out of sight.

"There now!" said Holmes bitterly as he came back, breathless and pale with anger, from among the vehicles. "Was there ever such bad luck and such bad management, too? Watson, Watson, if you are an honest man you will record this also and set it against my successes!"

"Who was the man?"

"I have no idea."

"A spy?"

"Well, it was clear from what we have heard that Baskerville has been very closely watched by someone since he has been in town. How else could it be known so quickly that it was the Northumberland Hotel that he had chosen? If they had followed him on the first day I thought they would follow him again on the second. You may have noticed that I walked to the window twice while Dr. Mortimer was reading his legend."

"Yes, I remember."

"I was looking for people waiting in the street, but I saw none. We are dealing with a clever man, Watson. This matter goes very deep, and though I have not finally made up my mind whether it is a helpful or a harmful person who is in touch with us, I am always conscious of power and planning. When our friends left I immediately followed them in the hope of identifying their invisible follower. He was so careful that he had not trusted himself on foot, but had used a cab so that he could go slowly behind or drive quickly past them and so escape their notice. His method had the additional advantage that if they were to take a cab he was ready to follow them. It has, however, one obvious disadvantage."

"It puts him at the mercy of the cabman."

"Exactly."

"What a pity we did not get the number!"

"My dear Watson, clumsy as I have been, you surely do not seriously imagine that I forgot to get the number? No. 2704 is our man. But that is no use to us at the moment."

"I fail to see how you could have done more."

"On seeing the cab I should have immediately turned and walked in the other direction. I should then at my leisure have hired a second cab and followed the first at a safe distance, or, better still, have driven to the Northumberland Hotel and waited there. When our unknown person had followed Baskerville home we should have had the opportunity of playing his own game back on him and seeing where he went. As it is, by a rash eagerness, which was taken advantage of with extraordinary quickness and energy by our opponent, we have given ourselves away and lost our man."

We had been walking slowly down Regent Street during this conversation, and Dr. Mortimer, with his companion, had long disappeared ahead of us.

"There is no point in following them," said Holmes. "The shadow has left and will not return. We must see what other cards we hold and play them with decision. Could you swear to that man's face in the cab?"

"I could swear only to the beard."

"And so could I — from which I gather that in all probability it was a false one. A clever man on such a delicate job has no use for a beard except to hide his features. Come in here, Watson!"

He turned into one of the district messenger offices, where he was warmly greeted by the manager.

"Ah, Wilson, I see you have not forgotten the little case in which I had the good fortune to help you?"

"No, sir, indeed I have not. You saved my good name, and perhaps my life."

"My dear friend, you exaggerate. I have some memory, Wilson, that you had among your boys a lad named Cartwright, who showed some ability during the investigation."

"Yes, sir, he is still with us."

"Could you call him up? — thank you! And I should be glad to have change of this five-pound note."

A boy of fourteen, with a bright, sharp face, had answered the manager's call. He stood now looking with great respect at the famous detective.

"Let me have the Hotel Directory," said Holmes. "Thank you! Now, Cartwright, there are the names of twenty-three hotels here, all in the immediate neighbourhood of Charing Cross. Do you see?"

"Yes, sir."

"You will visit each of these in turn."

"Yes, sir."

"You will begin in each case by giving the outside porter one shilling. Here are twenty-three shillings."

"Yes, sir."

"You will tell him that you want to see the waste-paper of yesterday. You will say that an important telegram has been lost and that you are looking for it. You understand?"

"Yes, sir."

"But what you are really looking for is the centre page of the Times with some holes cut in it with scissors. Here is a copy of the Times. It is this page. You could easily recognise it, could you not?"

"Yes, sir."

"In each case the outside porter will send for the hall porter, to whom also you will give a shilling. Here are twenty-three shillings. You will then learn in perhaps twenty cases out of the twenty-three that the waste-paper of the day before has been burned or removed. In the three other cases you will be shown a pile of paper and you will look for this page of the Times among it. The chances are very much against your finding it. There are ten shillings extra in case of emergencies. Let me have a report by telegram at Baker Street before evening. And now, Watson, it only remains for us to find out by telegram the identity of the cabman, No. 2704, and then we will drop into one of the Bond Street art galleries and fill in the time until we are due at the hotel."
