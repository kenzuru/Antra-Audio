TOM was a shining hero once more -- the favorite of the old, the envy of the young. His name even appeared in print, for the village newspaper praised him. Some believed he would become President yet, if he managed to avoid hanging.

As usual, the changing, thoughtless world took Muff Potter to its heart and treated him kindly, just as it had treated him badly before. But that kind of behavior shows the world in a good light; therefore it is not right to criticize it.

Tom's days were days of glory and great joy to him, but his nights were full of horror. Injun Joe filled all his dreams, always with revenge in his eye. Almost no temptation could get the boy to go outside after dark. Poor Huck was in the same state of misery and fear, for Tom had told the whole story to the lawyer the night before the great trial day, and Huck was very afraid that his part in the business might become known yet, even though Injun Joe's escape had saved him from having to testify in court. The poor fellow had made the lawyer promise secrecy, but what good was that? Since Tom's troubled conscience had driven him to the lawyer's house at night and forced a terrible story from lips that had been sealed with the most serious of oaths, Huck's trust in people was almost completely destroyed.

Every day Muff Potter's thankfulness made Tom glad he had spoken; but every night he wished he had kept his mouth shut.

Half the time Tom was afraid Injun Joe would never be caught; the other half he was afraid he would be. He felt sure he could never breathe easily again until that man was dead and he had seen the body.

Rewards had been offered, the surrounding area had been thoroughly searched, but no Injun Joe was found. One of those all-knowing and impressive figures, a detective, came up from St. Louis, looked around, shook his head, looked clever, and achieved that kind of success which members of that profession usually achieve. That is to say, he "found a clue." But you can't hang a "clue" for murder, and so after that detective had finished and gone home, Tom felt just as unsafe as before.

The slow days moved on, and each left behind it a slightly smaller weight of worry.
