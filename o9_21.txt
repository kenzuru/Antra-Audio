
  This journey of eight hundred miles was very dangerous on a small boat of twenty tons, and at that time of year. The Chinese seas are usually rough, with terrible storms, especially during the autumn season. It was now the beginning of November.

It would clearly have been better for the captain to take his passengers to Yokohama, since he was paid a certain amount per day. But it would have been foolish to try such a voyage, and it was even unwise to try to reach Shanghai. However, John Bunsby had faith in the "Tankadere," which moved over the waves like a seagull. And perhaps he was right.

Late in the day they sailed through the difficult waters of Hong Kong, and the "Tankadere," helped by good winds, performed very well.

"I don't need to tell you, pilot," said Phileas Fogg, when they reached the open sea, "to go as fast as possible."

"Trust me, your honour. We are carrying all the sail the wind allows. The extra poles would add nothing, and are only used when we are going into port."

"This is your work, not mine, pilot, and I trust you."

Phileas Fogg stood straight with his legs apart, like a sailor, and looked steadily at the rough water without losing his balance. The young woman, sitting at the back of the boat, felt deeply affected as she looked out at the ocean, now growing dark in the evening light, knowing she was on such a small vessel. Above her head the white sails moved in the wind, looking like large white wings. The boat, carried forward by the wind, seemed to fly through the air.

Night came. The moon was in its first phase, and its weak light would soon disappear into the mist on the horizon. Clouds were rising from the east and had already covered part of the sky.

The pilot had turned on his lights, which was very important in these busy seas. Crashes between ships were not rare, and at the speed they were going, the smallest shock could destroy the brave little boat.

Fix, sitting at the front, was lost in thought. He stayed away from the other travellers, knowing that Mr. Fogg liked silence. Besides, he did not feel comfortable talking to the man whose help he had accepted. He was also thinking about the future. It seemed certain that Fogg would not stop at Yokohama, but would immediately take a boat to San Francisco. The great size of America would keep him safe from the police. Fogg's plan seemed very simple to Fix. Instead of sailing directly from England to the United States, like an ordinary criminal, he had travelled three quarters of the world, so as to reach America more safely. And there, after losing the police, he would quietly enjoy the money stolen from the bank. But once in the United States, what should Fix do? Should he give up following this man? No, never! Until he had got his extradition, he would not lose sight of him for even one hour. It was his job, and he would do it until the end. At least there was one good thing: Passepartout was not with his master. After what Fix had told him, it was very important that the servant did not speak to his master.

Phileas Fogg was also thinking about Passepartout, who had disappeared so strangely. Looking at the situation from every angle, it did not seem impossible that the man had got on the "Carnatic" at the last moment by mistake. Aouda thought the same, and she felt very sorry to have lost the good man to whom she owed so much. They might find him at Yokohama. If the "Carnatic" had taken him there, it would be easy to find out if he had been on board.

A strong wind came up around ten o'clock. Although it might have been wise to reduce the sails, the pilot, after carefully watching the sky, left the boat fully rigged. The "Tankadere" carried its sails very well, as it sat deep in the water, and everything was ready for high speed if a storm came.

Mr. Fogg and Aouda went below to the cabin at midnight, where Fix had already gone to bed on one of the small beds. The pilot and crew stayed on deck all night.

At sunrise the next day, which was the 8th of November, the boat had travelled more than one hundred miles. The log showed an average speed of between eight and nine miles per hour. The "Tankadere" was still carrying all its sails and going as fast as it could. If the wind stayed the same, the chances would be in their favour. During the day the boat stayed near the coast, where the water currents helped them. The coast, with its uneven outline, was sometimes visible through the clear areas, and was at most five miles away. The sea was calmer, since the wind came from land. This was good for the boat, which, because of its small size, would suffer badly in heavy ocean waves.

The wind became weaker around noon and came from the south-west. The pilot put up his extra poles, but took them down again within two hours when the wind grew stronger again.

Mr. Fogg and Aouda, who were not bothered by the rough sea, ate with good appetite. Fix was invited to share their meal, which he accepted with hidden unhappiness. Travelling at this man's expense and eating his food was not pleasant to him. But he had to eat, and so he ate.

When the meal was over, he took Mr. Fogg to one side and said, "sir" — this word burned his lips, and he had to control himself to stop from grabbing this "gentleman" — "sir, you have been very kind to give me a place on this boat. But, although my money does not allow me to spend as freely as you, I must ask to pay my share—"

"Let us not talk about that, sir," replied Mr. Fogg.

"But if I insist—"

"No, sir," repeated Mr. Fogg, in a tone that allowed no argument. "This is part of my general expenses."

Fix bowed, feeling very uncomfortable, and went to the front of the boat, where he stayed quietly for the rest of the day.

Meanwhile they were moving well, and John Bunsby was full of hope. He told Mr. Fogg several times that they would reach Shanghai in time. Mr. Fogg replied that he was counting on it. The crew worked very hard, inspired by the reward they could earn. Every rope was pulled tight, every sail was raised with energy. No mistakes could be blamed on the person at the wheel. They worked as hard as if they were in a royal sailing race.

By evening, the log showed that two hundred and twenty miles had been covered from Hong Kong. Mr. Fogg could hope that he would reach Yokohama without recording any delay in his diary. In that case, the many problems that had happened to him since leaving London would not seriously damage his journey.

The "Tankadere" entered the Straits of Fo-Kien, which separate the island of Formosa from the Chinese coast, in the early hours of the night, and crossed the Tropic of Cancer. The sea was very rough in the straits, full of swirling water created by opposite currents. The choppy waves slowed the boat, and it became very hard to stand on deck.

At dawn the wind began to blow hard again, and the sky looked like a storm was coming. The barometer showed a quick change, the mercury moving up and down unpredictably. The sea in the south-east had long, heavy waves that meant a storm was near. The sun had set the evening before in a red mist, in the middle of the glowing flashes of the ocean.

John Bunsby studied the threatening sky for a long time, speaking quietly to himself. At last he said in a low voice to Mr. Fogg, "May I speak honestly with you, your honour?"

"Of course."

"Well, we are going to have a storm."

"Is the wind from the north or the south?" asked Mr. Fogg calmly.

"From the south. Look! A typhoon is coming."

"Good thing it's a typhoon from the south. It will carry us forward."

"Well, if you see it that way," said John Bunsby, "I have nothing more to say." John Bunsby's worries were confirmed. At a less advanced time of year, the typhoon, according to a famous weather expert, would have passed away like a bright flash of lightning. But in the winter season it was likely to hit them with great force.

The pilot prepared in advance. He reduced all sails, the extra masts were taken down, all hands moved to the front of the boat. A single triangular sail made of strong canvas was raised as a storm sail, to catch the wind from behind. Then they waited.

John Bunsby had asked his passengers to go below. But being in such a small space, with little air, while the boat was jumping in the storm, was far from pleasant. Neither Mr. Fogg, Fix, nor Aouda agreed to leave the deck.

The storm of rain and wind came upon them around eight o'clock. With just its small sail, the "Tankadere" was lifted like a feather by a wind so powerful it is hard to describe. Saying it went four times faster than a locomotive at full steam would not be strong enough.

The boat ran northward during the whole day, carried by enormous waves, always keeping, fortunately, a speed equal to theirs. Twenty times she almost seemed to go under those mountains of water rising behind her. But the clever handling of the pilot saved her. The passengers were often covered in sea spray, but accepted it calmly. Fix was surely cursing inside, but Aouda, with her eyes on her protector whose coolness amazed her, showed herself worthy of him, and bravely faced the storm. As for Phileas Fogg, it seemed as if the typhoon was simply part of his plan.

Up to this point the "Tankadere" had always kept going north. But towards evening the wind changed direction and came from the north-west. The boat, now lying low in the waves, shook and rolled terribly. The sea hit her with frightening force. At night the storm grew stronger. John Bunsby watched the growing darkness and the rising storm with deep worry. He thought for a while, then asked his crew if it was time to slow down. After a discussion he went to Mr. Fogg and said, "I think, your honour, that we should go towards one of the ports on the coast."

"I think so too."

"Ah!" said the pilot. "But which one?"

"I know only one," replied Mr. Fogg calmly.

"And that is—"

"Shanghai."

The pilot did not understand at first. He could hardly believe such determination and strength of will. Then he cried, "Well—yes! Your honour is right. To Shanghai!"

So the "Tankadere" kept steadily on her northward path.

The night was truly terrible. It would have been a miracle if the boat did not sink. Twice it could have been all over if the crew had not been watching constantly. Aouda was exhausted, but did not complain once. More than once Mr. Fogg rushed to protect her from the power of the waves.

Day came again. The storm still raged with the same fury, but the wind had now turned to the south-east. This was a helpful change, and the "Tankadere" moved forward again on the mountainous sea, although the waves crossed each other and sent strong jolts and counter-shocks that would have destroyed a less well-built boat. From time to time the coast was visible through the broken mist, but no other ship could be seen. The "Tankadere" was alone on the sea.

There were some signs of calm at noon, and these became clearer as the sun moved towards the horizon. The storm had been as short as it was terrible. The passengers, completely exhausted, could now eat a little and rest.

The night was fairly quiet. Some sails were raised again, and the speed of the boat was very good. The next morning at dawn they saw the coast, and John Bunsby could say confidently that they were not one hundred miles from Shanghai. A hundred miles, and only one day to cover them! That very evening Mr. Fogg was expected in Shanghai, if he did not want to miss the steamer to Yokohama. If there had been no storm, during which several hours were lost, they would now be within thirty miles of their destination.

The wind became clearly calmer, and happily the sea settled with it. All sails were now raised, and at noon the "Tankadere" was forty-five miles from Shanghai. There were still six hours left to cover that distance. Everyone on board feared it could not be done. Everyone — except Phileas Fogg, no doubt — felt their heart beating with impatience. The boat needed to keep an average speed of nine miles per hour, and the wind was becoming calmer every moment. It was an unpredictable breeze coming from the coast, and after it passed the sea became smooth. Still, the "Tankadere" was so light, and her fine sails caught the gentle winds so well, that, with the help of the currents, John Bunsby found himself at six o'clock not more than ten miles from the mouth of the Shanghai River. Shanghai itself is at least twelve miles up the river. At seven they were still three miles from Shanghai. The pilot said an angry word under his breath. The reward of two hundred pounds was clearly about to escape him. He looked at Mr. Fogg. Mr. Fogg was perfectly calm, even though his entire fortune was at risk at that moment.

At that same moment, a long black chimney covered with smoke appeared on the edge of the water. It was the American steamer, leaving for Yokohama at the agreed time.

"Damn it!" cried John Bunsby, pushing the rudder back with a desperate jerk.

"Signal her!" said Phileas Fogg quietly.

A small brass cannon stood on the front deck of the "Tankadere" for making signals in the fog. It was fully loaded. But just as the pilot was about to press a red-hot coal to the firing hole, Mr. Fogg said, "Raise your flag!"

The flag was raised to half-mast, and, this being the signal of danger, it was hoped that the American steamer, seeing it, would change her direction a little to help the pilot-boat.

"Fire!" said Mr. Fogg. And the loud bang of the small cannon filled the air.
