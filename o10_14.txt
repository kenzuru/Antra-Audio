

   Jo was very busy in the attic, because the October days were beginning to get cold and the afternoons were short. For two or three hours the sun shone warmly through the high window, showing Jo sitting on the old sofa, writing busily, with her papers spread out on a trunk in front of her, while Scrabble the pet rat walked along the beams above her head, accompanied by his oldest son, a fine young rat who was clearly very proud of his whiskers. Completely absorbed in her work, Jo wrote quickly until the last page was filled, then she signed her name with a flourish and threw down her pen, saying:

"There, I've done my best! If this won't do, I'll have to wait until I can do better."

Leaning back on the sofa, she read the piece carefully from beginning to end, making corrections here and there and adding many exclamation marks, which looked like little balloons. Then she tied it up with a bright red ribbon and sat for a minute looking at it with a serious, thoughtful expression that clearly showed how hard she had worked. Jo's work area up here was an old tin box hanging on the wall. In it she kept her papers and a few books, safely locked away from Scrabble, who, being also of a literary nature, enjoyed creating his own kind of library by eating the pages of any books left within his reach. From this tin storage space Jo took out another piece of writing, and putting both in her pocket, crept quietly downstairs, leaving her friends to chew on her pens and taste her ink.

She put on her hat and jacket as quietly as possible, and going to the back window, climbed out onto the roof of a low porch, swung herself down to the grassy slope, and took a long way around to the road. Once there, she composed herself, waved down a passing bus, and rode into town, looking very cheerful and secretive.

If anyone had been watching her, they would have found her behavior quite puzzling, because after getting off the bus, she walked quickly until she reached a certain address on a certain busy street. Having found the place with some difficulty, she went into the doorway, looked up the dirty staircase, and after standing completely still for a minute, suddenly went back out to the street and walked away just as fast as she had come. She repeated this action several times, to the great amusement of a dark-eyed young man who was leaning in a window of a building across the street. On returning for the third time, Jo gave herself a shake, pulled her hat down over her eyes, and walked up the stairs, looking as if she were going to have all her teeth pulled out.

There was a dentist's sign among others at the entrance, and after staring for a moment at the pair of artificial jaws that slowly opened and closed to attract attention to a fine set of false teeth, the young man put on his coat, took his hat, and went downstairs to stand in the opposite doorway, saying with a smile and a slight look of concern, "That's just like her—coming alone. But if she has a bad time she'll need someone to help her home."

In ten minutes Jo came running downstairs with a very red face and the general appearance of someone who had just gone through something difficult. When she saw the young man, she didn't look at all pleased and walked past him with only a nod. But he followed her, asking sympathetically, "Did you have a bad time?"

"Not very."

"You got through quickly."

"Yes, thank goodness!"

"Why did you go alone?"

"Didn't want anyone to know."

"You're the strangest person I've ever met. How many did you have taken out?"

Jo looked at her friend as if she didn't understand him, then began to laugh as if she found something very funny.

"There are two that I want to come out, but I'll have to wait a week."

"What are you laughing at? You're up to something, Jo," said Laurie, looking puzzled.

"So are you. What were you doing in that place across the street?"

"Excuse me, it wasn't a games room but a gym, and I was taking a fencing lesson."

"I'm glad about that."

"Why?"

"Because you could teach me, and then when we perform Hamlet, you can be Laertes and we'll make a great scene of the fencing part."

Laurie burst into a genuine laugh, which made several people passing by smile despite themselves.

"I'll teach you whether we do Hamlet or not. It's wonderful exercise and will improve your posture. But I don't believe that was the only reason you said 'I'm glad' so firmly, was it?"

"No, I was glad you weren't in a games room, because I hope you never go to such places. Do you?"

"Not often."

"I wish you wouldn't."

"There's no harm in it, Jo. I have a billiard table at home, but it's not fun unless you have good players, so since I like the game, I come here sometimes and play with Ned Moffat or some of the other boys."

"Oh dear, I'm sorry, because you'll enjoy it more and more and will waste time and money, and start to become like those unpleasant boys. I had really hoped you'd stay respectable and be a credit to your friends," said Jo, shaking her head.

"Can't a person have a little harmless fun sometimes without losing his reputation?" asked Laurie, looking annoyed.

"That depends on how and where he has it. I don't like Ned and his group, and I wish you'd stay away from them. Mother won't allow him in our house, though he wants to come. And if you become like him, she won't want us to spend time together the way we do now."

"Won't she?" asked Laurie, concerned.

"No, she can't stand fashionable young men, and she'd lock us all away rather than have us spend time with them."

"Well, she needn't worry yet. I'm not a fashionable person and don't plan to be, but I do enjoy some harmless fun now and then, don't you?"

"Yes, nobody minds that, so enjoy yourself. Just don't go too far, will you? Or our good times together will come to an end."

"I'll be an absolute saint."

"I can't stand saints. Just be a simple, honest, decent boy and we'll never abandon you. I don't know what I'd do if you behaved like Mr. King's son. He had plenty of money but didn't know how to handle it, got drunk and gambled, ran away, and I believe forged his father's name, and was altogether terrible."

"You think I'm likely to do the same? Thank you so much."

"No, I don't—oh, of course not—but I hear people saying that money is such a great temptation, and I sometimes wish you were poor. I wouldn't worry then."

"Do you worry about me, Jo?"

"A little, when you look moody and unhappy, as you sometimes do, because you have such a strong will that if you once started going in the wrong direction, I think it would be very hard to stop you."

Laurie walked in silence for a few minutes, and Jo watched him, wishing she had kept quiet, because his eyes looked angry, though his lips were smiling as if at her warnings.

"Are you going to lecture me the whole way home?" he asked eventually.

"Of course not. Why?"

"Because if you are, I'll take a bus. But if you're not, I'd like to walk with you and tell you something very interesting."

"I won't say another word about it, and I would very much like to hear the news."

"Very well, then, come on. It's a secret, and if I tell you, you must tell me yours."

"I haven't got any," began Jo, but stopped suddenly, remembering that she did have one.

"You know you do—you can't hide anything, so tell me, or I won't share mine," cried Laurie.

"Is your secret a good one?"

"Oh, it is! All about people you know, and so much fun! You really should hear it, and I've been wanting to tell you for a long time. Go on, you start."

"You won't say anything about it at home, will you?"

"Not a word."

"And you won't tease me about it in private?"

"I never tease."

"Yes, you do. You always get people to tell you everything. I don't know how you do it, but you have a natural gift for it."

"Thank you. Go ahead."

"Well, I've left two stories with a newspaper editor, and he'll give me his answer next week," whispered Jo into her friend's ear.

"Hurray for Miss March, the famous American writer!" cried Laurie, throwing his hat in the air and catching it again, to the great delight of two ducks, four cats, five hens, and half a dozen children, because they were now out of the city.

"Shh! It probably won't come to anything, I suppose, but I couldn't rest until I had tried, and I said nothing about it because I didn't want anyone else to be disappointed."

"It won't fail. Really, Jo, your stories are brilliant compared to half the terrible things that get published every day. Won't it be wonderful to see them in print, and won't we be proud of our writer?"

Jo's eyes sparkled, because it is always pleasant to have someone believe in you, and a friend's encouragement is always sweeter than a dozen newspaper reviews.

"Now tell me your secret. Play fair, Teddy, or I'll never trust you again," she said, trying to hold back the bright hopes that had been lit by his encouraging words.

"I might get into trouble for telling, but I didn't promise not to, so I will, because I always feel uncomfortable until I've told you any really interesting piece of news I get. I know where Meg's glove is."

"Is that all?" said Jo, looking disappointed, as Laurie nodded and looked mysterious and full of interesting information.

"It's quite enough for now, as you'll agree when I tell you where it is."

"Tell me, then."

Laurie leaned over and whispered three words in Jo's ear, which produced a funny change in her expression. She stood and stared at him for a minute, looking both surprised and unhappy, then walked on, asking sharply, "How do you know?"

"Saw it."

"Where?"

"In his pocket."

"All this time?"

"Yes, isn't that romantic?"

"No, it's awful."

"Don't you like it?"

"Of course I don't. It's ridiculous and it won't be allowed. Goodness! What would Meg say?"

"You're not to tell anyone. Remember that."

"I didn't promise."

"That was understood, and I trusted you."

"Well, I won't for now, anyway, but I don't like it and I wish you hadn't told me."

"I thought you'd be pleased."

"At the idea of someone coming to take Meg away? No, thank you."

"You'll feel differently about it when someone comes to take you away."

"I'd like to see anyone try it," cried Jo fiercely.

"So would I!" and Laurie laughed at the thought.

"I don't think secrets agree with me. I feel unsettled since you told me that," said Jo, not very gratefully.

"Race down this hill with me and you'll feel better," suggested Laurie.

No one was in sight, the smooth road sloped invitingly before her, and finding the temptation impossible to resist, Jo ran off, soon leaving her hat and comb behind her and scattering hairpins as she ran. Laurie reached the bottom first and was very satisfied with his method, because his friend came running up with her hair flying, bright eyes, pink cheeks, and no sign of unhappiness on her face.

"I wish I were a horse, then I could run for miles in this wonderful air without losing my breath. That was great, but look what a mess it's made of me. Go pick up my things, please, like the good friend you are," said Jo, sitting down under a maple tree that was covering the ground with red leaves.

Laurie went off at a leisurely pace to retrieve the lost items, and Jo fixed up her braids, hoping no one would pass by until she was tidy again. But someone did pass by, and it was Meg, looking particularly neat and grown-up in her best outfit, as she had been paying visits.

"What in the world are you doing here?" she asked, looking at her untidy sister with well-mannered surprise.

"Collecting leaves," Jo answered simply, sorting the handful of red ones she had just picked up.

"And hairpins," added Laurie, dropping half a dozen into Jo's lap. "They grow along this road, Meg, and so do combs and brown straw hats."

"You've been running, Jo. How could you? When will you stop behaving in this wild way?" said Meg disapprovingly, straightening her cuffs and smoothing her hair, which the wind had disturbed.

"Never, until I'm old and stiff and have to use a walking stick. Don't try to make me grow up before it's time, Meg. It's hard enough to see you changing so quickly. Let me stay young as long as I can."

As she spoke, Jo bent over the leaves to hide the fact that her lips were trembling, because lately she had felt that Meg was quickly becoming a young woman, and Laurie's secret made her dread the separation that would surely come sometime and now seemed very close. He noticed her expression and quickly drew Meg's attention away from it by asking, "Where have you been visiting, all dressed up?"

"At the Gardiners', and Sallie was telling me all about Belle Moffat's wedding. It was very grand, and they've gone to spend the winter in Paris. Just imagine how wonderful that must be!"

"Do you envy her, Meg?" asked Laurie.

"I'm afraid I do."

"I'm glad of that!" muttered Jo, tying on her hat firmly.

"Why?" asked Meg, looking surprised.

"Because if you care too much about wealth, you'll never go and marry a poor man," said Jo, frowning at Laurie, who was silently warning her to be careful what she said.

"I shall never go and marry anyone," said Meg, walking on with great dignity while the others followed, laughing, whispering, skipping stones, and behaving like children, as Meg said to herself, though she might have been tempted to join them if she hadn't been wearing her best dress.

For a week or two, Jo behaved so strangely that her sisters were quite confused. She rushed to the door when the postman came, was cold to Mr. Brooke whenever they met, sat staring at Meg with a worried expression, and occasionally jumped up to shake and then hug her in a very mysterious way. She and Laurie were always making signals to each other and talking about 'Spread Eagles' until the girls declared that they had both gone mad. On the second Saturday after Jo had climbed out of the window, Meg was sitting sewing at her window and was shocked to see Laurie chasing Jo all around the garden and finally catching her in Amy's corner. What happened there Meg couldn't see, but bursts of laughter were heard, followed by lower voices and a great rustling of newspapers.

"What shall we do with that girl? She never will behave like a young lady," sighed Meg, watching the chase with a disapproving expression.

"I hope she won't. She is so funny and dear just as she is," said Beth, who never let anyone know she was a little hurt that Jo had secrets with someone other than her.

"It's very frustrating, but we can never make her behave properly," added Amy, who was sitting making new decorations for herself, with her hair tied up in a very pretty way—two pleasant things that made her feel especially graceful and ladylike.

After a few minutes Jo came bouncing in, threw herself on the sofa, and pretended to read.

"Have you anything interesting there?" asked Meg, with a superior manner.

"Nothing but a story—it probably won't amount to much, I think," replied Jo, carefully keeping the name of the newspaper hidden.

"You'd better read it aloud. That will entertain us and keep you out of trouble," said Amy in her most grown-up tone.

"What's it called?" asked Beth, wondering why Jo was keeping her face behind the paper.

"The Rival Painters."

"That sounds interesting. Read it," said Meg.

With a loud clearing of her throat and a deep breath, Jo began to read very quickly. The girls listened with interest, because the story was romantic and rather sad, as most of the characters died at the end. "I like the part about the wonderful painting," was Amy's approving comment when Jo paused.

"I prefer the love story. Viola and Angelo are two of our favorite names—isn't that a coincidence?" said Meg, wiping her eyes, because the love story part was tragic.

"Who wrote it?" asked Beth, who had caught a glimpse of Jo's face.

The reader suddenly sat up, threw down the paper to reveal a flushed face, and with a funny mixture of seriousness and excitement replied in a loud voice, "Your sister."

"You?" cried Meg, dropping her sewing.

"It's very good," said Amy thoughtfully.

"I knew it! I knew it! Oh, my Jo, I'm so proud!" and Beth ran to hug her sister and celebrate this wonderful success.

How happy they all were, that's certain! Meg couldn't believe it until she saw the words 'Miss Josephine March' actually printed in the newspaper. How carefully Amy analyzed the artistic parts of the story and offered ideas for a continuation, which unfortunately couldn't be written since the main characters were dead. How excited Beth became, skipping and singing with joy. How Hannah exclaimed, "Well, I never!" in great amazement at "what that Jo has done." How proud Mrs. March was when she found out. How Jo laughed with tears in her eyes, saying she might as well accept being famous and be done with it, and how the newspaper might be said to wave its flag triumphantly over the March household as it passed from hand to hand.

"Tell us everything." "When did it come?" "How much did you get for it?" "What will Father say?" "Won't Laurie laugh?" cried the family, all at the same time as they gathered around Jo, because these warm, loving people celebrated every small household joy with great enthusiasm.

"Stop asking questions all at once, girls, and I'll tell you everything," said Jo, wondering if any other first-time writer had ever felt prouder than she did over her 'Rival Painters'. Having explained how she had handled her stories, Jo added, "And when I went to get my answer, the man said he liked both of them but didn't pay new writers—he only let them publish in his paper and gave their stories attention. It was good practice, he said, and when new writers improved, someone would pay them. So I let him have both stories, and today this arrived, and Laurie caught me reading it and insisted on seeing it, so I let him. And he said it was good, and I'm going to write more, and he's going to help me get paid for the next one, and I'm so happy—because in time I might be able to support myself and help my sisters."

Jo ran out of breath here, and wrapping her head in the newspaper, she cried a few genuine tears over her little story, because being independent and earning the love and approval of those she cared about were the most important wishes of her heart, and this seemed to be the first step toward that happy goal.
