

To shorten the journey, the guide went to the left of the railway line that was still being built. This line, because of the winding turns of the Vindhia Mountains, did not go in a straight direction. The Parsee, who knew the roads and paths in the area very well, said that they would save twenty miles by going straight through the forest.

Phileas Fogg and Sir Francis Cromarty, sitting deep inside the uncomfortable seats, were badly shaken by the elephant's fast movement, which the skilled Parsee kept pushing forward. But they bore the discomfort with true British calm, speaking little, and barely able to see each other. As for Passepartout, who was sitting on the animal's back and felt the full force of every step, he was very careful, following his master's advice, to keep his tongue between his teeth, or it would have been bitten off. The good fellow bounced from the elephant's neck to its back, jumping like a circus performer on a spring board. Yet he laughed in the middle of all his bouncing, and from time to time took a piece of sugar from his pocket and placed it in Kiouni's trunk, which the elephant accepted without slowing down his steady movement at all.

After two hours the guide stopped the elephant and gave him an hour to rest. Kiouni, after drinking from a nearby spring, began eating branches and bushes around him. Neither Sir Francis nor Mr. Fogg minded the break, and both got down feeling relieved. "Why, he's made of iron!" exclaimed the general, looking at Kiouni with admiration.

"Of forged iron," replied Passepartout, as he began to prepare a quick breakfast.

At midday the Parsee gave the signal to leave again. The land soon looked very wild. Areas with date trees and small palms followed the thick forests, then vast, dry plains with thin bushes and large blocks of dark rock. This part of Bundelcund, which few travellers visit, is home to a fanatical population that follows the most extreme practices of the Hindu faith. The English had not been able to gain full control over this area, which is under the influence of local rulers whom it is almost impossible to reach in their hidden mountain strongholds. The travellers several times saw groups of dangerous-looking Indians who, when they noticed the elephant crossing the land, made angry and threatening movements. The Parsee avoided them as much as possible. Few animals were seen along the way. Even the monkeys hurried out of their path with movements and faces that made Passepartout laugh.

But in the middle of his cheerfulness, one thought troubled the good servant. What would Mr. Fogg do with the elephant when he reached Allahabad? Would he take it with him? Impossible! The cost of transporting it would be ruinously expensive. Would he sell it, or set it free? The valuable animal certainly deserved some consideration. If Mr. Fogg chose to give Kiouni to him, Passepartout, that would be very difficult to manage. And these thoughts kept troubling him for a long time.

The main chain of the Vindhias was crossed by eight in the evening, and another stop was made on the northern side in a ruined bungalow. They had covered nearly twenty-five miles that day, and the same distance still lay between them and Allahabad station.

The night was cold. The Parsee lit a fire in the bungalow with a few dry branches, and the warmth was very welcome. Food bought at Kholby was enough for supper, and the travellers ate hungrily. The conversation, which began with a few separate phrases, soon gave way to loud steady snoring. The guide watched Kiouni, who slept standing up, leaning against the trunk of a large tree. Nothing happened during the night to disturb the sleepers, although occasional growls from leopards and chatter from monkeys broke the silence. The more dangerous animals made no sounds or attacks against the people in the bungalow. Sir Francis slept deeply, like an honest soldier worn out by tiredness. Passepartout had restless dreams about the bouncing of the day before. As for Mr. Fogg, he slept as peacefully as if he had been in his calm house in Saville Row.

The journey continued at six in the morning. The guide hoped to reach Allahabad by evening. In that case, Mr. Fogg would only lose part of the forty-eight hours he had saved since the start of the journey. Kiouni, moving at his fast pace again, soon came down the lower slopes of the Vindhias, and towards noon they passed through the village of Kallenger on the Cani, one of the branches of the Ganges. The guide avoided villages, thinking it safer to keep to the open land along the first lower areas of the great river's basin. Allahabad was now only twelve miles to the north-east. They stopped under a group of banana trees, whose fruit, as filling as bread and as rich as cream, was enjoyed fully and with great appreciation.

At two o'clock the guide entered a thick forest that stretched for several miles. He preferred to travel under the cover of the trees. So far they had not had any unpleasant encounters, and the journey seemed close to being completed successfully, when the elephant became restless and suddenly stopped.

It was four o'clock.

"What's the matter?" asked Sir Francis, putting his head out.

"I don't know, officer," replied the Parsee, listening carefully to a confused sound coming through the thick branches.

The sound soon became clearer. It now sounded like a distant mix of human voices and brass instruments. Passepartout had all his eyes and ears open. Mr. Fogg waited patiently without saying a word. The Parsee jumped to the ground, tied the elephant to a tree, and moved into the thick bushes. He returned soon, saying:

"A procession of Brahmins is coming this way. We must stop them from seeing us, if possible."

The guide untied the elephant and led him into the bushes, at the same time asking the travellers not to move. He was ready to climb back onto the animal at any moment if they needed to run. But he clearly believed that the procession of worshippers would pass without noticing them among the thick leaves where they were completely hidden.

The unpleasant sounds of voices and instruments came closer, and now slow, low singing mixed with the sound of tambourines and cymbals. The front of the procession soon appeared between the trees, about a hundred steps away. The strange figures taking part in the religious ceremony could easily be seen through the branches. First came the priests, wearing pointed hats and long decorated robes. They were surrounded by men, women, and children singing a kind of sad song, interrupted at regular intervals by tambourines and cymbals. Behind them was pulled a cart with large wheels, the spokes of which were shaped like intertwined snakes. On the cart, pulled by four richly decorated zebus, stood a frightening statue with four arms. The body was painted a dull red, with wild staring eyes, untidy hair, a tongue sticking out, and lips coloured with betel. It stood upright on the figure of a lying, headless giant.

Sir Francis, recognising the statue, whispered, "The goddess Kali. The goddess of love and death."

"Of death, perhaps," Passepartout quietly replied, "but of love — that ugly old figure? Never!"

The Parsee made a sign to keep silent.

A group of old holy men were jumping about and making a wild noise around the statue. These men were marked with orange paint and covered with cuts from which blood was falling drop by drop — foolish fanatics who, during large Indian ceremonies, still throw themselves under the wheels of the god Juggernaut's cart. Some Brahmins, dressed in rich and colourful Eastern clothing, were leading a woman who walked unsteadily. This woman was young, and as fair-skinned as a European. Her head and neck, shoulders, ears, arms, hands, and toes were covered with jewellery and gems — bracelets, earrings, and rings. A gold-edged tunic, covered by a light thin robe, showed the shape of her body.

The guards who followed the young woman looked very different from her, armed as they were with uncovered swords at their sides and long decorated pistols. They were carrying a dead body on a small covered platform. It was the body of an old man, richly dressed in the clothing of a rajah. He wore, as in life, a turban decorated with pearls, a robe of silk and gold cloth, a scarf of fine wool decorated with diamonds, and the magnificent weapons of an Indian prince. Next came musicians and a group of jumping holy men at the back, whose shouts sometimes drowned out the sounds of the instruments. These closed the procession.

Sir Francis watched the procession with a sad face, and turning to the guide, said, "A suttee."

The Parsee nodded and put his finger to his lips. The procession moved slowly under the trees, and soon its last members disappeared into the depths of the forest. The singing gradually faded. Occasionally voices were heard in the distance, until at last there was complete silence.

Phileas Fogg had heard what Sir Francis said, and as soon as the procession had disappeared, he asked: "What is a suttee?"

"A suttee," said the general, "is a human sacrifice, but a willing one. The woman you have just seen will be burned tomorrow at dawn."

"Oh, how terrible!" cried Passepartout, who could not hide his anger.

"And the dead body?" asked Mr. Fogg.

"Is that of the prince, her husband," said the guide. "An independent rajah of Bundelcund."

"Is it possible," said Phileas Fogg, his voice showing no emotion, "that these cruel customs still exist in India, and that the English have been unable to stop them?"

"These sacrifices do not happen in most parts of India," replied Sir Francis. "But we have no power over these wild territories, and especially here in Bundelcund. The whole area north of the Vindhias is a place of constant killings and robbery."

"The poor woman!" exclaimed Passepartout. "To be burned alive!"

"Yes," returned Sir Francis, "burned alive. And if she were not burned, you cannot imagine what treatment she would have to suffer from her husband's relatives. They would cut off her hair, feed her barely enough rice to survive, treat her with contempt. She would be seen as an unclean person and would die forgotten in some corner, like a sick dog. The thought of such a terrible life pushes these poor women to the sacrifice much more than love or religious belief. Sometimes, however, the sacrifice really is willing, and then the government must step in to prevent it. Several years ago, when I was living in Bombay, a young widow asked the governor for permission to be burned with her husband's body. But as you can imagine, he refused. The woman left the city, found shelter with an independent rajah, and there carried out her wish."

While Sir Francis was speaking, the guide shook his head several times, and now said: "The sacrifice that will happen tomorrow at dawn is not a willing one."

"How do you know?"

"Everyone in Bundelcund knows about this matter."

"But the poor woman did not seem to be trying to resist," said Sir Francis.

"That was because they had made her senseless with the fumes of hemp and opium."

"But where are they taking her?"

"To the temple of Pillaji, two miles from here. She will spend the night there."

"And the sacrifice will happen—"

"Tomorrow, at the first light of dawn."

The guide now led the elephant out of the bushes and climbed onto his neck. Just as he was about to move Kiouni forward with a particular whistle, Mr. Fogg stopped him and, turning to Sir Francis Cromarty, said, "Suppose we save this woman."

"Save the woman, Mr. Fogg!"

"I still have twelve hours to spare. I can give them to this."

"Why, you are a man with a heart!"

"Sometimes," replied Phileas Fogg, quietly. "When I have the time."
