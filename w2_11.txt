

  It started with strong wind in the afternoon. Maya noticed the sky turning dark while she was helping Lina bring her flower cart inside. The clouds were thick and fast-moving. The ocean looked angry.

“Storm’s coming,” Lina said, pushing a vase of roses toward safety. “And I forgot to close my window!”

“Go,” Maya said. “I’ll finish this.”

Lina ran off, and Maya pulled the cart under a shop awning.

Just then, Elias came around the corner, his coat flapping in the wind. “I was at the pier,” he said. “The waves are wild.”

“Why were you at the pier?” Maya asked, frowning.

“I needed to think. The storm’s beautiful. Dangerous, but beautiful.”

“That’s such an Elias thing to say,” she said, laughing nervously. “Come help me with this.”

Together, they pulled the cart inside the bookshop. The first drops of rain hit the windows like tiny stones. Then the thunder came.

“Lina okay?” he asked.

“She ran home.”

The lights flickered, then went out.

“Oh no,” Maya whispered. “Power’s gone.”

Elias pulled out his phone. “Flashlight on. Want me to check the circuit box?”

“It’s outside behind the shop,” she said. “Be careful.”

He nodded and left into the rain.

Maya stood near the front window, watching the storm. The trees danced wildly in the wind, and puddles formed in the street. She felt a mix of fear and excitement. Nature reminded her how small people really were.

Ten minutes passed.

Then twenty.

Elias hadn’t come back.

Maya grabbed a raincoat, pulled the hood over her head, and rushed outside. The wind pushed against her. Water splashed up to her knees. She reached the back of the shop.

“Elias?” she called.

No answer.

Then she saw him—soaked, kneeling beside the power box with a flashlight in his mouth, struggling with the wires.

“Elias!” she shouted. “What are you doing?!”

“I almost got it!” he yelled back, coughing.

“Forget it! Come inside!”

A spark flew from the box. Elias jumped back.

Maya grabbed his hand and pulled him toward the back door. They both stumbled inside, dripping wet and breathing hard.

“You could’ve been electrocuted!” she said.

“I was careful,” he replied, half-laughing.

“You’re insane,” she said, hitting his arm lightly. “But… thank you.”

The power stayed out, but Maya lit candles. She handed Elias a dry towel and some of her uncle’s old clothes.

Later, they sat on the floor, wrapped in blankets. Rain pounded the windows, thunder rolled in the sky—but in the bookstore, everything was warm.

Maya looked at him, his wet hair falling over his eyes, his hands still shaking a little.

“You came back for the storm,” she said. “But I think I’m the one shaken now.”

Elias smiled, tired but alive. “I didn’t want to be alone in it.”
