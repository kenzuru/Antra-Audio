“Curiouser and curiouser!” cried Alice. She was so surprised by what was happening that for a moment, she completely forgot how to speak proper English. “Now I am opening out, growing tall like the largest telescope that was ever made! Good-bye, my feet!” When she looked down, her feet seemed to be miles away, almost out of sight, because she was growing so rapidly. “Oh, my poor little feet, I wonder who will manage to put your shoes and stockings on you now, my dears? I am certain that *I* won’t be able to! I will be far too high up and too far away to worry about you. You will just have to manage the best way you can.” Then she had a sudden thought: “But I must be kind to them,” she reasoned, “or else perhaps they won’t walk in the direction I want to go! Let me see: I know what I’ll do. I will give them a brand new pair of boots every single Christmas.”

She continued to make detailed plans for how she would manage this difficult situation. “The boots will have to be sent by a delivery man,” she thought. “And how strange it will seem, sending gifts to my own feet! And how completely odd the address label will look!”

She started reciting the address in her head:
> Alice’s Right Foot, Esquire,
> Hearthrug,
> near the Fender,
> (with Alice’s love).

“Oh dear me, what silly nonsense I am talking!” she exclaimed, realizing how ridiculous the situation was.

Just at that very moment, her head hit the roof of the hall with a soft *thud*. She had grown so tall that she was now more than nine feet high! She quickly reached down—which was now a long way—picked up the little golden key, and hurried to the garden door, though she was so big now that she had to stoop awkwardly.

Poor Alice! Even when lying down sideways on the floor, all she could manage was to peer into the beautiful garden with just one eye. Getting through the tiny door was now more impossible than ever before. Completely heartbroken, she sat down on the floor and began to cry again, shedding massive tears.

“You should be thoroughly ashamed of yourself,” Alice scolded her giant self sharply. “A huge girl like you,” (she was definitely right to say this, as she was nine feet tall) “should not be crying in this manner! Stop this moment, I command you!” But she kept crying anyway, pouring out large amounts of tears, until a big pool of water formed all around her. The pool was about four inches deep and stretched halfway down the hall.

After some time had passed, she heard a sound of tiny, quick footsteps pattering in the distance. She quickly dried her eyes to see what was coming. It was the White Rabbit returning. He was dressed even more smartly than before, and he held a pair of white kid gloves in one hand and a large folding fan in the other. He came trotting along in a tremendous hurry, mumbling worriedly to himself as he approached. “Oh! The Duchess, the Duchess! Oh! Won’t she be absolutely furious if I have kept her waiting!”

Alice felt so utterly desperate and confused that she was ready to ask anyone for help, even a hurried, talking rabbit. So, when the Rabbit came close to her, she began to speak in a very low, timid voice, “If you please, sir—”

The Rabbit jumped violently, completely startled by the gigantic voice coming from above him. He dropped the white kid gloves and the fan and quickly scrambled away into the darkness of the hall, running as fast as his little legs could carry him.

Alice picked up the fan and the gloves. Because the large hall felt very warm, she began fanning herself constantly as she continued her strange conversation with herself. “Dear me! How strange and unusual everything is today! Just yesterday, things were perfectly normal. I wonder if I have been secretly changed during the night? Let me think hard: was I the same person when I woke up this morning? I almost feel like I can remember feeling slightly different. But if I am not the same Alice, then the next and most important question is, who in the world *am* I? Ah, **that** is the greatest puzzle of all!” She started thinking carefully about all the other children she knew who were about her age, trying to figure out if she had somehow been swapped for one of them.

“I’m sure I am not Ada,” she decided, “because Ada’s hair hangs down in very long curls, and my hair doesn’t curl at all. And I’m certain I cannot be Mabel, because I know all kinds of difficult things, and poor Mabel, oh! she knows such a very small amount! Besides,” Alice insisted, “*she* is Mabel, and *I* am Alice, and—oh dear, this is all so confusing!”

She decided to test her knowledge. “I will try to see if I still remember all the things I was taught. Let me see: four times five is twelve, and four times six is thirteen, and four times seven is—oh dear! I shall never even get to twenty at this rate!” she cried in distress. “Well, the Multiplication Table doesn’t matter right now. Let's try Geography. London is the capital of Paris, and Paris is the capital of Rome, and Rome—no, **that** is completely wrong, I know for certain! I *must* have been changed for Mabel! I will try reciting my poem.”

She folded her hands neatly in her lap as if she were giving a lesson in the schoolroom and began to repeat a well-known poem, but her voice sounded rough and strange, and the words that came out were not the ones she usually said:

“How doth the little crocodile
Improve his shining tail,
And pour the waters of the Nile
On every golden scale!

“How cheerfully he seems to grin,
How neatly spread his claws,
And welcome little fishes in
With gently smiling jaws!”

“I am positive those are not the right words,” poor Alice admitted, and her eyes filled with tears once more as she continued to reason, “I really must be Mabel after all, and I shall have to go and live in that cramped, plain little house, and have almost no toys to play with, and oh! so many boring lessons to learn! No, I have made up my mind completely; if I am Mabel, I will stay down here! It will be no good for them to put their heads down the rabbit-hole and say, ‘Come up again, dear!’ I will just look up and say, ‘Who am I then? Tell me that first, and then, if I like being that person, I will come up. If not, I will stay down here until I become someone else!’”

But, oh dear! cried Alice, suddenly bursting into a fresh flood of tears, “I do wish they *would* put their heads down and call me! I am so terribly tired of being completely alone here!”

As she said this, she happened to glance down at her hands. She was shocked to see that while she was talking, she had accidentally put on one of the Rabbit’s little white kid gloves. “How **could** I have done that?” she thought. “I must be shrinking smaller again.” She quickly stood up and went over to the table to measure her height against it. She realized, as best as she could guess, that she was now only about two feet tall, and she was shrinking rapidly! She quickly figured out that the cause of this new shrinking was the fan she was holding. She dropped it instantly, just in time to prevent herself from completely shrinking away to nothing.

“That **was** a very narrow escape!” said Alice, quite frightened by the sudden change, but very relieved to find herself still in existence. “And now, for the garden!” She ran as fast as she could back to the little door. But, alas! The little door was closed again, and the tiny golden key was lying on the glass table, just as before. “And things are worse than they’ve ever been,” thought the poor child sadly, “because I have never been this tiny before, never! And I declare, it is simply too bad!”

As she finished these words, her foot slipped, and in the very next moment, *splash!* She was swimming in cold water that came up to her chin. Her first thought was that she must have somehow fallen into the sea. “And in that case, I can simply go back home by railway,” she told herself. (Alice had only visited the seaside once, and she concluded that everywhere on the English coast, you would always find a row of colorful bathing machines in the water, children digging in the sand with wooden spades, a street of rental houses, and behind them, a railway station.) However, she quickly realized that she was not in the sea, but in the giant pool of tears that she had wept when she was nine feet tall.

“I wish I hadn’t cried so much!” Alice complained, swimming around and trying to find a way out of the watery hall. “I suppose I will be punished for it now by drowning in my own tears! That **will** be a peculiar thing, without a doubt! But then again, everything is peculiar today.”

Just then, she heard something else splashing in the pool not far away. She swam closer to see what it could be. At first, she thought it was some huge animal, like a walrus or a hippopotamus, but then she remembered how small she currently was. She soon realized it was only a Mouse that had slipped and fallen in, just as she had.

“Would it be useful, I wonder,” Alice thought, considering the situation, “to speak to this Mouse? Everything down here is so strange and out-of-the-way that I think it is very likely it can talk. At least, there is no harm in trying to speak to it.”

So she began: “O Mouse, do you know the way to get out of this pool? I am extremely tired of swimming here, O Mouse!” (Alice thought this was the proper and most polite way to address a mouse. She had never spoken to one before, but she remembered seeing in her brother’s Latin Grammar book a list of words that included, “A mouse—of a mouse—to a mouse—a mouse—O mouse!”)

The Mouse looked at her with a questioning expression and seemed to wink one of its little eyes at her, but it did not say anything back.

“Perhaps it doesn’t understand English,” thought Alice. “I suppose it is a French Mouse that came over with William the Conqueror.” (For even with all her school knowledge of history, Alice never had a very clear idea of how long ago historical events had actually happened.) So she tried again, speaking the first French sentence she knew: “*Où est ma chatte*?” which means, “Where is my cat?”

The Mouse gave a violent leap straight out of the water and seemed to shake all over with fear. “Oh, I beg your pardon!” Alice cried out quickly, afraid she had accidentally offended the poor animal. “I completely forgot that you don’t like cats.”

“Not like cats!” cried the Mouse, in a high, sharp, and angry voice. “Would **you** like cats if you were me?”

“Well, perhaps not,” said Alice in a calm, gentle tone. “Please don’t be angry about it. And yet, I do wish I could show you our cat, Dinah. I really think you would start to like cats if you could just meet her. She is such a sweet, quiet thing,” Alice continued, talking partly to herself as she swam lazily through the pool. “And she sits by the fire purring so sweetly, licking her paws and washing her face—and she is so soft and lovely to hold—and she is such an excellent hunter for catching mice—oh, I beg your pardon again!” Alice cried out, genuinely distressed, for this time the Mouse’s fur was standing up all over its body, and she was certain it was truly offended. “We will not talk about her anymore if you would prefer that.”

“We indeed!” cried the Mouse, who was trembling all the way to the end of his tail. “As if **I** would willingly talk about such a horrid subject! Our entire family has always **hated** cats: they are nasty, common, vulgar creatures! Never let me hear that name spoken again!”

“I promise I won’t!” said Alice, rushing to change the subject of the conversation. “Are you—are you fond—of—of dogs?”

The Mouse did not answer, so Alice quickly continued, speaking eagerly: “There is such a lovely little dog that lives near our house that I would love to show you! It’s a small, bright-eyed terrier, you know, with oh, such long, curly brown hair! And it will happily fetch things when you throw them, and it will sit up and beg politely for its dinner, and do all sorts of clever things—I can’t even remember half of them—and it belongs to a farmer, and he says it is so useful that it is worth a hundred pounds! He says it kills all the rats and—oh dear!” cried Alice in a sad voice, “I am afraid I have offended it again!”

The Mouse was swimming away from her as fast as it possibly could, creating quite a disturbance in the water as it went.

So, Alice called softly after it, “Dear Mouse! Please do come back, and we won’t talk about cats or dogs either, if you don’t like them!” When the Mouse heard this promise, it slowly turned around and swam back toward her. Its face was very pale (with anger, Alice thought), and it said in a low, shaky voice, “Let us get to the shore immediately, and then I will tell you my life story, and you will fully understand why it is that I hate both cats and dogs.”

It was definitely time to leave the water, because the pool was becoming quite crowded with all sorts of birds and animals that had also fallen in. There were a Duck and a Dodo, a Lory and an Eaglet, and several other unusual creatures. Alice led the way, and the entire mixed group swam together toward the edge of the pool and pulled themselves onto the dry shore.
