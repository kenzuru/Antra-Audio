

The thing to do is to enjoy the sunshine while it lasts, and when it rains, to look forward to the sun coming back, knowing that it surely will. A terrible storm was keeping the little people of the Green Forest, the Green Meadows, and the Old Orchard trapped in their homes or in whatever shelter they had been able to find.

But the storm couldn't last forever, and they knew it. Knowing this was the only thing keeping some of them alive.

You see, they were starving. Yes, they were starving. You and I would be very hungry — very hungry indeed — if we had to go without food for two whole days, but if we were warm and comfortable, it wouldn't really hurt us. With the small wild animals, especially the little birds, it is very different. They are naturally so active that they need to eat very often in order to keep their little bodies warm and full of energy. So when their food is completely cut off, they starve or freeze very quickly. A great many small lives end this way in every long, hard winter storm.

It was late in the afternoon of the second day when rough Brother North Wind decided that he had shown his strength and fierceness long enough, and rumbling and grumbling he left the Green Meadows and the Green Forest, blowing the snow clouds away with him. For just a little while before going to bed behind the Purple Hills, jolly, round, red Mr. Sun smiled down on the white land, and never was his smile more welcome. Out from their shelters rushed all the little prisoners, for they had to make the most of the short time before the cold night came.

Little Tommy Tit the Chickadee was so weak that he could hardly fly, and he shook with chills. He went straight to the apple tree where Farmer Brown's boy always keeps a piece of animal fat tied to a branch for Tommy and his friends. Drummer the Woodpecker was there before him. Now it is one of the rules of good manners among birds that when one is eating from a piece of fat, a newcomer should wait for his turn.

"Dee, dee, dee!" said Tommy Tit faintly but happily, because he couldn't help being cheerful even if he tried. "Dee, dee, dee! That looks good to me."

"It is good," mumbled Drummer, pecking away at the fat greedily. "Come on, Tommy Tit. Don't wait for me, because I won't be finished for a long time. I'm nearly starved, and I think you must be too."

"I am," admitted Tommy, as he flew over beside Drummer. "Thank you so much for not making me wait."

"Don't mention it," replied Drummer, with his mouth full. "This is no time for polite behavior. Here comes Yank Yank the Nuthatch. I think there is room for him too."

Yank Yank was quickly invited to join them and did so after apologizing for seeming so greedy.

"If I couldn't fill my stomach before night, I would certainly freeze to death before morning," he said. "What a wonderful thing it is to have all this good food waiting for us. If I had to search for my usual food on the trees, I certainly would have to give up and die. It took all my strength just to get here. My, I feel like a new bird already! Here comes Sammy Jay. I wonder if he will try to push us away as he usually does."

Sammy did nothing of the kind. He was very quiet and very polite. "Can you make room for a starving fellow to have a bite?" he asked. "I wouldn't ask, but I couldn't last another night without food."

"Dee, dee, dee! Always room for one more," replied Tommy Tit, moving over to make room for Sammy. "Wasn't that a terrible storm?"

"The worst I have ever known," mumbled Sammy. "I wonder if I will ever be warm again."

Until their stomachs were full, not another word was said. Meanwhile Chatterer the Red Squirrel had noticed that the storm was over. As he pushed through the snow toward another apple tree, he saw Tommy Tit and his friends, and in his heart he was glad that they had found food waiting for them. His own troubles were over, for in the tree he was heading to was a store of corn.
