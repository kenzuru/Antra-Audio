
  Three passengers including Passepartout had disappeared. Had they been killed in the fighting? Had they been taken prisoner by the Sioux? It was impossible to know.

There were many wounded, but none with life-threatening injuries. Colonel Proctor was among the most seriously hurt. He had fought bravely, and a bullet had hit him in the side. He was carried into the station along with the other wounded passengers, to receive whatever medical help was possible.

Aouda was safe, and Phileas Fogg, who had been in the middle of the fighting, had not received a single scratch. Fix was slightly wounded in the arm. But Passepartout was nowhere to be found, and tears ran down Aouda's cheeks.

All the passengers had got off the train, and its wheels were stained with blood. From the metal parts hung torn pieces of flesh. As far as the eye could see across the white flat land behind them, red trails were visible. The last Sioux were disappearing to the south, along the banks of Republican River.

Mr. Fogg, with his arms folded, stood still. He had a serious decision to make. Aouda, standing near him, looked at him without speaking, and he understood what her look meant. If his servant was a prisoner, should he not risk everything to free him from the Native Americans? "I will find him, alive or dead," he said quietly to Aouda.

"Ah, Mr. — Mr. Fogg!" she cried, taking his hands and covering them with tears.

"Alive," added Mr. Fogg, "if we do not lose a single moment."

Phileas Fogg, by making this decision, was sacrificing himself. He was accepting his own defeat. A delay of one single day would cause him to miss the steamer in New York, and his bet would certainly be lost. But, thinking "It is my duty," he did not hesitate.

The officer in charge of Fort Kearney was there. A hundred of his soldiers had taken up positions to defend the station, in case the Sioux attacked it.

"Sir," said Mr. Fogg to the captain, "three passengers have disappeared."

"Dead?" asked the captain.

"Dead or captured. That is the question that must be answered. Do you plan to go after the Sioux?"

"That is a serious thing to do, sir," replied the captain. "These Native Americans may retreat beyond the Arkansas River, and I cannot leave the fort unprotected."

"The lives of three men are in question, sir," said Phileas Fogg.

"That is true. But can I risk the lives of fifty men to save three?"

"I don't know whether you can, sir. But you should."

"Nobody here," replied the other, "has the right to tell me my duty."

"Very well," said Mr. Fogg coldly. "I will go alone."

"You, sir!" cried Fix, coming forward. "You go alone in pursuit of the Sioux?"

"Would you have me leave this poor fellow to die — the man to whom everyone here owes their life? I shall go."

"No, sir, you shall not go alone," cried the captain, moved despite himself. "No! You are a brave man. Thirty volunteers!" he added, turning to the soldiers.

The entire company stepped forward at once. The captain only had to choose. Thirty were selected, with an old sergeant placed in charge.

"Thank you, captain," said Mr. Fogg.

"Will you let me go with you?" asked Fix.

"Do as you please, sir. But if you wish to do me a favour, stay with Aouda. In case anything should happen to me—"

A sudden paleness spread over the detective's face. Separate himself from the man he had followed so persistently, step by step! Leave him to wander about in this wilderness! Fix looked carefully at Mr. Fogg, and, despite his suspicions and the struggle going on inside him, he lowered his eyes before that calm and open look.

"I will stay," he said.

A few moments later, Mr. Fogg pressed the young woman's hand and, having left his important travel bag with her, went off with the sergeant and his small group of soldiers. But before going he had said to the soldiers, "My friends, I will share five thousand dollars among you if we save the prisoners."

It was just after noon.

Aouda went to a waiting room, and there she waited alone, thinking about the simple and noble generosity and the calm courage of Phileas Fogg. He had sacrificed his fortune and was now risking his life — all without hesitation, from a sense of duty, and in silence.

Fix did not have the same thoughts and could hardly hide his worry. He walked restlessly up and down the platform, but soon returned to his outward calm. He now saw the foolishness of what he had done in letting Fogg go alone. What! The man he had just followed around the world had been allowed to separate himself from him! He began to criticise himself harshly, and, as if he were a police chief, gave himself a serious talking to for his stupidity.

"I have been an idiot!" he thought. "This man will see it. He has gone and won't come back! But how is it that I, Fix — who has in my pocket a warrant for his arrest — have been so fascinated by him? I am clearly nothing but a fool!"

This is how the detective reasoned while the hours passed far too slowly. He did not know what to do. Sometimes he was tempted to tell Aouda everything. But he could have no doubt about how the young woman would react to what he had to say. What should he do? He thought of following Fogg across the vast white flat lands. It did not seem impossible that he might catch up with him. Footsteps were easy to see in the snow. But soon, under a new layer of snow, every mark would be gone.

Fix became discouraged. He felt a kind of strong desire to give up the whole business entirely. He could now leave Fort Kearney station and continue his journey home in peace.

Around two o'clock in the afternoon, while heavy snow was falling, long whistle sounds were heard coming from the east. A large shape, preceded by a wild light, slowly moved forward. It appeared even larger through the mist, which gave it an unreal appearance. No train was expected from the east, and there had not been enough time for the help requested by telegraph to arrive. The train from Omaha to San Francisco was not due until the next day. The mystery was quickly explained.

The engine that was slowly approaching with its loud whistles was the one that had been disconnected from the train and had continued forward at a terrifying speed, carrying the unconscious engineer and the stoker. It had gone several miles when, because the fire was dying from lack of fuel, the steam had slowed. It had finally stopped an hour later, about twenty miles past Fort Kearney. Neither the engineer nor the stoker had died. After staying unconscious for some time, they had recovered. The train had then stopped. The engineer, finding himself in the middle of nowhere with the engine and no carriages, understood what had happened. He could not imagine how the engine had become separated from the train. But he had no doubt that the train left behind was in trouble.

He knew what to do. It would be sensible to continue to Omaha, since it would be dangerous to return to the train, which the Native Americans might still be robbing. Nevertheless, he began to rebuild the fire in the furnace. The pressure rose again, and the engine returned, going backwards to Fort Kearney. This was the engine now whistling in the mist.

The travellers were very happy to see the engine take its place at the front of the train again. They could now continue the journey that had been so terribly interrupted.

Aouda, on seeing the engine arrive, hurried out of the station and asked the conductor, "Are you going to leave?"

"At once, madam."

"But the prisoners — our unfortunate fellow travellers—"

"I cannot delay the trip," replied the conductor. "We are already three hours behind."

"And when will the next train come from San Francisco?"

"Tomorrow evening, madam."

"Tomorrow evening! But then it will be too late! We must wait—"

"That is impossible," replied the conductor. "If you wish to go, please get in."

"I will not go," said Aouda.

Fix had heard this conversation. A little while before, when there had seemed to be no chance of moving on, he had made up his mind to leave Fort Kearney. But now that the train was there, ready to leave, and he only had to take his seat in the carriage, something he could not resist held him back. The station platform seemed to burn under his feet, and he could not move. The conflict inside him began again. Anger and failure were choking him. He wanted to keep fighting until the end.

Meanwhile, the passengers and some of the wounded — including Colonel Proctor, whose injuries were serious — had taken their places in the train. The humming of the overheated boiler could be heard, and steam was escaping from the valves. The engineer blew the whistle, the train started, and it soon disappeared, mixing its white smoke with the swirling, heavily falling snow.

The detective had stayed behind.

Several hours passed. The weather was miserable and very cold. Fix sat without moving on a bench in the station. He might have been asleep. Aouda, despite the storm, kept coming out of the waiting room, going to the end of the platform, and looking through the snowstorm, as if trying to see through the mist that closed in on the horizon around her, and to hear some welcome sound if possible. She heard and saw nothing. Then she would go back inside, chilled through, only to come out again after a few moments, but always without success.

Evening came, and the small group had not returned. Where could they be? Had they found the Native Americans and were they fighting, or were they still lost in the mist? The commander of the fort was worried, though he tried to hide his fears. As night approached, the snow fell less heavily, but the cold became very intense. Absolute silence rested over the flat lands. There was no sound of bird or animal to break the perfect stillness.

Throughout the night Aouda, full of sad fears and with her heart heavy with worry, walked about near the edge of the flat lands. Her imagination took her far away and showed her many dangers. What she went through during those long hours cannot be described.

Fix did not move from the same spot, but he did not sleep. Once a man came up and spoke to him, and the detective simply shook his head in reply.

So the night passed. At dawn, the half-extinguished shape of the sun rose above a misty horizon. But it was now possible to make out objects two miles away. Phileas Fogg and the group had gone southward. In the south all was still empty. It was now seven o'clock.

The captain, who was genuinely alarmed, did not know what to do.

Should he send another group to search for the first? Should he sacrifice more men with so little chance of saving those already sent? He did not hesitate for long, however. He called one of his officers and was about to order a search when gunshots were heard. Was it a signal? The soldiers rushed out of the fort, and half a mile away they saw a small group returning in good order.

Mr. Fogg was walking at their head, and just behind him were Passepartout and the other two travellers, rescued from the Sioux.

They had met and fought the warriors ten miles south of Fort Kearney. Just before the group of soldiers arrived, Passepartout and his companions had begun to fight back against their captors. Passepartout had knocked down three of them with his fists when his master and the soldiers came to their rescue.

All were welcomed with happy cries. Phileas Fogg gave out the reward he had promised to the soldiers, while Passepartout, with good reason, said quietly to himself, "It must certainly be admitted that I cost my master a great deal!"

Fix, without saying a word, looked at Mr. Fogg, and it would have been very difficult to understand the thoughts that were struggling within him. As for Aouda, she took her protector's hand and pressed it in her own, too moved to speak.

Meanwhile, Passepartout was looking around for the train. He thought he would find it there, ready to leave for Omaha, and he hoped that the time lost could still be made up.

"The train! The train!" he cried.

"Gone," replied Fix.

"And when does the next train come through here?" said Phileas Fogg.

"Not until this evening."

"Ah!" replied the calm gentleman quietly.
