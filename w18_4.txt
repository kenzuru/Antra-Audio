Mowgli’s life in the jungle settled into a routine. He trained with the wolf cubs, learned lessons from Baloo, and practiced moving quietly with Bagheera. He still wasn’t as fast as the wolves or as silent as the panther, but he improved every day.

The only real problems came from the monkeys.

The monkeys—called the Bandar-log—did whatever they wanted. They had no leader, no rules, and no respect for any animal in the jungle. They jumped from tree to tree, shouted nonsense, and bragged about things they never actually did. Most animals ignored them.

But the monkeys didn’t ignore Mowgli.

They thought he was interesting because he looked different from the wolves. Whenever Mowgli climbed trees during training, the monkeys copied him. When he practiced jumping from rock to rock, they cheered loudly, making a big show of it.

Baloo warned him early.
“Stay away from them,” he said. “They cause trouble. They don’t follow the law.”

Mowgli tried to listen, but the monkeys kept appearing. They dropped fruit on his head, tugged on his hair, and shouted for him to join them. At first, it was just annoying. Then it became distracting.

One day, while Mowgli was practicing climbing with Baloo, the monkeys started calling down from the branches.

“Come with us, man-cub!”
“We know secret places!”
“We can show you things the wolves can’t!”

Mowgli ignored them, but Baloo wasn’t pleased. “You are giving them attention by reacting at all,” he said. “They want you to notice them. Don’t.”

Bagheera agreed. “They will try to pull you away from your lessons. They don’t understand responsibility.”

Mowgli nodded, but staying away wasn’t as easy as it sounded.

A few days later, a group of young monkeys surrounded him while he was gathering fruit for the pack. They chattered nonstop.

“You should live with us!” one monkey said.
“We’ll make you our leader!” another shouted.
“You look like us more than wolves!”

Mowgli laughed a little, thinking they were joking, but they weren’t.

“Don’t talk to them,” came Bagheera’s voice from above. The panther climbed down the tree, and the monkeys scattered instantly. Bagheera frowned. “They are getting too close.”

That night, Baloo and Bagheera spoke privately with Mother Wolf and Father Wolf. Mowgli didn’t hear everything, but he caught pieces of the conversation—“monkeys,” “danger,” “too curious,” “watch him closely.”

Mowgli didn’t want to cause trouble, so the next day he stayed away from the trees where the monkeys usually gathered. But the monkeys had their own plans.

They watched him.
They waited.
And they chose a moment when he was alone.

Mowgli was sitting near a stream when a monkey suddenly grabbed his arm. Before he could react, more monkeys dropped down from the trees, fast and noisy. They didn’t give him time to shout. They lifted him up, passed him from one to another, and carried him into the branches.

“Stop! Put me down!” Mowgli yelled, but the monkeys only laughed.

They swung higher and higher until the ground was far below. Mowgli tried to grab a branch, but the monkeys moved too quickly. They carried him deeper into the jungle, past trees he had never seen before.

Back near the stream, Baloo arrived too late. He looked up at the shaking branches and shouted Mowgli’s name, but the monkeys were already gone—far away and getting farther with every leap.

Bagheera arrived moments later. “They’ve taken him,” he said sharply.

Baloo blamed himself. “I should’ve kept him closer.”

Bagheera shook his head. “Blaming yourself won’t bring him back. We need help.”

There was only one animal in the jungle the monkeys feared.
Only one who could reach them in the highest branches.
Only one who might be willing to help—

Kaa, the great python.

And finding Kaa would be their next step.
