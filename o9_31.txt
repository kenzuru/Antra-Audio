
   Phileas Fogg was twenty hours late. Passepartout, who had accidentally caused this delay, was desperate. He felt he had ruined his master!

At that moment the detective came up to Mr. Fogg, looked carefully at his face, and said:

"Seriously, sir, are you in a big hurry?"

"Very seriously."

"I am asking for a reason," Fix continued. "Is it completely necessary that you arrive in New York on the 11th, before nine o'clock in the evening, when the ship leaves for Liverpool?"

"It is completely necessary."

"And if the Indians had not stopped your journey, you would have reached New York on the morning of the 11th?"

"Yes, with eleven hours to spare before the ship left."

"Good. So you are twenty hours late. Twenty minus twelve leaves eight. You need to get back eight hours. Do you want to try?"

"On foot?" asked Mr. Fogg.

"No, on a sledge," replied Fix. "A sledge with sails. A man suggested this idea to me."

It was the man who had spoken to Fix during the night, and whose offer Fix had refused.

Phileas Fogg did not answer right away. But Fix pointed to the man, who was walking up and down in front of the station, and Mr. Fogg went over to him. A moment later, Mr. Fogg and the American, whose name was Mudge, went into a small building just below the fort.

There Mr. Fogg looked at a strange vehicle — a kind of frame on two long beams, raised slightly at the front like the runners of a sledge, with space for five or six people. A tall mast was fixed to the frame, held tightly by metal ropes, with a large sail attached to it. The mast had an iron support for hoisting a front sail. At the back, a kind of rudder was used to steer the vehicle. It was, in short, a sledge built like a small sailing boat. In winter, when trains are blocked by snow, these sledges travel very fast across the frozen fields between stations. With more sails than a small boat, and the wind behind them, they move across the flat land as fast as, or even faster than, express trains.

Mr. Fogg quickly agreed on a price with the owner of this land vehicle. The wind was good — strong and blowing from the west. The snow had frozen hard, and Mudge was very confident he could bring Mr. Fogg to Omaha within a few hours. From there, trains ran east often to Chicago and New York. It was not impossible to make up the lost time, and this chance could not be missed.

Not wanting to make Aouda travel in the cold open air, Mr. Fogg suggested leaving her with Passepartout at Fort Kearney, with Passepartout taking her to Europe by a better and more comfortable route. But Aouda refused to leave Mr. Fogg, and Passepartout was happy about her decision — nothing would make him leave his master while Fix was still with them.

It was hard to know what the detective was thinking. Had Phileas Fogg's return changed his mind, or did he still think Fogg was a very clever criminal who, after completing his trip around the world, would feel completely safe in England? Perhaps Fix's opinion of Phileas Fogg had changed a little, but he was still determined to do his duty and bring the whole group back to England as quickly as possible.

At eight o'clock the sledge was ready to leave. The passengers sat down on it and wrapped themselves tightly in their travel coats. The two large sails were raised, and pushed by the wind, the sledge moved across the hard snow at forty miles an hour.

The distance between Fort Kearney and Omaha, in a straight line, was at most two hundred miles. If the wind stayed strong, the journey might take only five hours. If nothing went wrong, the sledge could reach Omaha by one o'clock.

What a journey! The travellers sat close together and could not speak because of the cold, which felt even worse because of how fast they were moving. The sledge moved as lightly as a boat on water. When the wind blew close to the ground, the sledge seemed to lift off the snow because of its sails. Mudge held the rudder steady, keeping a straight line, and used small movements to correct any sliding the vehicle tended to do. All the sails were up, and the front sail was set so it did not block the main sail. An extra mast was raised, and another front sail, spread out into the wind, added more power to the other sails. Although the exact speed could not be measured, the sledge could not have been going less than forty miles an hour.

"If nothing breaks," said Mudge, "we will get there!"

Mr. Fogg had made it worth Mudge's effort to reach Omaha on time, by offering him a generous reward.

The flat land the sledge was crossing in a straight line looked like a wide, frozen sea. It seemed like a huge frozen lake. The railway that went through this area ran from the south-west to the north-west, passing through Great Island, Columbus — an important town in Nebraska — Schuyler, and Fremont, all the way to Omaha. It followed the right bank of the Platte River the whole way. The sledge took a shorter path, cutting across the curve that the railway made. Mudge was not worried about the Platte River blocking them because it was frozen. The route was completely clear of obstacles, and Phileas Fogg had only two things to worry about — an accident to the sledge, or the wind changing or stopping.

But the wind, far from getting weaker, blew hard enough to bend the mast — which, however, the metal ropes held firmly in place. These ropes, like the strings of a musical instrument, made sounds as if played with a violin bow. The sledge moved forward in the middle of a sad but beautiful melody.

"Those ropes make the fifth and the octave," said Mr. Fogg.

These were the only words he said during the whole journey. Aouda, comfortably wrapped in furs and coats, was protected as much as possible from the freezing wind. As for Passepartout, his face was as red as the setting sun in the mist, and he breathed in the sharp cold air with difficulty. With his natural cheerful spirit, he began to feel hopeful again. They would reach New York on the evening, or maybe even the morning, of the 11th, and there was still some chance they would arrive before the ship left for Liverpool.

Passepartout even felt a strong desire to shake Fix's hand. He remembered that it was the detective who had found the sledge — the only way to reach Omaha on time. But held back by some feeling he could not explain, he kept his usual distance. However, there was one thing Passepartout would never forget — the sacrifice Mr. Fogg had made without hesitation to save him from the Sioux. Mr. Fogg had risked his money and his life. No! His servant would never forget that!

While each person in the group was lost in very different thoughts, the sledge flew across the wide white snow. The small streams they passed over could not be seen. Fields and rivers disappeared under the same white covering. The flat land was completely empty. Between the Union Pacific road and the branch line connecting Kearney with Saint Joseph, it formed a large, empty stretch of land. No village, station, or fort appeared anywhere. From time to time they rushed past a ghost-like tree, whose white bare branches twisted and rattled in the wind. Sometimes flocks of wild birds flew up, or groups of thin, hungry, fierce wolves ran howling behind the sledge. Passepartout, with his gun in his hand, was ready to shoot at any that came too close. If an accident had happened to the sledge at that moment, the travellers, attacked by these animals, would have been in terrible danger. But the sledge kept moving steadily, quickly pulled away from the wolves, and before long left the howling pack far behind.

Around noon, Mudge recognised certain landmarks and understood that he was crossing the Platte River. He said nothing, but he was sure that Omaha was now less than twenty miles away. Less than an hour later, he let go of the rudder and brought down the sails, while the sledge, still carried forward by the great speed the wind had given it, continued for half a mile more without its sails spread.

It finally stopped, and Mudge, pointing to a group of snow-covered rooftops, said: "We have arrived!"

Arrived! At the station that connects every day, by many trains, with the Atlantic coast!

Passepartout and Fix jumped off, stretched their stiff legs, and helped Mr. Fogg and the young woman down from the sledge. Phileas Fogg thanked Mudge generously, Passepartout shook his hand warmly, and the group walked towards the Omaha railway station.

The Pacific Railroad ends at this important Nebraska town. Omaha is connected to Chicago by the Chicago and Rock Island Railroad, which runs directly east and stops at fifty stations.

A train was ready to leave when Mr. Fogg and his group reached the station, and they just had time to get on. They had seen nothing of Omaha, but Passepartout admitted to himself that this was not a problem, since they were not travelling as tourists.

The train moved quickly across the state of Iowa, through Council Bluffs, Des Moines, and Iowa City. During the night it crossed the Mississippi at Davenport, and through Rock Island it entered Illinois. The next day, the 10th, at four o'clock in the afternoon, it arrived in Chicago, which had already recovered from its great fire and sat proudly on the shore of beautiful Lake Michigan.

Nine hundred miles separated Chicago from New York, but trains from Chicago were plentiful. Mr. Fogg moved straight from one train to the other, and the locomotive of the Pittsburgh, Fort Wayne, and Chicago Railway left at full speed, as if it understood perfectly that this gentleman had no time to waste. It passed through Indiana, Ohio, Pennsylvania, and New Jersey like lightning, rushing through towns with old-sounding names, some of which had streets and tram lines but still no houses. At last the Hudson River came into view, and at a quarter past eleven on the evening of the 11th, the train stopped at the station on the right bank of the river, right in front of the pier of the Cunard line.

The "China," headed for Liverpool, had left three-quarters of an hour earlier!
