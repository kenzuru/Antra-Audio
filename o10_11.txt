
   "The first of June! The Kings are leaving for the seaside tomorrow, and I'm free. Three months of vacation—how much I'll enjoy it!" said Meg happily, coming home on a warm day to find Jo lying on the sofa looking very tired, while Beth took off her dusty boots and Amy made lemonade for everyone.

"Aunt March left today, and thank goodness for that!" said Jo. "I was really afraid she'd ask me to go with her. If she had, I would have felt like I had to say yes, but Plumfield is about as fun as a graveyard, you know, and I'd rather not go. We had a busy time helping the old lady get ready to leave, and I was nervous every time she spoke to me, because I was trying so hard to be helpful and pleasant that I was afraid she'd decide she couldn't leave without me. I was worried until she was finally in the carriage, and even then I had one last scare, because as it drove away, she leaned out her head and said, 'Josyphine, won't you—?' I didn't hear the rest, because I turned and ran away. I really did run, and went around the corner where I felt safe."

"Poor Jo! She came in looking like something was chasing her," said Beth, gently holding her sister's feet in a caring way.

"Aunt March is a real vampire, isn't she?" said Amy, tasting her lemonade carefully.

"She means vampire, not seaweed, but it doesn't matter. It's too hot to worry about using the right word," said Jo quietly.

"What will you do during your vacation?" asked Amy, changing the subject nicely.

"I'll sleep late and do nothing," answered Meg from the depths of the rocking chair. "I've had to get up early all winter and spend my days working for other people, so now I'm going to rest and enjoy myself as much as I want."

"That lazy idea wouldn't work for me," said Jo. "I've collected a pile of books, and I'm going to spend my time reading up in the old apple tree, when I'm not having fun—"

"Don't say 'fun'!" begged Amy, as a way to get back at Jo for correcting her earlier.

"I'll say 'a good time' then, with Laurie. That sounds nicer and makes more sense, since he's such a cheerful person."

"Let's not do any lessons, Beth, for a while, but just play and rest like the others plan to," suggested Amy.

"Well, I will, if Mother doesn't mind. I want to learn some new songs, and my dolls need some attention for the summer. They're in terrible shape and really need new clothes."

"May we, Mother?" asked Meg, turning to Mrs. March, who sat sewing in what they called 'Marmee's corner'.

"You may try your experiment for a week and see how you like it. I think by Saturday night you will find that all play and no work is just as bad as all work and no play."

"Oh, no! It will be wonderful, I'm sure," said Meg happily.

"I now suggest a toast, as my friend Sairy Gamp says. Fun forever, and no hard work!" cried Jo, standing up with her glass raised as the lemonade was passed around.

They all drank to that happily and started the experiment by relaxing for the rest of the day. The next morning, Meg didn't come downstairs until ten o'clock. Her lonely breakfast didn't taste good, and the room looked messy and empty, because Jo hadn't filled the flower vases, Beth hadn't dusted, and Amy's books were scattered everywhere. Nothing was neat and pleasant except 'Marmee's corner', which looked the same as always. There Meg sat, meaning to 'rest and read', which really meant yawning and imagining what pretty summer dresses she would buy with her salary. Jo spent the morning on the river with Laurie, and the afternoon reading and crying over The Wide, Wide World, up in the apple tree. Beth started by taking everything out of the big closet where her dolls lived, but she got tired before she was halfway done, left everything in a mess, and went to her music, happy that she had no dishes to wash. Amy arranged her little corner of the garden, put on her best white dress, fixed her hair, and sat down to draw under the honeysuckle, hoping someone would come by and ask who the young artist was. But the only visitor was a curious daddy-longlegs who looked closely at her work, so she went for a walk, got caught in a rain shower, and came home wet.

At teatime they compared how their days had gone, and all agreed that it had been a nice, though unusually long day. Meg, who had gone shopping in the afternoon and bought a pretty blue cotton dress, discovered after she had cut it that it couldn't be washed, which upset her a little. Jo had burned her nose from being out in the sun while boating, and got a bad headache from reading too long. Beth was troubled by the mess in her closet and by trying to learn three or four songs at the same time. Amy was very upset about the damage to her dress, because Katy Brown's party was the next day and, like Flora McFlimsey, she had 'nothing to wear'. But these were small problems, and they all told their mother that the experiment was going very well. She smiled, said nothing, and with Hannah's help did their forgotten work, keeping the house pleasant and everything running smoothly. It was surprising what a strange and uncomfortable situation was created by all this 'resting and enjoying'. The days kept feeling longer and longer, the weather kept changing, and so did everyone's moods. An unsettled feeling came over everyone, and with idle hands, trouble found its way in. As a great luxury, Meg sent out some of her sewing to someone else, and then found that time passed so slowly that she started cutting and ruining her clothes as she tried to update them to be more fashionable. Jo read until her eyes hurt and she was tired of books, became so restless that even good-natured Laurie had an argument with her, and felt so low in spirits that she really wished she had gone with Aunt March. Beth got along fairly well, because she kept forgetting that it was supposed to be all play and no work, and went back to her usual ways from time to time. But something in the air affected her too, and more than once she was quite upset, so much so that at one point she actually shook poor dear Joanna the doll and told her she was 'a fright'. Amy had the hardest time of all, because she had fewer ways to keep herself busy, and when her sisters left her to entertain herself, she quickly found that even a clever and important little person like herself could be a great burden to herself. She didn't like dolls, fairy tales felt too childish, and you couldn't draw all day long. Tea parties weren't much fun, and neither were picnics, unless they were very well planned. "If someone could have a nice house full of good friends, or go traveling, the summer would be wonderful, but staying home with three selfish sisters and one grown-up boy is enough to test anyone's patience," complained Amy, after several days of pleasure-seeking, complaining, and boredom.

Nobody wanted to admit they were tired of the experiment, but by Friday night each of them privately agreed that she was glad the week was almost over. Hoping to make the lesson stronger, Mrs. March, who had a good sense of humor, decided to end the experiment in a fitting way, so she gave Hannah a day off and let the girls feel the full effect of the play system.

When they got up on Saturday morning, there was no fire in the kitchen, no breakfast in the dining room, and their mother was nowhere to be seen.

"Oh no! What has happened?" cried Jo, looking around in confusion.

Meg ran upstairs and soon came back, looking relieved but a little confused and slightly embarrassed.

"Mother isn't sick, only very tired, and she says she's going to stay quietly in her room all day and let us do the best we can. It's a very strange thing for her to do, she doesn't act like herself at all. But she says it's been a hard week for her, so we mustn't complain but take care of ourselves."

"That's easy enough, and I like the idea. I'm really wanting something to do—I mean some new activity, you know," added Jo quickly.

In fact it was a great relief to them all to have a little work to do, and they started eagerly, but soon understood the truth of Hannah's saying, "Housekeeping isn't easy." There was plenty of food in the kitchen, and while Beth and Amy set the table, Meg and Jo made breakfast, wondering as they did why servants ever complained about hard work.

"I'll take some up to Mother, even though she said not to worry about her and that she'd take care of herself," said Meg, who was in charge and felt very grown-up standing behind the teapot.

So a tray was prepared before anyone started eating, and was taken upstairs with the cook's compliments. The boiled tea was very bitter, the omelette was burned, and the biscuits had dark spots from too much baking soda, but Mrs. March received her breakfast with thanks and laughed about it after Jo had left.

"Poor girls, they'll have a hard time, I'm afraid, but they won't suffer, and it will be good for them," she said, taking out the better food she had brought for herself, and quietly getting rid of the bad breakfast so their feelings wouldn't be hurt—a kind little act of a mother for which they would have been grateful.

There were many complaints downstairs, and the main cook was very unhappy about her failures. "Never mind, I'll make dinner and be the servant, you be the mistress, keep your hands clean, receive guests, and give orders," said Jo, who knew even less than Meg about cooking.

This kind offer was happily accepted, and Meg went to the living room, which she quickly tidied by pushing the mess under the sofa and closing the blinds to avoid having to dust. Jo, confident in her own abilities and wanting to make up for her argument with Laurie, immediately sent him a note inviting him to dinner.

"You'd better see what you have before you think about having guests," said Meg, when she heard about the generous but impulsive invitation.

"Oh, there's corned beef and plenty of potatoes, and I'll get some asparagus and a lobster as a special treat, as Hannah says. We'll have lettuce and make a salad. I don't know how, but the cookbook will tell me. I'll make blanc mange and strawberries for dessert, and coffee too, if you want it to be really nice."

"Don't try too many complicated dishes, Jo, because you can't make anything good except gingerbread and molasses candy. I'm not getting involved in this dinner party, and since you invited Laurie on your own, you can take care of him yourself."

"I don't want you to do anything except be polite to him and help serve the pudding. You'll give me advice if I get into trouble, won't you?" asked Jo, feeling a little hurt.

"Yes, but I don't know much, except about bread and a few simple things. You'd better ask Mother's permission before you order anything," answered Meg sensibly.

"Of course I will. I'm not silly." And Jo went off feeling upset at the doubts about her cooking skills.

"Get what you like, and don't disturb me. I'm going out for dinner and can't worry about things at home," said Mrs. March, when Jo came to ask her. "I never really enjoyed housekeeping, and I'm going to take a vacation today—reading, writing, visiting, and enjoying myself."

The unusual sight of her usually busy mother rocking comfortably and reading so early in the morning made Jo feel like something very unnatural was happening, because an earthquake or a volcanic eruption would hardly have seemed stranger.

"Everything is out of sorts, somehow," she said to herself, going back downstairs. "Beth is crying, and that's always a sign that something is wrong in this family. If Amy is causing trouble, I'll sort her out."

Feeling quite troubled herself, Jo hurried into the living room and found Beth sobbing over Pip the canary, who lay dead in the cage with his little feet stretched out sadly, as if begging for the food he had died without.

"It's all my fault. I forgot him—there isn't a seed or a drop of water left. Oh, Pip! Oh, Pip! How could I be so cruel to you?" cried Beth, picking the poor little bird up in her hands and trying to bring him back to life.

Jo looked into his half-closed eye, felt his tiny heart, and finding him stiff and cold, shook her head and offered her domino box as a coffin.

"Put him in the oven, and maybe he'll get warm and come back to life," said Amy hopefully.

"He died from having no food, and he's not going to be baked now that he's dead. I'll make him a burial cloth, and he'll be buried in the garden. I'll never have another bird—never—my Pip! Because I'm too careless to own one," murmured Beth, sitting on the floor with her pet held gently in her hands.

"We'll have the funeral this afternoon, and we'll all go. Now don't cry, Beth. It's sad, but nothing has gone right this week, and Pip has had the worst of it. Make the burial cloth and lay him in my box, and after the dinner party we'll have a nice little funeral," said Jo, beginning to feel like she had taken on too much.

Leaving the others to comfort Beth, she went to the kitchen, which was in a very discouraging state of mess. Putting on a large apron, she got to work and piled up the dishes ready for washing, when she discovered that the fire had gone out.

"What a wonderful situation!" said Jo unhappily, opening the stove door and poking around among the ashes.

After getting the fire going again, she decided to go to the market while the water heated. The walk improved her mood, and feeling pleased that she had found good deals, she walked home carrying a very small lobster, some very old asparagus, and two boxes of sour strawberries. By the time she had cleaned everything up, it was time to start cooking and the stove was very hot. Hannah had left a pan of bread dough to rise. Meg had worked it earlier in the morning, left it near the stove to rise a second time, and forgotten about it. Meg was entertaining Sallie Gardiner in the living room when the door flew open and a messy, flushed figure with flour on her face appeared, asking sharply:

"I say, hasn't the bread risen enough when it's running over the sides of the pans?"

Sallie started to laugh, but Meg nodded and raised her eyebrows as high as she could, which caused the messy figure to disappear and put the sour bread in the oven without further delay. Mrs. March went out after quietly checking on things here and there, and also said a few comforting words to Beth, who sat making a burial cloth while the dear departed lay in the domino box. A strange feeling of helplessness came over the girls as the gray bonnet disappeared around the corner, and they felt desperate when, a few minutes later, Miss Crocker arrived saying she had come to dinner. This woman was a thin, yellowish woman with a sharp nose and curious eyes who noticed everything and gossiped about everything she saw. They didn't like her, but had been taught to be kind to her, simply because she was old and poor and had few friends. So Meg gave her the comfortable chair and tried to keep her company, while she asked questions, criticized everything, and told stories about the people she knew.

Words cannot describe the worries, experiences, and efforts that Jo went through that morning, and the dinner she served became a story that was told as a joke for a long time. Afraid to ask for any more advice, she did her best alone and learned that something more than energy and good intentions is needed to be a good cook. She boiled the asparagus for an hour and was sad to find that the tops had fallen off and the stalks were harder than ever. The bread burned black, and the salad dressing caused her so much trouble that she couldn't make it taste good. The lobster was a mystery to her, but she hammered and poked at it until it was out of its shell, and its small size was hidden under a pile of lettuce leaves. The potatoes had to be rushed so they'd be ready at the same time as everything else, and they weren't properly done. The blanc mange was lumpy, and the strawberries weren't as ripe as they looked.

"Well, they can eat beef and bread and butter if they're hungry. It's just disappointing to spend your whole morning for nothing," thought Jo, as she rang the bell half an hour late than usual and stood, hot, tired, and unhappy, looking at the meal set before Laurie, who was used to much finer food, and Miss Crocker, whose gossiping tongue would spread stories about it everywhere.

Poor Jo would have happily hidden under the table as one dish after another was tasted and left, while Amy giggled, Meg looked worried, Miss Crocker pressed her lips together tightly, and Laurie talked and laughed with all his energy to give a cheerful atmosphere to the sad meal. Jo's one success was the fruit, because she had added plenty of sugar, and there was a pitcher of rich cream to go with it. Her hot cheeks cooled a little, and she took a long breath as the pretty glass plates went around and everyone looked pleasantly at the little pink berries floating in a sea of cream. Miss Crocker tasted first, made a face, and quickly drank some water. Jo, who had refused some, thinking there might not be enough—since there had been fewer and fewer after the picking—glanced at Laurie, but he was eating bravely, though there was a slight look of discomfort on his face and he kept his eyes fixed on his plate. Amy, who liked delicate food, took a large spoonful, began to choke, hid her face in her napkin, and left the table quickly.

"Oh, what is it?" cried Jo, feeling very anxious.

"Salt instead of sugar, and the cream is sour," replied Meg with a dramatic gesture.

Jo let out a groan and fell back in her chair, remembering that she had given the berries a final quick sprinkle from one of the two boxes on the kitchen table and had forgotten to put the milk in the refrigerator. Her face turned red and she was about to cry when she caught Laurie's eyes, which were full of humor despite his best efforts to look serious. The funny side of the whole situation suddenly struck her, and she laughed until tears ran down her cheeks. So did everyone else, even 'Croaker' as the girls called the old woman, and the unfortunate dinner ended happily with bread, butter, olives, and laughter.

"I don't have enough energy to clean up now, so let's calm ourselves down with a funeral," said Jo, as they stood up and Miss Crocker got ready to leave, eager to share this new story at another friend's dinner table.

They did calm down for Beth's sake. Laurie dug a grave under the ferns in the grove, little Pip was placed inside with many tears from his loving owner, and covered with moss, while a little wreath of violets and small flowers was hung on the stone that had his poem, written by Jo while she struggled with the dinner:

Here lies Pip March,
Who died the 7th of June;
Loved and mourned greatly,
And not forgotten soon.

At the end of the ceremony, Beth went to her room, overcome by emotion and too much food, but there was no place to rest, because the beds hadn't been made, and she found that her sadness was eased by fluffing up the pillows and putting things in order. Meg helped Jo clean up the remains of the meal, which took half the afternoon and left them so tired that they agreed to be happy with tea and toast for supper.

Laurie took Amy for a drive, which was a kind act, because the sour cream seemed to have had a bad effect on her mood. Mrs. March came home to find the three older girls working hard in the middle of the afternoon, and a look at the closet gave her a clear idea of how one part of the experiment had gone.

Before the hardworking girls could rest, several visitors came, and there was a rush to get ready to see them. Then tea had to be made, errands done, and one or two necessary bits of sewing that had been left to the last minute. As evening came, soft and quiet, they gathered one by one on the porch where the June roses were beautifully beginning to bloom, and each one groaned or sighed as she sat down, as if tired or troubled.

"What a terrible day this has been!" started Jo, who was usually the first to speak.

"It felt shorter than usual, but so uncomfortable," said Meg.

"Nothing like home at all," added Amy.

"It can't feel like home without Marmee and little Pip," sighed Beth, looking sadly at the empty cage above her head.

"Here's Mother, dear, and you shall have another bird tomorrow if you want one."

As she spoke, Mrs. March came and took her place among them, looking as if her own day off had not been much more pleasant than theirs.

"Are you satisfied with your experiment, girls, or do you want another week of it?" she asked, as Beth moved close to her and the rest turned toward her with their faces brightening, the way flowers turn toward the sun.

"I don't want another week!" cried Jo firmly.

"Nor I," echoed the others.

"So you think it's better to have a few responsibilities and live a little for others, do you?"

"Relaxing and doing nothing all day doesn't work," said Jo, shaking her head. "I'm tired of it and plan to go back to doing something useful right away."

"Perhaps you should learn basic cooking. That's a useful skill that every woman should have," said Mrs. March, laughing quietly at the memory of Jo's dinner party, because she had met Miss Crocker and heard her account of it.

"Mother, did you go away and leave everything as it was, just to see how we'd manage?" cried Meg, who had suspected this all day.

"Yes, I wanted you to see how everyone's comfort depends on each person doing her share faithfully. While Hannah and I did your work, you got along fairly well, though I don't think you were very happy or pleasant to be around. So I thought that, as a small lesson, I would show you what happens when everyone thinks only of herself. Don't you feel that it is nicer to help one another, to have daily duties which make free time feel sweeter when it comes, and to be patient and kind, so that home may be comfortable and pleasant for all of us?"

"We do, Mother, we do!" cried the girls.

"Then let me advise you to take up your small responsibilities again, because although they sometimes feel heavy, they are good for us and become lighter as we learn to carry them. Work is healthy, and there is plenty for everyone. It keeps us from boredom and trouble, is good for our health and spirits, and gives us a sense of strength and independence that is better than money or fashion."

"We'll work like bees, and love it too—just see if we don't," said Jo. "I'll learn basic cooking as my holiday project, and the next dinner party I have will be a success."

"I'll make the set of shirts for Father, instead of letting you do it, Marmee. I can and I will, though I'm not fond of sewing. That will be better than fussing over my own things, which are already nice enough," said Meg.

"I'll do my lessons every day and not spend so much time with my music and dolls. I'm being foolish and should be studying, not playing," was Beth's decision, while Amy followed their example by bravely declaring, "I shall learn to sew buttonholes properly, and pay more attention to my grammar."

"Very good! Then I am quite satisfied with the experiment, and I believe we won't need to repeat it. Only don't go to the other extreme and work yourselves to exhaustion. Have regular times for work and play, make each day both useful and pleasant, and prove that you understand the value of time by using it well. Then your young years will be happy, old age will bring few regrets, and life will become a beautiful success, despite being poor."

"We'll remember, Mother!" and they did.
