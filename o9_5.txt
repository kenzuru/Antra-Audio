

   Phileas Fogg rightly guessed that his departure from London would cause great excitement in the West End. The news of the bet spread through the Reform Club and gave its members an exciting topic of conversation. From the club it quickly reached the newspapers throughout England. The boasted "tour of the world" was talked about, argued over, and debated with as much energy as if it were a major legal dispute. Some people took Phileas Fogg's side, but the large majority shook their heads and declared themselves against him. It was absurd and impossible, they said, that the tour of the world could be made — except in theory and on paper — in this minimum of time, with the existing means of travel. The Times, the Standard, the Morning Post, the Daily News, and twenty other highly respected newspapers dismissed Mr. Fogg's plan as madness. Only the Daily Telegraph hesitantly supported him. People in general thought he was crazy, and blamed his friends at the Reform Club for accepting a bet that showed how disturbed its proposer's mind was.

Passionate and somewhat logical articles appeared on the question, for geography is one of the favourite subjects of the English, and the columns written about Phileas Fogg's adventure were eagerly read by all kinds of readers. At first some reckless people, mainly women, supported his cause, and it became even more popular when the Illustrated London News published his portrait, copied from a photograph in the Reform Club. A few readers of the Daily Telegraph even dared to say, "Why not, after all? Stranger things have happened."

At last, on the 7th of October, a long article appeared in the bulletin of the Royal Geographical Society, which examined the question from every point of view and showed clearly that the whole project was completely foolish.

Everything, it said, was against the travellers — every obstacle created by both man and nature. A perfect matching of departure and arrival times, which was impossible, was absolutely necessary for his success. He might perhaps count on the trains arriving at the planned hours in Europe, where the distances were relatively short. But when he planned to cross India in three days and the United States in seven, could he rely on completing his task without any doubts? There were mechanical breakdowns, the risk of trains going off the tracks, collisions, bad weather, roads blocked by snow — were all these not against Phileas Fogg? When travelling by steamer in winter, would he not be at the mercy of winds and fog? Was it unusual for the best ocean steamers to be two or three days late? But a single delay would be enough to break the whole chain of connections. If Phileas Fogg should miss even one steamer by an hour, he would have to wait for the next one, and that would completely ruin his attempt.

This article made a great deal of noise, and, being copied into all the newspapers, seriously reduced the number of people who supported the bold traveller.

Everyone knows that England is the home of people who love betting, and who are of a higher class than simple gamblers. Betting is part of the English character. Not only the members of the Reform Club, but the general public too made large bets for or against Phileas Fogg. He was listed in the betting books as if he were a racehorse. Certificates of the bets appeared on the Stock Exchange. "Phileas Fogg" shares were offered at face value or above, and a great amount of business was done in them. But five days after the article in the bulletin of the Geographical Society appeared, demand began to fall. "Phileas Fogg" shares went down in value. They were offered in groups — first of five, then of ten — until at last nobody would accept fewer than twenty, fifty, or a hundred!

Lord Albemarle, an elderly gentleman who could not walk and was tied to his chair, was now the only remaining supporter of Phileas Fogg. This nobleman would have given his whole fortune to be able to make the tour of the world himself, even if it took ten years. He bet five thousand pounds on Phileas Fogg. When the foolishness as well as the pointlessness of the adventure was pointed out to him, he simply replied, "If the thing can be done, the first to do it should be an Englishman."

The number of people supporting Fogg grew smaller and smaller. Everyone was turning against him, and the bets stood at a hundred and fifty and two hundred to one against. Then, a week after his departure, something happened that removed even his last remaining supporters.

At nine o'clock one evening, the police commissioner was sitting in his office when the following telegraphic message was placed in his hands:

Suez to London.

ROWAN, COMMISSIONER OF POLICE, SCOTLAND YARD:
    I have found the bank robber, Phileas Fogg. Send without delay a warrant for his arrest to Bombay.

FIX, Detective.

The effect of this message was immediate. The polished gentleman disappeared and was replaced by the image of the bank robber. His photograph, which hung with those of the other members at the Reform Club, was carefully examined, and it matched, feature by feature, the description of the robber that had been given to the police. Phileas Fogg's mysterious habits were remembered — his solitary ways, his sudden departure. It seemed clear that, by planning a tour around the world on the excuse of a bet, he had had no other purpose than to escape from the detectives and throw them off his trail.
