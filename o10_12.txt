

  Beth was in charge of the family mailbox, because she was home the most and could check it regularly, and she truly loved the daily job of unlocking the little door and giving out the mail. One day in July she came in with her hands full and went around the house delivering letters and packages like a postman.

"Here's your flowers, Mother! Laurie never forgets," she said, putting the fresh flowers in the vase that stood in 'Marmee's corner' and was always kept filled by the kind-hearted boy.

"Miss Meg March, one letter and a glove," continued Beth, giving the items to her sister, who sat near her mother, sewing cuffs.

"Why, I left a pair over there, and here is only one," said Meg, looking at the gray cotton glove. "Didn't you drop the other in the garden?"

"No, I'm sure I didn't, because there was only one in the mailbox."

"I hate having unmatched gloves! Never mind, the other may turn up. My letter is only a translation of the German song I wanted. I think Mr. Brooke did it, because this isn't Laurie's handwriting."

Mrs. March glanced at Meg, who was looking very pretty in her light morning dress, with small curls blowing around her forehead, and very grown-up as she sat sewing at her little worktable, full of neat white rolls of fabric, so unaware of what her mother was thinking as she sewed and sang, her fingers moving quickly while her thoughts were full of the simple, innocent daydreams of a young girl, that Mrs. March smiled and felt content.

"Two letters for Doctor Jo, a book, and a funny old hat, which covered the whole mailbox and stuck out the sides," said Beth, laughing as she went into the study where Jo sat writing.

"What a clever fellow Laurie is! I said I wished bigger hats were fashionable, because I burn my face every hot day. He said, 'Why follow fashion? Wear a big hat and be comfortable!' I said I would if I had one, and he's sent me this to test me. I'll wear it for fun and show him I don't care about fashion." And hanging the old wide-brimmed hat on a statue of Plato, Jo read her letters.

One from her mother made her cheeks glow and her eyes fill with tears, because it said:

My Dear:

I write a short note to tell you how much satisfaction I feel watching your efforts to control your temper. You say nothing about your struggles, failures, or successes, and perhaps think that no one notices them except the Friend whose help you ask for daily, if I may trust the well-worn cover of your prayer book. I, too, have seen them all, and truly believe in the sincerity of your commitment, since it is beginning to show results. Keep going, dear, patiently and bravely, and always know that no one sympathizes more warmly with you than your loving...

Mother

"That does me good! That's worth more than money or any amount of praise. Oh, Marmee, I do try! I will keep on trying and not give up, since I have you to help me."

Laying her head on her arms, Jo let a few happy tears fall over her little story, because she had thought that no one noticed or appreciated her efforts to be good, and this reassurance was doubly precious and doubly encouraging, because it was unexpected and came from the person whose approval she valued most. Feeling stronger than ever to face and overcome her faults, she pinned the note inside her dress as a reminder in case she was caught off guard, and opened her other letter, ready for either good or bad news. In a large, bold handwriting, Laurie wrote:

Dear Jo, Hello!

Some English girls and boys are coming to visit me tomorrow and I want to have a great time. If the weather is good, I'm going to set up my tent in Longmeadow and row everyone up to have lunch and play croquet—make a fire, cook over it like we're camping, and have all kinds of fun. They are nice people and like that sort of thing. Brooke will come to keep us boys in order, and Kate Vaughn will be the responsible one for the girls. I want all of you to come. I won't take no for an answer from Beth, and nobody will bother her. Don't worry about food—I'll take care of that and everything else. Just come, please!

In a great hurry, Yours always, Laurie.

"What wonderful news!" cried Jo, rushing in to tell Meg.

"Of course we can go, Mother? It will be a real help to Laurie, because I can row, and Meg can look after the lunch, and the others can help in some way."

"I hope the Vaughns aren't fancy grown-up people. Do you know anything about them, Jo?" asked Meg.

"Only that there are four of them. Kate is older than you, Fred and Frank are twins about my age, and there's a little girl named Grace who is nine or ten. Laurie knew them in Europe and liked the boys. From the way he pressed his lips together when he talked about Kate, I got the feeling he didn't think much of her."

"I'm so glad my French cotton dress is clean—it's just right and very flattering!" said Meg happily. "Do you have anything decent to wear, Jo?"

"My red and gray boating outfit—good enough for me. I'll be rowing and walking around, so I don't want to worry about keeping anything smart. Will you come, Beth?"

"If you won't let any boys talk to me."

"Not a single boy!"

"I like to please Laurie, and I'm not afraid of Mr. Brooke—he's so kind. But I don't want to play games or sing or have to say much. I'll work hard and not bother anyone, and you'll take care of me, Jo, so I'll go."

"That's my good girl. You really do try to fight your shyness, and I love you for it. Fighting faults isn't easy, as I know, and an encouraging word gives a real boost. Thank you, Mother." And Jo gave the thin cheek a grateful kiss, which meant more to Mrs. March than if it had brought back all the freshness of her youth.

"I got a box of chocolate drops and the picture I wanted to copy," said Amy, showing her mail.

"And I got a note from Mr. Laurence, asking me to come over and play for him tonight before the lamps are lit, and I shall go," added Beth, whose friendship with the kind old gentleman was growing very well.

"Now let's work extra hard today so that we can enjoy tomorrow with nothing on our minds," said Jo, putting down her pen and picking up a broom.

When the sun looked into the girls' room early the next morning and promised a fine day, he saw a funny sight. Each girl had prepared for the outing in the way she thought most necessary. Meg had put an extra row of small paper curlers across her forehead, Jo had put cold cream all over her sunburned face, Beth had taken her doll Joanna to bed with her to make up for the coming separation, and Amy had topped everything off by putting a clothespin on her nose to improve its shape. It was the kind artists use to hold paper on their drawing boards, so it seemed quite suitable for this purpose. This funny sight seemed to amuse the sun, because he shone so brightly that Jo woke up and woke her sisters with a good laugh at Amy's accessory.

Sunshine and laughter were good signs for a fun outing, and soon there was lively activity in both houses. Beth, who was ready first, kept reporting on what was happening next door and kept her sisters entertained as they got ready, with frequent updates from the window.

"There goes the man with the tent! I can see Mrs. Barker packing the lunch in a hamper and a large basket. Now Mr. Laurence is looking up at the sky and the weathervane. I wish he would come too. There's Laurie—he looks like a sailor, what a nice boy! Oh my goodness! Here's a carriage full of people—a tall lady, a little girl, and two lively-looking boys. One of them has a crutch, poor thing. Laurie didn't tell us that. Hurry, girls! It's getting late. Oh, there's Ned Moffat—what a surprise! Meg, isn't that the man who waved to you once when we were shopping?"

"Yes, it is. How strange that he should come. I thought he was in the mountains. There is Sallie. I'm glad she got back in time. Do I look all right, Jo?" cried Meg nervously.

"Perfect. Straighten your dress and put your hat on properly—it looks odd tilted like that and will blow off at the first breath of wind. Now then, come on!"

"Oh, Jo, you're not going to wear that terrible hat? It's too ridiculous! You can't make yourself look silly like that," said Meg disapprovingly, as Jo tied the wide-brimmed, old-fashioned hat that Laurie had sent as a joke onto her head with a red ribbon.

"Oh yes I will, because it's wonderful—so shady, light, and big. It'll be fun, and I don't mind looking silly if I'm comfortable." With that, Jo marched off and the rest followed—a bright little group of sisters, all looking their best in summer outfits, with happy faces under their hat brims.

Laurie ran to meet them and introduced them to his friends in the most welcoming way. The lawn was the reception area, and for several minutes there was a lively scene. Meg was relieved to see that Miss Kate, though twenty years old, dressed simply in a way that American girls would do well to copy, and was very pleased by Mr. Ned's assurances that he had come especially to see her. Jo understood why Laurie had pressed his lips together when speaking of Kate, because that young lady had a cold, don't-come-near-me manner, which was very different from the relaxed, friendly ways of the other girls. Beth observed the new boys and decided that the one with the crutch was not 'dreadful' but gentle and quiet, and she would be kind to him for that reason. Amy found Grace to be a well-mannered, cheerful little person, and after staring silently at each other for a few minutes, they suddenly became very good friends.

Since the tent, lunch, and croquet equipment had been sent ahead earlier, the party was soon on the water, and the two boats pushed off together, leaving Mr. Laurence waving his hat from the shore. Laurie and Jo rowed one boat, Mr. Brooke and Ned the other, while Fred Vaughn, the energetic twin, did his best to cause trouble by paddling around in a small rowboat like an excited water insect. Jo's funny hat proved very useful—it broke the ice by making everyone laugh, it created a pleasant breeze as it flapped back and forth while she rowed, and she said it would make an excellent umbrella for the whole party if rain came. Miss Kate decided that Jo was 'unusual' but rather clever, and smiled at her from a distance.

Meg, in the other boat, was in a very pleasant position, sitting face to face with the rowers, who both admired the view and rowed with great skill. Mr. Brooke was a quiet, serious young man with handsome brown eyes and a pleasant voice. Meg liked his calm manner and thought of him as someone who knew a great deal about useful things. He didn't talk to her much, but he looked at her often, and she felt sure he didn't dislike her. Ned, being in college, naturally showed off all the confidence that first-year students feel they must display. He wasn't very wise, but very good-natured, and altogether a fine person to have at a picnic. Sallie Gardiner was focused on keeping her white dress clean and chatting with the energetic Fred, who kept poor Beth in constant fear with his pranks.

It wasn't far to Longmeadow, but the tent was set up and the croquet wickets were in the ground by the time they arrived. It was a pleasant green field with three wide oak trees in the middle and a smooth strip of grass for croquet.

"Welcome to Camp Laurence!" said the young host, as they landed with happy exclamations.

"Brooke is the commander, I'm in charge of food, the other fellows are staff officers, and you ladies are the company. The tent is for your special use, and that oak tree is your sitting room, this one is the dining room, and the third is the camp kitchen. Now, let's have a game before it gets too hot, and then we'll see about dinner."

Frank, Beth, Amy, and Grace sat down to watch the game played by the other eight. Mr. Brooke chose Meg, Kate, and Fred as his team. Laurie took Sallie, Jo, and Ned. The English players played well, but the Americans played better and fought for every point as strongly as if the spirit of independence drove them. Jo and Fred had several arguments, and once almost came to harsh words. Jo had gone through the last wicket and had missed her stroke, which annoyed her greatly. Fred was right behind her and it was his turn before hers. He hit his ball, it touched the wicket, and stopped just a tiny bit on the wrong side. No one was very close, and running up to look, he gave it a quick push with his foot, which put it just barely on the right side.

"I'm through! Now, Miss Jo, I'll beat you and get to the stake first," cried the young man, swinging his mallet for another shot.

"You pushed it. I saw you. It's my turn now," said Jo sharply.

"Honestly, I didn't move it. It rolled a little, perhaps, but that's allowed. So please step aside and let me have a go at the stake."

"We don't cheat in America, but you can, if you choose," said Jo angrily.

"Americans are the most dishonest of all—everyone knows that. There you go!" replied Fred, hitting her ball far away.

Jo opened her mouth to say something rude but stopped herself in time, turned red right up to her forehead, and stood for a minute, hammering a wicket into the ground as hard as she could, while Fred hit the stake and announced he had won with great excitement. She went to get her ball and took a long time finding it in the bushes, but she came back looking calm and quiet, and waited patiently for her turn. It took several strokes to get back to where she had been, and by the time she got there, the other team had almost won, because Kate's ball was the last one and was lying near the stake.

"It's all over for us! Goodbye, Kate. Miss Jo owes me, so you're finished," cried Fred excitedly, as everyone gathered to watch the end of the game.

"Americans have a habit of being generous to their enemies," said Jo, giving the boy a look that made him go red. "Especially when they beat them," she added, as, leaving Kate's ball untouched, she won the game with a clever shot.

Laurie threw up his hat, then remembered it wouldn't be right to celebrate the defeat of his guests, and stopped in the middle of his cheer to whisper to his friend, "Well done, Jo! He did cheat—I saw him. We can't say so directly, but he won't do it again, trust me."

Meg pulled Jo aside, pretending to fix a loose braid, and said with approval, "It was very annoying, but you kept your temper, and I'm so glad, Jo."

"Don't praise me, Meg, because I could still slap him right now. I would certainly have lost my temper if I hadn't stayed among the plants until I got my anger under control enough to keep quiet. It's still simmering, so I hope he'll stay out of my way," replied Jo, biting her lips as she looked at Fred from under her big hat.

"Time for lunch," said Mr. Brooke, looking at his watch. "Person in charge of food, will you make the fire and get water, while Miss March, Miss Sallie, and I spread out the food? Who can make good coffee?"

"Jo can," said Meg, glad to recommend her sister. So Jo, feeling that her recent cooking lessons should do her credit now, went to take charge of the coffee, while the children gathered dry sticks, and the boys made a fire and got water from a nearby spring. Miss Kate sketched and Frank talked to Beth, who was making little mats of woven grass to use as plates.

The person in charge and his helpers soon spread the cloth with a tempting selection of food and drink, prettily decorated with green leaves. Jo announced that the coffee was ready, and everyone settled down to a good meal, because young people are rarely not hungry, and exercise makes healthy appetites stronger. It was a very cheerful lunch, because everything seemed fresh and fun, and frequent bursts of laughter startled a dignified old horse that was eating nearby. The uneven table caused many spills of cups and plates, acorns dropped into the milk, tiny black ants joined in the meal without being invited, and little caterpillars swung down from the tree to see what was happening. Three fair-haired children peeked over the fence, and an annoying dog barked at them from the other side of the river with all his energy.

"There's salt here," said Laurie, as he handed Jo a dish of berries.

"Thank you, I prefer spiders," she replied, fishing out two small ones that had fallen in. "How dare you remind me of that terrible dinner party, when yours is so nice in every way?" she added, as they both laughed and ate from the same plate since there weren't enough dishes.

"I had a wonderful time that day and still haven't gotten over it. This is no credit to me, you know—I don't really do anything. It's you and Meg and Brooke who make it all work, and I'm very grateful to you. What shall we do when we can't eat anymore?" asked Laurie, feeling that his best moment had been the lunch.

"Play games until it's cooler. I brought a card game, and I expect Miss Kate knows something new and fun. Go and ask her. She's a guest, and you should spend more time with her."

"Aren't you company too? I thought she'd be good company for Brooke, but he keeps talking to Meg, and Kate just stares at them through that ridiculous eyeglass of hers. I'm going, so you needn't try to tell me what to do, because you can't, Jo."

Miss Kate did know several new games, and since the girls wouldn't and the boys couldn't eat anymore, they all went to sit under the oak tree to play a storytelling game.

"One person begins a story—any kind of nonsense you like—and tells it as long as he pleases, only making sure to stop at an exciting moment, when the next person takes it up and does the same. It's very funny when done well and makes a wonderful mix of dramatic and comic material to laugh over. Please start, Mr. Brooke," said Kate in a commanding tone that surprised Meg, who treated the tutor with the same respect she gave any other gentleman.

Lying on the grass at the feet of the two young ladies, Mr. Brooke began the story obediently, his handsome brown eyes fixed on the sunny river.

"Once upon a time, a knight went out into the world to seek his fortune, for he had nothing but his sword and his shield. He traveled for a long time—nearly twenty-eight years—and had a difficult time of it, until he came to the palace of a good old king who had offered a reward to anyone who could tame and train a fine but wild young horse that he loved very much. The knight agreed to try, and made slow but steady progress, for the horse was a brave animal and soon learned to love his new master, though he was unpredictable and wild. Every day, when he gave lessons to the king's favorite horse, the knight rode him through the city, and as he rode, he looked everywhere for a certain beautiful face he had seen many times in his dreams but never found in real life. One day, as he rode through a quiet street, he saw at the window of a crumbling old castle the lovely face. He was very excited, asked who lived in this old castle, and was told that several captive princesses were kept there under a spell and spent all day spinning to save money to buy their freedom. The knight desperately wished he could free them, but he was poor and could only ride past each day, watching for the sweet face and wishing he could see it in the sunshine. At last he decided to get into the castle and ask how he could help. He went and knocked. The great door flew open, and he saw..."

"A breathtakingly beautiful lady, who cried with great joy, 'At last! At last!'" continued Kate, who had read French novels and liked dramatic style. "'It is she!' cried Count Gustave, and fell at her feet with great joy. 'Oh, please rise!' she said, holding out a perfectly white hand. 'Never! Not until you tell me how I can rescue you,' swore the knight, still on his knees. 'My cruel fate keeps me here until my captor is destroyed.' 'Where is this villain?' 'In the purple room. Go, brave heart, and save me from despair.' 'I obey, and will return victorious or dead!' With these dramatic words he rushed away, and throwing open the door of the purple room, was about to enter when he received..."

"A stunning blow from a large Greek dictionary, which an old man in a black robe threw at him," said Ned. "Immediately, the knight recovered himself, threw the villain out of the window, and turned to rejoin the lady—victorious but with a bump on his head—found the door locked, tore up the curtains, made a rope ladder, got halfway down when the ladder broke, and he fell headfirst into the water sixty feet below. He could swim like a duck, paddled around the castle until he found a small door guarded by two large men, knocked their heads together until they cracked like nuts, then, with an enormous effort of his great strength, smashed in the door, went up a stone staircase covered with a foot of dust, toads as big as your fist, and spiders that would frighten you terribly, Miss March. At the top of these steps he came suddenly upon a sight that took his breath away and froze his blood..."

"A tall figure, completely in white with a veil over its face and a lamp in its thin hand," continued Meg. "It beckoned and glided silently before him down a corridor as dark and cold as any tomb. Figures in armor stood on either side, a dead silence filled everything, the lamp burned blue, and the ghostly figure kept turning its face toward him, showing the gleam of frightening eyes through its white veil. They reached a curtained door, behind which beautiful music could be heard. He jumped forward to enter, but the ghost pulled him back and waved threateningly in front of him a..."

"Snuffbox," said Jo in a deep, creepy voice, which made everyone laugh. "'Thank you,' said the knight politely, as he took some and sneezed seven times so violently that his head fell off. 'Ha! Ha!' laughed the ghost, and after peeking through the keyhole at the princesses spinning away energetically, the evil spirit picked up her victim and put him in a large tin box, where there were eleven other knights packed together without their heads, like sardines, who all rose and began to..."

"Dance a lively dance," added Fred quickly, as Jo paused to catch her breath. "And as they danced, the crumbling old castle turned into a sailing ship at full sail. 'Up with the sail, tighten the ropes, turn the wheel hard, and man the guns!' roared the captain, as a Portuguese pirate ship appeared with a completely black flag flying from its front mast. 'Go in and win, everyone!' says the captain, and a tremendous fight began. Of course the British won—they always do."

"No, they don't!" cried Jo quietly.

"Having taken the pirate captain prisoner, sailed right over the other ship whose decks were covered with fallen enemies, for the order had been, 'Fight hard and don't give up!' 'First officer, tie up this villain and question him immediately,' said the British captain. The Portuguese said nothing and walked the plank, while the sailors cheered like mad. But the clever man dove underwater, came up under the British ship, made a hole in it, and down it went, with all sails up, 'To the bottom of the sea, sea, sea,' where..."

"Oh, goodness! What shall I say?" cried Sallie, as Fred finished his story in which he had mixed together sailing terms and facts from one of his favorite books. "Well, they went to the bottom, and a kind mermaid welcomed them, but was very sad to find the box of headless knights, and carefully preserved them in salt water, hoping to solve the mystery about them, for being a woman, she was naturally curious. After a while a diver came down, and the mermaid said, 'I'll give you a box of pearls if you can take it up,' because she wanted to bring the poor knights back to life and couldn't lift the heavy box herself. So the diver pulled it up and was very disappointed to find no pearls inside. He left it in a large empty field, where it was found by a..."

"Little goose girl, who kept a hundred fat geese in the field," said Amy, when Sallie ran out of ideas. "The little girl felt sorry for them and asked an old woman what she should do to help. 'Your geese will tell you—they know everything,' said the old woman. So she asked what she should use for new heads, since the old ones were lost, and all the geese opened their hundred mouths and screamed..."

"'Cabbages!'" continued Laurie promptly. "'Just the thing,' said the girl, and she ran to get twelve fine ones from her garden. She put them on, the knights immediately came back to life, thanked her, and went on their way happily, never knowing the difference, because there were so many similar heads in the world that no one thought anything of it. The knight I'm interested in went back to find the pretty face and learned that the princesses had all spun themselves free and gone away to get married—all except one. He was very upset about this, and mounting the horse that had stayed faithfully by his side, he rushed to the castle to see which one was left. Peeking over the hedge, he saw the one he loved picking flowers in her garden. 'Will you give me a rose?' said he. 'You must come and get it. I can't come to you—it wouldn't be proper,' said she, as sweet as honey. He tried to climb over the hedge, but it seemed to grow taller and taller. Then he tried to push through it, but it grew thicker and thicker, and he was becoming desperate. So he patiently broke one twig after another until he had made a small hole through which he looked, saying desperately, 'Let me in! Let me in!' But the pretty princess didn't seem to understand, because she picked her roses quietly and left him to find his own way in. Whether he did or not, Frank will tell you."

"I can't. I'm not playing—I never do," said Frank, worried about having to resolve the romantic situation the others had created. Beth had disappeared behind Jo, and Grace had fallen asleep.

"So the poor knight is to be left stuck in the hedge, is he?" asked Mr. Brooke, still watching the river and playing with the wild rose in his buttonhole.

"I think the princess gave him a flower and opened the gate after a while," said Laurie, smiling to himself as he threw acorns at his tutor.

"What a wonderful piece of nonsense we've made! With practice we could do something quite clever. Do you know the Truth game?"

"I hope so," said Meg seriously.

"The game, I mean?"

"What is it?" asked Fred.

"You all stack your hands, choose a number, and take turns pulling away, and the person who pulls at the right number has to honestly answer any question asked by the others. It's great fun."

"Let's try it," said Jo, who liked new games.

Miss Kate, Mr. Brooke, Meg, and Ned didn't want to play, but Fred, Sallie, Jo, and Laurie joined in, and the choice fell to Laurie.

"Who are your heroes?" asked Jo.

"Grandfather and Napoleon."

"Which lady here do you think is prettiest?" asked Sallie.

"Margaret."

"Which do you like best?" asked Fred.

"Jo, of course."

"What silly questions you ask!" said Jo with a dismissive shrug as the others laughed at Laurie's matter-of-fact tone.

"Try again. Truth isn't a bad game," said Fred.

"It's a very good one for you," replied Jo quietly. Then it was her turn.

"What is your greatest fault?" asked Fred, testing in her the quality he himself lacked.

"A quick temper."

"What do you wish for most?" asked Laurie.

"A pair of boot laces," answered Jo, guessing his intention and ruining his plan.

"That's not a real answer. You must say what you truly want most."

"Talent as a writer. Don't you wish you could give it to me, Laurie?" And she smiled at his disappointed face.

"What qualities do you most admire in a man?" asked Sallie.

"Courage and honesty."

"Now my turn," said Fred, as his hand came last.

"Let's test him," whispered Laurie to Jo, who nodded and immediately asked...

"Didn't you cheat at croquet?"

"Well, yes, a little bit."

"Good! Didn't you borrow your story from The Sea Lion?" said Laurie.

"Yes, somewhat."

"Don't you think the English nation is perfect in every way?" asked Sallie.

"I should be ashamed of myself if I didn't."

"He's a true Englishman. Now, Miss Sallie, you can have a turn without waiting. I'll start gently by asking if you don't think you are a bit of a flirt," said Laurie, giving Fred a nod to show that peace was made.

"What a rude boy! Of course I'm not," exclaimed Sallie, in a manner that suggested the opposite.

"What do you hate most?" asked Fred.

"Spiders and rice pudding."

"What do you like best?" asked Jo.

"Dancing and French gloves."

"Well, I think Truth is a very silly game. Let's have a good game of Authors to clear our minds," suggested Jo.

Ned, Frank, and the little girls joined in this game, and while it was going on, the three older ones sat apart talking. Miss Kate took out her sketchbook, and Meg watched her, while Mr. Brooke lay on the grass with a book which he wasn't reading.

"How beautifully you draw! I wish I could do it," said Meg, with both admiration and regret in her voice.

"Why don't you learn? I think you have the taste and ability for it," replied Miss Kate pleasantly.

"I haven't the time."

"Your mother probably prefers other skills, I imagine. So did mine, but I proved to her that I had talent by taking a few lessons privately, and then she was quite willing for me to continue. Can't you do the same with your teacher?"

"I don't have one."

"I forgot that young ladies in America go to school more than they do with us. Very fine schools they are too, Papa says. You go to a private one, I suppose?"

"I don't go at all. I am a teacher myself."

"Oh, really!" said Miss Kate, but she might as well have said, "How unfortunate!" because her tone suggested it, and something in her face made Meg go red and wish she hadn't been so open.

Mr. Brooke looked up and said quickly, "Young ladies in America value independence as much as their ancestors did and are admired and respected for supporting themselves."

"Oh, yes, of course it's very admirable and proper of them to do so. We have many very respectable and worthy young women who do the same and are employed by the nobility, because, being from good families, they are both well brought up and educated, you know," said Miss Kate in a superior tone that hurt Meg's pride and made her work seem not only unpleasant but beneath her.

"Did the German song suit you, Miss March?" asked Mr. Brooke, breaking an awkward silence.

"Oh, yes! It was very beautiful, and I'm very grateful to whoever translated it for me." And Meg's sad face brightened as she spoke.

"Don't you read German?" asked Miss Kate with a look of surprise.

"Not very well. My father, who taught me, is away, and I don't make much progress on my own, because I have no one to correct my pronunciation."

"Try a little now. Here is Schiller's Mary Stuart and a teacher who loves to teach." And Mr. Brooke put his book on her lap with a welcoming smile.

"It's so difficult, I'm afraid to try," said Meg, grateful but shy in front of the accomplished young lady beside her.

"I'll read a bit to encourage you." And Miss Kate read one of the most beautiful passages in a technically correct but completely flat way.

Mr. Brooke made no comment as she gave the book back to Meg, who said innocently, "I thought it was poetry."

"Some of it is. Try this passage."

There was a small smile on Mr. Brooke's face as he opened to the sad speech of poor Mary.

Meg obediently followed the long grass stem which her new teacher used as a pointer, and read slowly and carefully, without realizing it, she was making music of the difficult words through the soft, gentle sound of her voice. Down the page went the green guide, and after a while, forgetting she had a listener in her interest in the sad scene, Meg read as if she were alone, giving a little dramatic feeling to the words of the unhappy queen. If she had seen the brown eyes watching her then, she would have stopped immediately, but she never looked up, and the lesson was not spoiled for her.

"Very well indeed!" said Mr. Brooke, when she paused, completely ignoring her many mistakes and looking as if he truly did love to teach.

Miss Kate raised her eyeglass and, after looking at the little scene before her, closed her sketchbook, saying with a superior manner, "You have a nice accent and will in time be a good reader. I would advise you to continue learning, since German is a useful skill for teachers. I must check on Grace—she is getting too wild." And Miss Kate walked away, adding to herself with a small shrug, "I didn't come to look after a governess, though she is young and pretty. What unusual people these Americans are. I'm afraid Laurie will be quite spoiled among them."

"I forgot that English people look down on governesses and don't treat them as we do," said Meg, watching the departing figure with an annoyed expression.

"Tutors also have a hard time of it there, as I know from experience. There's no place like America for those of us who work, Miss Margaret." And Mr. Brooke looked so happy and cheerful that Meg was ashamed to feel sorry for herself.

"I'm glad I live here then. I don't love my work, but I do get quite a bit of satisfaction from it after all, so I won't complain. I only wish I enjoyed teaching as much as you do."

"I think you would if you had Laurie as a student. I'll be very sorry to lose him next year," said Mr. Brooke, poking holes in the grass with his finger.

"Going to college, I suppose?" Meg's lips asked the question, but her eyes added, "And what will you do then?"

"Yes, it's certainly time he went, because he's ready, and as soon as he's gone, I shall become a soldier. I am needed."

"I'm glad of that!" exclaimed Meg. "I think every young man should want to go, though it's hard for the mothers and sisters who stay behind," she added sadly.

"I have neither, and very few friends who would care whether I live or die," said Mr. Brooke quietly, as he absently placed the dead rose in the small hole he had made and covered it up, like a tiny grave.

"Laurie and his grandfather would care very much, and we would all be very sad if anything happened to you," said Meg warmly.

"Thank you, that is pleasant to hear," began Mr. Brooke, looking more cheerful, but before he could finish speaking, Ned came riding up on the old horse, wanting to show off his riding skills to the young ladies, and there was no more quiet conversation that day.

"Don't you love to ride?" asked Grace of Amy, as they rested after running around the field with the others, led by Ned.

"I love it more than anything. My sister Meg used to ride when Papa was wealthy, but we don't have horses now, except Ellen Tree," said Amy, laughing.

"Tell me about Ellen Tree. Is it a donkey?" asked Grace curiously.

"Well, you see, Jo and I are both crazy about horses, but we've only got an old side saddle and no horse. In our garden there's an apple tree with a nice low branch, so Jo put the saddle on it, attached some reins to the part that curves up, and we bounce around on Ellen Tree whenever we like."

"How funny!" laughed Grace. "I have a pony at home and go riding nearly every day in the park with Fred and Kate. It's very nice, because my friends come too, and the path is full of ladies and gentlemen."

"How wonderful! I hope I'll go abroad someday, but I'd rather go to Rome than ride in the park," said Amy, having no idea what riding path Grace meant and being too proud to ask.

Frank, sitting just behind the little girls, heard what they were saying and pushed his crutch away impatiently as he watched the active boys doing all kinds of energetic exercises. Beth, who was picking up the scattered card game pieces, looked up and said in her quiet but friendly way, "I'm afraid you're tired. Can I do anything for you?"

"Talk to me, please. It's dull sitting by myself," answered Frank, who was clearly used to being well looked after at home.

If he had asked her to give a speech in Latin, it would not have seemed a more impossible request to shy Beth, but there was nowhere to run, no Jo to hide behind now, and the poor boy looked at her so hopefully that she bravely decided to try.

"What do you like to talk about?" she asked, nervously shuffling the cards and dropping half of them as she tried to tie them up.

"Well, I like to hear about cricket and rowing and hunting," said Frank, who hadn't yet learned to choose activities that matched his limited physical abilities.

Oh goodness! What will I do? I don't know anything about any of those, thought Beth, and forgetting the boy's difficulties in her nervousness, she said, hoping to get him talking, "I've never seen any hunting, but I suppose you know all about it."

"I did once, but I can never hunt again, because I got hurt jumping over a high gate, so there are no more horses and hunting dogs for me," said Frank with a sigh that made Beth hate herself for her innocent mistake.

"Your deer are much prettier than our ugly buffaloes," she said, turning to the subject of the American plains for help, and glad she had read some of the adventure books that Jo loved.

Buffaloes turned out to be a good and interesting topic, and in her eagerness to entertain another person, Beth forgot herself and was completely unaware of her sisters' surprise and delight at the unusual sight of Beth chatting away with one of the 'dreadful boys' she had asked to be protected from.

"Look at her! She feels sorry for him, so she's being kind to him," said Jo happily, watching from the croquet area.

"I always said she was a little saint," added Meg, as if this proved it beyond any doubt.

"I haven't heard Frank laugh so much in ages," said Grace to Amy, as they sat talking about dolls and making tiny tea sets from acorn cups.

"My sister Beth is a very fascinating girl when she wants to be," said Amy, pleased with Beth's success. She meant 'fascinating', but since Grace didn't know the exact meaning of either word, 'fascinating' sounded impressive and made a good impression.

A spontaneous little circus performance, games, and a friendly game of croquet finished the afternoon. At sunset, the tent was taken down, the hampers were packed, the wickets were pulled up, the boats were loaded, and the whole group floated down the river, singing as loudly as they could. Ned, feeling sentimental, sang a song with the sad refrain:

Alone, alone, ah! How sad, alone,

and at the lines:

We each are young, we each have a heart,
Oh, why should we stand thus coldly apart?

he looked at Meg with such a dramatic expression that she laughed out loud and ruined his song.

"How can you be so cruel to me?" he whispered, under cover of a lively chorus. "You've stayed close to that stiff English woman all day, and now you're rejecting me."

"I didn't mean to, but you looked so funny I really couldn't help it," replied Meg, not responding to the first part of his complaint, because it was quite true that she had avoided him, remembering the Moffat party and the gossip that followed.

Ned was offended and turned to Sallie for comfort, saying to her rather sulkily, "There's not the slightest bit of flirtatiousness in that girl, is there?"

"Not at all, but she's a dear person," replied Sallie, defending her friend even while acknowledging her faults.

"She's not easily impressed, anyway," said Ned, trying to be clever with about as much success as most very young men have.

On the lawn where it had gathered, the group said warm good nights and goodbyes, because the Vaughns were going to Canada. As the four sisters walked home through the garden, Miss Kate watched them and said, without the superior tone in her voice, "In spite of their expressive manners, American girls are very nice when you get to know them."

"I completely agree with you," said Mr. Brooke.
