

  Jo was the first to wake in the gray light of Christmas morning. No stockings hung at the fireplace, and for a moment she felt as disappointed as she did long ago, when her little sock fell down because it was stuffed so full of goodies. Then she remembered her mother's promise and, slipping her hand under her pillow, pulled out a little red-covered book. She knew it very well, because it was that beautiful old story of the best life ever lived, and Jo felt that it was a true guidebook for anyone going on a long journey. She woke Meg with a "Merry Christmas," and told her to see what was under her pillow. A green-covered book appeared, with the same picture inside, and a few words written by their mother, which made their one present very precious in their eyes. Soon Beth and Amy woke up to search and find their little books also, one gray-colored, the other blue, and all sat looking at them and talking about them, while the east grew rosy with the coming day.

In spite of her small vanities, Margaret had a sweet and gentle nature, which quietly influenced her sisters, especially Jo, who loved her very much, and listened to her because her advice was given so gently.

"Girls," said Meg seriously, looking from the untidy head beside her to the two little night-capped ones in the room beyond, "Mother wants us to read and love and follow these books, and we must begin right away. We used to be faithful about it, but since Father went away and all this war trouble upset us, we have neglected many things. You can do as you please, but I shall keep my book on the table here and read a little every morning as soon as I wake, because I know it will do me good and help me through the day."

Then she opened her new book and began to read. Jo put her arm around her and, leaning cheek to cheek, read also, with the quiet expression so rarely seen on her restless face.

"How good Meg is! Come, Amy, let's do as they do. I'll help you with the hard words, and they'll explain things if we don't understand," whispered Beth, very impressed by the pretty books and her sisters' example.

"I'm glad mine is blue," said Amy, and then the rooms were very quiet while the pages were softly turned, and the winter sunshine came in to touch the bright heads and serious faces with a Christmas greeting.

"Where is Mother?" asked Meg, as she and Jo ran down to thank her for their gifts, half an hour later.

"Goodness only knows. Some poor person came begging, and your mother went straight off to see what was needed. There never was such a woman for giving away food and drink, clothes and firewood," replied Hannah, who had lived with the family since Meg was born, and was considered by them all more as a friend than a servant.

"She will be back soon, I think, so fry your cakes, and have everything ready," said Meg, looking over the presents which were collected in a basket and kept under the sofa, ready to be brought out at the right time. "Where is Amy's bottle of cologne?" she added, as the little bottle did not appear.

"She took it out a minute ago, and went off with it to put a ribbon on it, or something like that," replied Jo, dancing around the room to break in her new army slippers.

"How nice my handkerchiefs look, don't they? Hannah washed and ironed them for me, and I marked them all myself," said Beth, looking proudly at the slightly uneven letters which had cost her so much effort.

"Good heavens! She's gone and put 'Mother' on them instead of 'M. March'. How funny!" cried Jo, picking one up.

"Isn't that right? I thought it was better to do it that way, because Meg's initials are M.M., and I don't want anyone to use these but Marmee," said Beth, looking troubled.

"It's perfectly fine, dear, and a very pretty idea, quite sensible too, because no one can ever make a mistake now. It will please her very much, I know," said Meg, with a frown for Jo and a smile for Beth.

"There's Mother. Hide the basket, quick!" cried Jo, as a door closed and steps were heard in the hall.

Amy came in quickly, and looked rather embarrassed when she saw her sisters all waiting for her.

"Where have you been, and what are you hiding behind you?" asked Meg, surprised to see, by her hood and coat, that lazy Amy had been out so early.

"Don't laugh at me, Jo! I didn't mean anyone to know until the time came. I only meant to exchange the little bottle for a big one, and I gave all my money to get it, and I'm truly trying not to be selfish anymore."

As she spoke, Amy showed the beautiful bottle which replaced the cheap one, and looked so serious and humble in her little effort to think of others that Meg hugged her right away, and Jo called her a good person, while Beth ran to the window and picked her finest rose to decorate the impressive bottle.

"You see I felt ashamed of my present, after reading and talking about being good this morning, so I ran around the corner and changed it the minute I was up, and I'm so glad, because mine is now the most handsome one."

Another bang of the street door sent the basket under the sofa, and the girls to the table, eager for breakfast.

"Merry Christmas, Marmee! Many of them! Thank you for our books. We read some, and plan to every day," they all cried together.

"Merry Christmas, little daughters! I'm glad you began right away, and I hope you will keep on. But I want to say one word before we sit down. Not far away from here lives a poor woman with a little newborn baby. Six children are crowded into one bed to keep from freezing, because they have no fire. There is nothing to eat over there, and the oldest boy came to tell me they were suffering from hunger and cold. My girls, will you give them your breakfast as a Christmas present?"

They were all unusually hungry, having waited almost an hour, and for a minute no one spoke. Only a minute, for Jo exclaimed enthusiastically, "I'm so glad you came before we began!"

"May I go and help carry the things to the poor little children?" asked Beth eagerly.

"I'll take the cream and the muffins," added Amy, bravely giving up the thing she liked best.

Meg was already covering the pancakes, and piling the bread onto one big plate.

"I thought you'd do it," said Mrs. March, smiling as if satisfied. "You shall all go and help me, and when we come back we will have bread and milk for breakfast, and make up for it at dinner."

They were soon ready, and the group set out. Fortunately it was early, and they went through back streets, so few people saw them, and no one laughed at the unusual party.

A poor, bare, miserable room it was, with broken windows, no fire, torn bedclothes, a sick mother, a crying baby, and a group of pale, hungry children huddled under one old blanket, trying to keep warm.

How the big eyes stared and the pale lips smiled as the girls came in.

"Oh, my God! It is good angels come to us!" said the poor woman, crying for joy.

"Funny angels in hoods and mittens," said Jo, and made them all laugh.

In a few minutes it really did seem as if kind helpers had been at work there. Hannah, who had carried wood, made a fire, and covered the broken windows with old hats and her own coat. Mrs. March gave the mother tea and warm food, and comforted her with promises of help, while she dressed the little baby as tenderly as if it had been her own. The girls meanwhile set the table, sat the children around the fire, and fed them like so many hungry birds, laughing, talking, and trying to understand the funny broken English.

"Das ist gut!" "Die Engel-kinder!" cried the poor things as they ate and warmed their purple hands at the comfortable fire. The girls had never been called angel children before, and thought it was very nice, especially Jo, who had been considered rough ever since she was born. That was a very happy breakfast, though they didn't eat any of it. And when they went away, leaving comfort behind, I think there were not in all the city four happier people than the hungry little girls who gave away their breakfasts and were satisfied with bread and milk on Christmas morning.

"That's loving our neighbor better than ourselves, and I like it," said Meg, as they set out their presents while their mother was upstairs collecting clothes for the poor Hummel family.

Not a very impressive show, but there was a great deal of love wrapped up in the few little bundles, and the tall vase of red roses, white chrysanthemums, and trailing vines, which stood in the middle, gave quite an elegant air to the table.

"She's coming! Play something, Beth! Open the door, Amy! Three cheers for Marmee!" cried Jo, jumping about while Meg went to bring Mother to the seat of honor.

Beth played her happiest march, Amy threw open the door, and Meg acted as escort with great dignity. Mrs. March was both surprised and touched, and smiled with her eyes full of tears as she looked at her presents and read the little notes which came with them. The slippers went on immediately, a new handkerchief was put in her pocket, smelling nicely of Amy's cologne, the rose was pinned to her dress, and the nice gloves were declared a perfect fit.

There was a good deal of laughing and kissing and explaining, in the simple, loving way which makes these home celebrations so pleasant at the time and so sweet to remember long afterward, and then all fell to work.

The morning's generosity and celebrations took so much time that the rest of the day was devoted to getting ready for the evening's entertainment. Being still too young to go to the theater often, and not rich enough to spend much money on private performances, the girls used their creativity, and with necessity being the mother of invention, made whatever they needed. Some of their creations were very clever—cardboard guitars, old-fashioned lamps made from old butter dishes covered with silver paper, grand robes made from old cotton, glittering with tin pieces from a pickle factory, and armor covered with the same useful diamond-shaped bits left over when the lids of preserve pots were cut out. The big room upstairs was the scene of many innocent entertainments.

No men were allowed, so Jo played male parts to her heart's content and took great pleasure in a pair of reddish-brown leather boots given her by a friend, who knew a lady who knew an actor. These boots, an old sword, and a cut-style jacket once used by an artist for some picture, were Jo's greatest treasures and appeared on all occasions. The small size of the group made it necessary for the two main actors to play several parts each, and they certainly deserved credit for the hard work they did in learning three or four different parts, quickly changing in and out of various costumes, and managing the stage as well. It was excellent practice for their memories, a harmless entertainment, and used many hours which otherwise would have been idle, lonely, or spent in less worthwhile activities.

On Christmas night, a dozen girls crowded onto the bed which served as the front row, and sat before the blue and yellow curtains in a very excited state of anticipation. There was a good deal of noise and whispering behind the curtain, a little lamp smoke, and an occasional giggle from Amy, who tended to get overexcited in the excitement of the moment. Presently a bell rang, the curtains flew open, and the dramatic tragedy began.

"A dark wood," according to the one show program, was represented by a few potted plants, green cloth on the floor, and a cave in the distance. This cave was made with a clothes rack for a roof, dressers for walls, and in it was a small furnace going full blast, with a black pot on it and an old witch bending over it. The stage was dark and the glow of the furnace had a great effect, especially as real steam came from the pot when the witch took off the cover. A moment was allowed for the first excitement to calm, then Hugo, the villain, came in with a clanking sword at his side, a large hat, black beard, mysterious coat, and the boots. After pacing back and forth in great agitation, he struck his forehead and burst out in a wild song, singing of his hatred for Roderigo, his love for Zara, and his pleasant plan to kill one and win the other. The rough tones of Hugo's voice, with an occasional shout when his feelings overcame him, were very impressive, and the audience applauded the moment he paused for breath. Bowing like someone used to public praise, he went to the cave and ordered Hagar to come out with a commanding, "What ho, minion! I need thee!"

Out came Meg, with gray false hair hanging about her face, a red and black dress, a staff, and magical symbols on her coat. Hugo demanded a potion to make Zara adore him, and one to destroy Roderigo. Hagar, in a fine dramatic song, promised both, and proceeded to call up the spirit who would bring the love potion.

Hither, hither, from thy home,
Airy sprite, I bid thee come!
Born of roses, fed on dew,
Charms and potions canst thou brew?
Bring me here, with elfin speed,
The fragrant philter which I need.
Make it sweet and swift and strong,
Spirit, answer now my song!

A soft strain of music sounded, and then at the back of the cave appeared a little figure in cloudy white, with glittering wings, golden hair, and a garland of roses on its head. Waving a wand, it sang...

Hither I come,
From my airy home,
Afar in the silver moon.
Take the magic spell,
And use it well,
Or its power will vanish soon!

And dropping a small, golden bottle at the witch's feet, the spirit disappeared. Another chant from Hagar produced another apparition, not a lovely one, for with a bang an ugly black creature appeared and, having croaked a reply, threw a dark bottle at Hugo and disappeared with a mocking laugh. Having sung his thanks and put the potions in his boots, Hugo left, and Hagar told the audience that as he had killed a few of her friends in the past, she had cursed him, and planned to ruin his plans and get revenge on him. Then the curtain fell, and the audience rested and ate candy while discussing the merits of the play.

A good deal of hammering went on before the curtain rose again, but when it was clear what a masterpiece of stage construction had been built, no one complained about the delay. It was truly superb. A tower rose to the ceiling, halfway up appeared a window with a lamp burning in it, and behind the white curtain appeared Zara in a lovely blue and silver dress, waiting for Roderigo. He came in magnificent costume, with a feathered cap, red coat, curly hair, a guitar, and the boots, of course. Kneeling at the foot of the tower, he sang a song in sweet tones. Zara replied and, after a musical conversation, agreed to run away. Then came the great moment of the play. Roderigo brought out a rope ladder with five steps, threw up one end, and invited Zara to come down. Carefully she climbed from her window, put her hand on Roderigo's shoulder, and was about to jump gracefully down when "Oh no! Poor Zara!" she forgot her long skirt. It caught in the window, the tower wobbled, leaned forward, and fell with a crash, burying the unfortunate lovers in the ruins.

Everyone screamed as the reddish-brown boots waved wildly from the wreck and a golden head appeared, saying, "I told you so! I told you so!" With great presence of mind, Don Pedro, the harsh father, rushed in, pulled out his daughter, saying quietly,

"Don't laugh! Act as if it was all right!" and, ordering Roderigo up, sent him away from the kingdom with anger and scorn. Though clearly shaken by the tower falling on him, Roderigo defied the old man and refused to move. This brave example inspired Zara. She also defied her father, and he ordered them both to the deepest dungeons of the castle. A small servant came in with chains and led them away, looking very frightened and clearly forgetting the speech he was supposed to make.

Act three was the castle hall, and here Hagar appeared, having come to free the lovers and finish Hugo. She hears him coming and hides, watches him put the potions into two cups of wine and tell the nervous little servant, "Carry them to the prisoners in their cells, and tell them I shall come soon." The servant takes Hugo aside to tell him something, and Hagar changes the cups for two others which are harmless. Ferdinando, the 'minion', carries them away, and Hagar puts back the cup which holds the poison meant for Roderigo. Hugo, becoming thirsty after a long song, drinks it, loses his mind, and after a great deal of grabbing and stamping, falls flat and dies, while Hagar tells him what she has done in a song of great power and beauty.

This was a truly exciting scene, though some people might have thought that the sudden falling down of a large amount of long red hair rather ruined the effect of the villain's death. He was called before the curtain, and very fittingly appeared, leading Hagar, whose singing was considered more wonderful than all the rest of the performance put together.

Act four showed the despairing Roderigo about to stab himself because he has been told that Zara has left him. Just as the knife is at his heart, a lovely song is sung under his window, telling him that Zara is faithful but in danger, and he can save her if he acts. A key is thrown in, which unlocks the door, and in a burst of joy he tears off his chains and rushes away to find and rescue his beloved.

Act five opened with a stormy argument between Zara and Don Pedro. He wants her to go into a convent, but she refuses, and after a touching appeal, is about to faint when Roderigo rushes in and demands her hand. Don Pedro refuses, because he is not rich. They shout and gesture tremendously but cannot agree, and Roderigo is about to carry away the exhausted Zara, when the nervous servant enters with a letter and a bag from Hagar, who has mysteriously disappeared. The letter informs the group that she leaves great wealth to the young couple and a terrible fate to Don Pedro, if he doesn't make them happy. The bag is opened, and many fake coins shower down on the stage until it glitters with the shine. This completely softens the harsh father. He agrees without complaint, everyone joins in a joyful song, and the curtain falls on the lovers kneeling to receive Don Pedro's blessing in the most romantic poses.

Loud applause followed, but was given an unexpected interruption, for the folding bed on which the audience sat suddenly folded up and trapped the enthusiastic watchers. Roderigo and Don Pedro ran to the rescue, and all were helped out unhurt, though many could not speak from laughing. The excitement had barely calmed down when Hannah appeared, saying, "Mrs. March's compliments, and would the ladies come down to supper."

This was a surprise even to the actors, and when they saw the table, they looked at one another in amazed delight. It was so like Marmee to prepare a little treat for them, but anything so wonderful had not been seen since the old days of plenty. There was ice cream—actually two dishes of it, pink and white—and cake and fruit and delightful French sweets and, in the middle of the table, four large bouquets of greenhouse flowers.

It quite took their breath away, and they stared first at the table and then at their mother, who looked as if she enjoyed it very much.

"Is it fairies?" asked Amy.

"Santa Claus," said Beth.

"Mother did it." And Meg smiled her sweetest, despite her gray beard and white eyebrows.

"Aunt March had a kind moment and sent the supper," cried Jo, with a sudden thought.

"All wrong. Old Mr. Laurence sent it," replied Mrs. March.

"The Laurence boy's grandfather! What in the world made him think of it? We don't know him!" exclaimed Meg.

"Hannah told one of his servants about your breakfast gift. He is an unusual old gentleman, but that pleased him. He knew my father years ago, and he sent me a polite note this afternoon, saying he hoped I would allow him to show his friendly feeling toward my children by sending them a few things in honor of the day. I couldn't refuse, and so you have a little feast at night to make up for the bread-and-milk breakfast."

"That boy gave him the idea, I know he did! He's a wonderful fellow, and I wish we could get to know him. He looks as if he'd like to know us but he's shy, and Meg is so proper she won't let me speak to him when we pass," said Jo, as the plates went around, and the ice cream began to disappear with sighs of satisfaction.

"You mean the people who live in the big house next door, don't you?" asked one of the girls. "My mother knows old Mr. Laurence, but says he's very proud and doesn't like to mix with his neighbors. He keeps his grandson home, when he isn't riding or walking with his teacher, and makes him study very hard. We invited him to our party, but he didn't come. Mother says he's very nice, though he never speaks to us girls."

"Our cat ran away once, and he brought her back, and we talked over the fence, and were getting on wonderfully, all about cricket, and so on, when he saw Meg coming, and walked away. I intend to get to know him someday, because he needs fun, I'm sure he does," said Jo firmly.

"I like his manners, and he looks like a little gentleman, so I have no objection to your knowing him, if a proper opportunity comes. He brought the flowers himself, and I should have asked him in, if I had been sure what was going on upstairs. He looked so wistful as he went away, hearing the fun and clearly not having any of his own."

"It's a good thing you didn't, Mother!" laughed Jo, looking at her boots. "But we'll have another play sometime that he can see. Maybe he'll help act. Wouldn't that be wonderful?"

"I never had such a fine bouquet before! How pretty it is!" And Meg examined her flowers with great interest.

"They are lovely. But Beth's roses are sweeter to me," said Mrs. March, smelling the half-dead bunch of flowers in her dress.

Beth moved closer to her, and whispered softly, "I wish I could send my bunch to Father. I'm afraid he isn't having such a merry Christmas as we are."
