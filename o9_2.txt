

 "Well," muttered Passepartout, slightly flustered, "I've seen wax figures at Madame Tussaud's that were livelier than my new master!"

Madame Tussaud's "people," it should be said, are made of wax and are much visited in London. The only thing missing to make them human is the ability to speak.

During his short meeting with Mr. Fogg, Passepartout had been carefully watching him. He appeared to be a man of about forty years of age, with fine, attractive features and a tall, well-shaped figure. His hair and side whiskers were light-coloured, his forehead smooth and unwrinkled, his face rather pale, and his teeth excellent. His face had, to the highest degree, what experts in reading faces call "calm in action" — a quality of people who act rather than talk. Calm and composed, with clear eyes, Mr. Fogg seemed a perfect example of that English self-control that the painter Angelica Kauffmann showed so well on canvas. Seen during the various moments of his daily life, he gave the impression of being perfectly balanced, as precisely regular as a Leroy precision clock. Phileas Fogg was, in fact, the very image of exactness, and this showed even in the way he moved his hands and feet. For in men, as in animals, even the limbs show what a person is feeling.

He was so precise that he was never in a hurry, was always ready, and was careful about every step and every movement. He never took one unnecessary step, and always went to his destination by the shortest way. He made no unnecessary gestures, and was never seen to be moved or upset. He was the most careful and deliberate person in the world, yet he always arrived exactly on time.

He lived alone and, in a way, outside of all social connection. He knew that in this world one must account for friction, and that friction causes delay, so he never rubbed anyone the wrong way.

As for Passepartout, he was a true Parisian from Paris. Since he had left his own country for England to work as a servant, he had searched without success for a master who suited him. Passepartout was not at all one of those rude, arrogant servants shown in Molière's plays, with a bold look and a nose held high. He was an honest fellow with a pleasant face, lips a little pushed forward, gentle in manner and willing to help, with a good round head of the kind you like to see on the shoulders of a friend. His eyes were blue, his face was red-cheeked, his figure was almost stocky and well-built, his body was muscular, and his physical strength was well developed from the exercises of his younger years. His brown hair was a little untidy. While the ancient sculptors are said to have known eighteen ways of arranging the goddess Minerva's hair, Passepartout knew only one way of doing his own: three strokes of a wide-tooth comb was all his grooming required.

It was difficult to predict how Passepartout's lively nature would get along with Mr. Fogg. It was impossible to tell whether the new servant would turn out to be as perfectly organised as his master required. Only experience could answer that question. Passepartout had lived a sort of wandering life in his early years, and now wanted peace and quiet. But so far he had failed to find it, even after working in ten English households. He could not settle in any of them. With frustration, he found his employers were always unpredictable and irregular, constantly travelling around the country or looking for adventure. His last employer, young Lord Longferry, a Member of Parliament, after spending his nights in the taverns of the Haymarket, was too often carried home in the early morning on policemen's shoulders. Passepartout, wanting to respect the gentleman he worked for, gently complained about such behaviour. This was not well received, so he left. When he heard that Mr. Phileas Fogg was looking for a servant, and that his life was one of complete regularity, that he neither travelled nor stayed away from home overnight, Passepartout felt sure this was the position he was looking for. He applied and was accepted, as we have seen.

At half past eleven, then, Passepartout found himself alone in the house in Saville Row. He began to look around it without delay, going through it from the basement to the roof. The clean, well-organised, and quiet house pleased him. It felt to him like a snail's shell, lit and heated by gas, which was enough for both purposes. When Passepartout reached the second floor he immediately recognised the room he was to live in, and he was quite happy with it. Electric bells and speaking tubes allowed communication with the lower floors. On the mantelpiece stood an electric clock, exactly like the one in Mr. Fogg's bedroom, both ticking at the same second at the same moment. "That's good, that'll do," said Passepartout to himself.

He suddenly noticed, hanging above the clock, a card which, when he looked at it closely, turned out to be a programme of the house's daily routine. It included everything that was required of the servant, from eight in the morning — the exact hour at which Phileas Fogg got up — until half past eleven, when he left the house for the Reform Club. All the details of the service were listed: tea and toast at twenty-three minutes past eight, shaving water at thirty-seven minutes past nine, and getting dressed at twenty minutes before ten. Everything was organised and planned, from half past eleven in the morning until midnight, the hour at which this orderly gentleman went to bed.

Mr. Fogg's wardrobe was well supplied and in excellent taste. Each pair of trousers, each coat and waistcoat had a number on it, showing the time of year and season when they were to be worn in turn. The same system applied to the master's shoes. In short, the house in Saville Row, which must have been a place of great disorder and confusion under the famous but wild Sheridan, had become the perfect idea of comfort, ease, and order. There was no study, nor were there books, which would have been quite useless to Mr. Fogg, since at the Reform Club two libraries — one for general literature and one for law and politics — were available to him. In his bedroom stood a medium-sized safe, built to resist both fire and burglars. But Passepartout found neither weapons nor hunting equipment anywhere. Everything showed the most calm and peaceful way of life.

Having examined the house from top to bottom, he rubbed his hands together, a broad smile spread across his face, and he said happily, "This is exactly what I wanted! Ah, Mr. Fogg and I will get along well together! What a home-loving and regular gentleman! A real machine — well, I don't mind working for a machine."
