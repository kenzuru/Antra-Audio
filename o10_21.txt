

  Jo's face showed something strange the next day, because the secret was heavy on her, and she found it difficult not to look as if she knew something important. Meg noticed it, but did not try to ask questions, because she had learned that the best way to deal with Jo was to do the opposite of what she expected, so she was sure Jo would tell her everything if she did not ask. She was quite surprised, then, when Jo stayed quiet and acted as if she knew something special, which made Meg annoyed. Meg then acted calm and serious, and spent her time with her mother. This left Jo alone, because Mrs. March had taken her place as nurse, and told Jo to rest, go outside, and enjoy herself after being inside for so long. Amy was away, so Laurie was her only company, and even though she liked being with him, she was a little afraid of him just then, because he loved to tease, and she worried he would get the secret out of her.

She was right, because the playful boy started looking for the mystery as soon as he thought there was one, and made life very difficult for Jo. He tried kindness, bribery, teasing, and threats. He acted like he did not care, so he could catch her off guard. He said he already knew, then said he did not care. Finally, after trying very hard, he figured out that the secret was about Meg and Mr. Brooke. He was angry that his teacher had not trusted him with the secret, so he decided to get back at him in some way.

Meg, meanwhile, seemed to have forgotten the whole thing and was busy getting ready for her father's return. But then, suddenly, something seemed to change in her, and for a day or two, she was not like herself at all. She jumped when someone spoke to her, went red when someone looked at her, was very quiet, and sat over her sewing with a nervous, worried look on her face. She told her mother she was fine, and she asked Jo to leave her alone.

"She can feel it in the air — love, I mean — and she is falling for it fast. She has most of the signs — she is nervous and irritable, she does not eat, she lies awake, and she stays by herself in corners. I caught her singing that song he gave her, and once she said 'John', just like you do, and then went as red as a flower. What should we do?" said Jo, looking ready for any action, even a strong one.

"Nothing but wait. Leave her alone, be kind and patient, and Father's coming will fix everything," answered her mother.

"Here's a letter for you, Meg, all sealed up. How strange! Teddy never seals mine," said Jo the next day, as she gave out the letters from the little post box.

Mrs. March and Jo were busy with their own things, when a sound from Meg made them look up and see her staring at her letter with a frightened face.

"My child, what is wrong?" cried her mother, running to her, while Jo tried to take the paper that had caused the trouble.

"It's all a mistake — he didn't send it. Oh, Jo, how could you do this?" and Meg covered her face with her hands, crying as if her heart was broken.

"Me! I have done nothing! What is she talking about?" cried Jo, confused.

Meg's gentle eyes filled with anger as she pulled a folded letter from her pocket and threw it at Jo, saying in an upset voice, "You wrote it, and that bad boy helped you. How could you be so rude, so mean, and so cruel to both of us?"

Jo hardly listened, because she and her mother were reading the letter, which was written in a strange handwriting.

"My Dearest Margaret,

"I can no longer hold back my feelings, and I must know what you think before I return. I do not dare tell your parents yet, but I think they would agree if they knew that we love each other. Mr. Laurence will help me find a good position, and then, my dear girl, you will make me happy. I beg you to say nothing to your family yet, but to send one word of hope through Laurie to,

"Your devoted John."

"Oh, the little troublemaker! That is how he planned to get back at me for keeping my promise to Mother. I will scold him well and bring him over to apologize," cried Jo, wanting to act right away. But her mother held her back, saying with a serious look she rarely wore,

"Wait, Jo, you must first show that you were not part of this. You have played so many tricks that I am worried you had a hand in this one."

"I promise, Mother, I didn't! I never saw that letter before, and I know nothing about it, honestly!" said Jo, so seriously that they believed her. "If I had been part of it, I would have done it better than this, and written a letter that made sense. I would think you would know that Mr. Brooke would not write something so foolish," she added, throwing the paper down with scorn.

"It looks like his handwriting," said Meg quietly, comparing it with the note in her hand.

"Oh, Meg, did you write back?" cried Mrs. March quickly.

"Yes, I did!" and Meg covered her face again, full of shame.

"What a mess! Let me bring that terrible boy over to explain and be told off. I cannot rest until I get hold of him." And Jo moved toward the door again.

"Wait! Let me deal with this, for it is worse than I thought. Margaret, tell me everything," said Mrs. March firmly, sitting down next to Meg, but holding onto Jo so she could not run off.

"I got the first letter from Laurie, who did not look like he knew anything about it," began Meg, without looking up. "I was worried at first and wanted to tell you, but then I remembered how much you like Mr. Brooke, so I thought you would not mind if I kept my small secret for a few days. I am so silly that I liked thinking no one knew, and while I was deciding what to say, I felt like the girls in books, who have such things happen to them. Forgive me, Mother, I am being punished for my silliness now. I can never look him in the face again."

"What did you write to him?" asked Mrs. March.

"I only said I was too young to do anything about it yet, that I did not want to have secrets from you, and that he should speak to Father. I said I was very grateful for his kindness, and that I would be his friend, but nothing more, for a long time."

Mrs. March smiled, as if she was pleased, and Jo clapped her hands, saying with a laugh, "You are almost as wise as Caroline Percy, who was known for being careful! Go on, Meg. What did he say to that?"

"He wrote back in a completely different way, telling me that he never sent any love letter at all, and that he is very sorry that my troublesome sister, Jo, used our names like that. It is very kind and respectful, but think how awful for me!"

Meg leaned against her mother, looking completely sad, and Jo walked around the room, saying angry things about Laurie. Then she stopped suddenly, picked up the two letters, and after looking at them carefully, said firmly, "I do not think Brooke ever saw either of these letters. Teddy wrote both, and he is keeping yours to use against me because I would not tell him my secret."

"Do not keep secrets, Jo. Tell Mother and stay out of trouble, as I should have done," said Meg with a warning.

"Oh, child! Mother already told me."

"That is enough, Jo. I will comfort Meg while you go and get Laurie. I am going to find out exactly what happened, and stop these tricks immediately."

Jo ran off, and Mrs. March gently told Meg what Mr. Brooke truly felt. "Now, dear, how do you feel? Do you love him enough to wait until he can make a home for you, or would you rather stay completely free for now?"

"I have been so frightened and worried, I do not want anything to do with people who like me romantically for a long time, perhaps never," answered Meg with irritation. "If John knows nothing about this nonsense, do not tell him, and make Jo and Laurie keep quiet. I will not be tricked and bothered and made to look foolish. It is not fair!"

Seeing that Meg's usually gentle temper had been hurt and her pride damaged by this mean joke, Mrs. March calmed her with promises to stay completely silent and to be very careful in the future. The moment Laurie's footsteps were heard in the hall, Meg ran into the study, and Mrs. March met the guilty boy alone. Jo had not told him why he was wanted, worried he would not come, but he knew the moment he saw Mrs. March's face, and stood turning his hat in his hands with a guilty look that showed he was responsible. Jo was asked to leave, but she chose to walk back and forth in the hall like a guard, a little afraid that the prisoner might escape. The sound of voices in the sitting room went up and down for half an hour, but the girls never found out what was said during that meeting.

When they were called in, Laurie was standing next to their mother with such a sorry face that Jo forgave him right away, but decided it was better not to show it. Meg accepted his humble apology, and felt much better when she learned that Brooke knew nothing about the joke.

"I will never tell him for as long as I live, nothing in the world will make me tell, so please forgive me, Meg, and I will do anything to show how truly sorry I am," he added, looking very ashamed of himself.

"I will try, but it was a very ungentlemanly thing to do. I did not think you could be so sneaky and mean, Laurie," replied Meg, trying to hide her embarrassment behind a serious and disapproving expression.

"It was completely awful, and I do not deserve to be spoken to for a month, but you will, though, won't you?" And Laurie put his hands together with such a begging gesture, speaking in his most persuasive voice, that it was impossible to stay angry with him, even after his terrible behavior.

Meg forgave him, and Mrs. March's serious face relaxed, even though she tried to stay stern, when she heard him promise to make up for what he had done in all kinds of ways, and behave humbly before the person he had hurt.

Jo stood apart from all of this, trying to stay angry with him, and only managing to make her face look very disapproving. Laurie looked at her once or twice, but since she showed no sign of softening, he felt hurt, and turned his back to her until the others were finished with him. Then he gave her a low bow and walked away without a word.

As soon as he had gone, she wished she had been kinder, and when Meg and her mother went upstairs, she felt lonely and missed Teddy. After holding herself back for a while, she gave in to the feeling, and carrying a book to return, went over to the big house.

"Is Mr. Laurence at home?" asked Jo, to a housemaid who was coming downstairs.

"Yes, miss, but I do not think he wants to see anyone just yet."

"Why not? Is he ill?"

"Oh no, miss, but he has had a difficult situation with Mr. Laurie, who is in one of his bad moods about something, which has upset the old gentleman, so I dare not go near him."

"Where is Laurie?"

"Shut in his room, and he will not answer, even though I have knocked. I do not know what to do with the dinner, because it is ready, and there is no one to eat it."

"I will go and see what is wrong. I am not afraid of either of them."

Up went Jo, and knocked firmly on the door of Laurie's small study.

"Stop that, or I will open the door and make you!" called out the young gentleman in an angry tone.

Jo knocked again immediately. The door flew open, and she went in before Laurie could recover from his surprise. Seeing that he really was in a bad mood, Jo, who knew how to handle him, put on a sorry expression, and going down on her knees, said gently, "Please forgive me for being so cold. I came to make things right, and I cannot leave until I have."

"It is fine. Get up, and do not be silly, Jo," was the relaxed reply to her request.

"Thank you, I will. May I ask what is wrong? You do not look very comfortable."

"I was grabbed, and I will not put up with it!" said Laurie angrily.

"Who did that?" asked Jo.

"Grandfather. If it had been anyone else I would have..." And the upset young man finished his sentence with a strong movement of his right arm.

"That is nothing. I grab you sometimes, and you do not mind," said Jo calmly.

"That is different! You are a girl, and it is fine, but no man will grab me!"

"I do not think anyone would want to try it, if you looked as angry as you do now. Why did that happen to you?"

"Just because I would not say what your mother wanted me for. I had promised not to tell, and of course I was not going to break my word."

"Could you not explain to your grandfather in another way?"

"No, he wanted the full truth, everything. I would have told him my part of the mess, if I could have done it without involving Meg. Since I could not, I said nothing and accepted the scolding until the old gentleman grabbed me. Then I ran, because I was afraid I might do something I would regret."

"It was not nice, but he is sorry, I know, so go down and make peace. I will help you."

"Absolutely not! I am not going to be lectured and shaken by everyone, just for a small bit of fun. I was sorry about Meg, and I apologized properly, but I will not do it again for something that was not my fault."

"He did not know that."

"He should trust me, and not treat me like a child. It is no use, Jo, he needs to learn that I can take care of myself, and do not need anyone watching over me."

"What difficult people you both are!" sighed Jo. "How do you plan to sort this out?"

"Well, he should apologize and believe me when I say I cannot tell him what the trouble was about."

"Really! He will not do that."

"I will not go down until he does."

"Now, Teddy, be sensible. Let it go, and I will explain what I can. You cannot stay up here, so what is the point of making things dramatic?"

"I do not plan to stay here long, anyway. I will slip away and travel somewhere, and when Grandfather misses me he will come around quickly enough."

"Maybe so, but you should not go and upset him."

"Do not lecture me. I will go to Washington and see Brooke. It is lively there, and I will enjoy myself after all this trouble."

"What fun that would be! I wish I could run away too," said Jo, forgetting her role as advisor at the thought of the exciting life at the capital.

"Come then! Why not? You can go and surprise your father, and I will go and visit old Brooke. It would be a wonderful joke. Let us do it, Jo. We will leave a letter saying we are safe, and leave immediately. I have enough money. It will be good for you, and not harmful, since you would be going to your father."

For a moment Jo looked as if she might agree, because wild as the plan was, it suited her perfectly. She was tired of worry and being stuck at home, she wanted a change, and thoughts of her father mixed temptingly with the exciting idea of camps and hospitals, freedom and fun. Her eyes brightened as they looked toward the window, but then they fell on the old house across the road, and she shook her head with a sad but firm decision.

"If I were a boy, we would run away together and have a wonderful time, but since I am a girl, I must be proper and stay home. Do not tempt me, Teddy, it is a crazy plan."

"That is what makes it fun," began Laurie, who had made up his mind to break free from the rules in some way.

"Stop talking!" cried Jo, covering her ears. "Being good and proper is my fate, and I may as well accept it. I came here to be sensible, not to hear things that make me want to run away."

"I know Meg would say no to such an idea, but I thought you had more spirit," began Laurie in a persuasive tone.

"You bad boy, be quiet! Sit down and think about your own mistakes, and do not help me add to mine. If I get your grandfather to apologize for the grabbing, will you give up the idea of running away?" asked Jo seriously.

"Yes, but you will not manage it," answered Laurie, who wanted to make things right, but felt that his hurt pride needed to be satisfied first.

"If I can manage the young one, I can manage the old one," said Jo quietly to herself, as she walked away, leaving Laurie bent over a train map with his head in both hands.

"Come in!" and Mr. Laurence's rough voice sounded rougher than ever, as Jo knocked at his door.

"It is only me, sir, I came to return a book," she said pleasantly, as she walked in.

"Want any more?" asked the old gentleman, looking serious and annoyed, but trying not to show it.

"Yes, please. I liked old Sam so much, I think I will try the second volume," answered Jo, hoping to please him by asking for more of Boswell's Johnson, which he had suggested as a good read.

The thick eyebrows relaxed a little as he moved the stepladder toward the shelf where the books were kept. Jo climbed up, and sitting at the top, pretended to be looking for her book, but was really thinking about how best to raise the difficult subject of her visit. Mr. Laurence seemed to guess that she had something on her mind, because after walking quickly around the room a few times, he turned to face her and spoke so suddenly that Rasselas tumbled face down to the floor.

"What has that boy been doing? Do not try to protect him. I know he has been causing trouble by the way he behaved when he came home. I cannot get a word from him, and when I threatened to shake the truth out of him he ran upstairs and locked himself in his room."

"He did something wrong, but we forgave him, and we all promised not to tell anyone," began Jo reluctantly.

"That will not do. He cannot hide behind a promise from you soft-hearted girls. If he has done something wrong, he must confess, apologize, and be punished. Tell me, Jo. I will not be kept in the dark."

Mr. Laurence looked so serious and spoke so sharply that Jo would have been happy to run away, if she could, but she was sitting up on the steps, and he stood at the bottom, blocking her way, so she had to stay and face it.

"Indeed, sir, I cannot tell. Mother said I must not. Laurie has confessed, apologized, and been punished enough. We are not staying quiet to protect him, but to protect someone else, and it will cause more trouble if you get involved. Please do not. It was partly my fault, but everything is fine now. So let us forget it, and talk about the Rambler or something pleasant."

"Forget the Rambler! Come down and give me your word that this troublesome boy of mine has not done anything disrespectful or ungrateful. If he has, after all your family's kindness to him, I will punish him myself."

The threat sounded scary, but did not worry Jo, because she knew the irritable old gentleman would never actually hurt his grandson, no matter what he said. She came down obediently, and made the trouble seem as small as she could without lying or getting Meg into more trouble.

"Hmm... well, if the boy stayed quiet because he had made a promise, and not because he was being difficult, I will forgive him. He is a stubborn boy and hard to manage," said Mr. Laurence, running his hand through his hair until it stood up, and smoothing the frown from his forehead with a look of relief.

"So am I, but a kind word works with me when nothing else would," said Jo, trying to put in a good word for her friend, who seemed to get out of one trouble only to fall into another.

"You think I am not kind to him, do you?" was the sharp reply.

"Oh, of course not, sir. You are sometimes too kind, and then just a little bit quick to get angry when he tests your patience. Do you not think that is true?"

Jo was determined to say what she had come to say, and tried to look calm, even though she was a little nervous after her brave speech. To her great relief and surprise, the old gentleman only threw his glasses onto the table with a clatter and said honestly, "You are right, girl, I am! I love the boy, but he tries my patience to the limit, and I know what will happen if we carry on like this."

"I will tell you — he will run away." Jo was sorry she said that the moment it came out. She had meant to warn him that Laurie would not put up with too much control, and she hoped he would be gentler with the boy.

Mr. Laurence's red face changed suddenly, and he sat down, with a troubled look at the picture of a handsome man that hung over his table. It was Laurie's father, who had run away when he was young, and married against the strong-willed old man's wishes. Jo thought he was remembering and regretting the past, and she wished she had said nothing.

"He will not do it unless he is very upset, and he only talks about it sometimes, when he gets tired of studying. I often think I would like to as well, especially since my hair was cut, so if you ever find us both missing, you can look for two boys on the ships heading for India."

She laughed as she spoke, and Mr. Laurence looked relieved, clearly taking the whole thing as a joke.

"You bold girl, how dare you talk like that? Where is your respect for me, and your good behavior? Those boys and girls! What trouble they are, yet we cannot do without them," he said, pinching her cheeks in a friendly way. "Go and bring that boy down to his dinner, tell him everything is fine, and tell him not to act so dramatically with his grandfather. I will not put up with it."

"He will not come, sir. He feels bad because you did not believe him when he said he could not tell you. I think being grabbed hurt his feelings a great deal."

Jo tried to look sad but must not have managed it, because Mr. Laurence started to laugh, and she knew she had won.

"I am sorry about that, and I suppose I should thank him for not grabbing me back. What on earth does the boy expect?" and the old gentleman looked a little ashamed of his own bad temper.

"If I were you, I would write him an apology, sir. He says he will not come down until he has one, and he talks about Washington, and carries on in a ridiculous way. A proper apology will help him see how silly he is being, and bring him downstairs in a much better mood. Try it. He likes humor, and this approach works better than talking. I will take it up, and teach him how to behave."

Mr. Laurence gave her a sharp look, and put on his glasses, saying slowly, "You are a clever girl, but I do not mind being handled by you and Beth. Here, give me a piece of paper, and let us finish with this nonsense."

The note was written in the way a gentleman would write to another after causing offense. Jo gave a quick kiss to the top of Mr. Laurence's bald head, and ran up to slip the apology under Laurie's door, telling him through the keyhole to be humble, well-behaved, and a few other things he would find difficult. Finding the door locked again, she left the note to do its work, and was quietly walking away, when the young gentleman slid down the banister, and waited for her at the bottom, saying, with his most innocent expression, "What a good friend you are, Jo! Did he shout at you?" he added, laughing.

"No, he was quite calm, on the whole."

"Oh! I got it from everyone. Even you were cold to me over there, and I felt ready to just give up," he began in an apologetic tone.

"Do not talk like that, make a fresh start and begin again, Teddy, my son."

"I keep making fresh starts, and ruining them, just like I used to ruin my practice books, and I make so many beginnings that there is never an end," he said sadly.

"Go and eat your dinner, you will feel better after it. People always complain when they are hungry," and Jo went out through the front door after that.

"That describes me perfectly," answered Laurie, quoting Amy, as he went to eat his dinner humbly with his grandfather, who was completely kind in mood and extremely polite in manner for the rest of the day.

Everyone thought the matter was finished and the small problem had passed, but the damage was done, because even though others forgot it, Meg remembered. She never talked about a certain person, but she thought of him a great deal, dreamed more than ever, and once Jo, searching her sister's desk for stamps, found a piece of paper covered in the words, 'Mrs. John Brooke', which made her groan sadly and throw it into the fire, feeling that Laurie's trick had brought the dreaded day closer.
