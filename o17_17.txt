BUT there was no happiness in the little town that same calm Saturday afternoon. The Harpers, and Aunt Polly's family, were dressing in black clothes, in great sadness and with many tears. An unusual quiet filled the village, although it was normally quiet enough in any case. The villagers went about their business in a distracted way, and talked little; but they sighed often. Saturday felt like a heavy burden to the children. They had no heart for their games, and slowly gave them up.

In the afternoon Becky Thatcher found herself sadly walking around the empty schoolyard, feeling very sad. But she found nothing there to comfort her. She talked to herself:

"Oh, if I only had a brass fireplace tool handle again! But I have nothing now to remember him by." And she held back a little sob.

Presently she stopped, and said to herself:

"It was right here. Oh, if it was to do over again, I wouldn't say that -- I wouldn't say it for the whole world. But he's gone now; I'll never, never, never see him any more."

This thought broke her down, and she wandered away, with tears rolling down her cheeks. Then quite a group of boys and girls -- friends of Tom's and Joe's -- came by, and stood looking over the wooden fence and talking in quiet, respectful voices about how Tom did so-and-so the last time they saw him, and how Joe said this and that small unimportant thing (which seemed so important now, they could easily see!) -- and each speaker pointed out the exact spot where the lost boys stood at the time, and then added something like "and I was standing just so -- just as I am now, and as if you were him -- I was as close as that -- and he smiled, just this way -- and then something seemed to go all over me, like -- awful, you know -- and I never thought what it meant, of course, but I can see now!"

Then there was a disagreement about who saw the dead boys last while they were still alive, and many claimed that sad and important honor, and offered proof, more or less changed by memory; and when it was finally settled who DID see the missing ones last, and exchanged the last words with them, the lucky people took on a kind of special importance, and were looked at with wide eyes and envied by all the rest. One poor boy, who had no other greatness to offer, said with obvious pride:

"Well, Tom Sawyer hit me once."

But that attempt at fame was a failure. Most of the boys could say that, and so it made the distinction less special. The group moved slowly away, still talking about the lost heroes in quiet, awed voices.

When Sunday school was finished the next morning, the bell began to ring slowly and sadly, instead of ringing in its usual way. It was a very still Sunday, and the sad sound seemed right for the quiet that lay over everything. The villagers began to gather, waiting a moment at the entrance to talk quietly about the sad event. But there was no whispering in the church; only the quiet sound of dresses as the women went to their seats broke the silence there. No one could remember when the little church had been so full before. There was a waiting pause, an expectant quiet, and then Aunt Polly entered, followed by Sid and Mary, and they by the Harper family, all in dark black, and the whole congregation, including the old minister, rose with respect and stood until the mourners were seated in the front row. There was another shared silence, broken at times by muffled sobs, and then the minister spread his hands and prayed. A moving hymn was sung, and the reading that followed was: "I am the Resurrection and the Life."

As the service went on, the minister described the good qualities, the charming ways, and the great promise of the lost boys so vividly that every person there, feeling they recognized these descriptions, felt a sharp pain of regret at remembering that they had always before ignored these qualities, and had always seen only faults and flaws in the poor boys. The minister also told many touching stories from the lives of the missing ones, which showed their sweet, generous natures, and the people could now easily see how noble and beautiful those events had been, and remembered with sadness that at the time they happened, they had seemed like terrible behavior, well deserving of punishment. The congregation became more and more emotional as the sad tale went on, until at last the whole group broke down and joined the weeping mourners in a chorus of painful sobs, and the minister himself gave way to his feelings, and cried at the front of the church.

There was a rustling in the upper seating area, which nobody noticed; a moment later the church door creaked; the minister raised his tearful eyes above his handkerchief, and stood frozen! First one and then another pair of eyes followed the minister's, and then almost all at once the congregation rose and stared while the three dead boys came walking up the aisle, Tom in the lead, Joe next, and Huck, in a mess of old rags, sneaking in with shame at the back! They had been hidden in the unused upper area, listening to their own funeral sermon!

Aunt Polly, Mary, and the Harpers threw themselves upon their returned loved ones, covered them with kisses and gave thanks to God, while poor Huck stood embarrassed and uncomfortable, not knowing exactly what to do or where to hide from so many unfriendly eyes. He hesitated, and started to slip away, but Tom grabbed him and said:

"Aunt Polly, it isn't fair. Somebody's got to be glad to see Huck."

"And so they shall. I'm glad to see him, poor motherless thing!" And the loving care Aunt Polly gave him was the one thing that could make him feel even more uncomfortable than he already was.

Suddenly the minister shouted at the top of his voice: "Praise God from whom all blessings flow -- SING! -- and put your hearts in it!"

And they did. The old hymn swelled up with a powerful sound, and while it shook the ceiling beams Tom Sawyer the Pirate looked around at the other envious children near him and admitted to himself that this was the proudest moment of his life.

As the congregation marched out they said they would almost be willing to be embarrassed again to hear that hymn sung like that once more.

Tom received more hugs and kisses that day -- depending on Aunt Polly's changing moods -- than he had earned in a whole year; and he hardly knew which expressed the most gratitude to God and love for himself.
