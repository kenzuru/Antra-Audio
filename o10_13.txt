

  Laurie was lying comfortably swinging back and forth in his hammock on a warm September afternoon, wondering what his neighbors were up to, but too lazy to go and find out. He was in one of his difficult moods, because the day had been both unproductive and unsatisfying, and he was wishing he could live it over again. The hot weather made him lazy, and he had avoided his studies, tested Mr. Brooke's patience to the limit, upset his grandfather by playing the piano for half the afternoon, frightened the housemaids terribly by hinting that one of his dogs had gone mad, and after an argument with the stable worker about what he thought was neglect of his horse, he had thrown himself into his hammock to be annoyed about the stupidity of the world in general, until the peace of the lovely day calmed him despite himself. Staring up into the green shade of the horse-chestnut trees above him, he daydreamed about all kinds of things, and was just imagining himself on a sailing ship going around the world when the sound of voices brought him back to reality. Peeking through the netting of the hammock, he saw the March girls coming out of their house, looking as if they were setting off on some kind of outing.

"What in the world are those girls doing now?" thought Laurie, opening his sleepy eyes to take a good look, because there was something rather unusual about the appearance of his neighbors. Each wore a large, wide-brimmed hat, a brown linen bag hung over one shoulder, and carried a long walking stick. Meg had a cushion, Jo a book, Beth a basket, and Amy a portfolio. All of them walked quietly through the garden, out at the small back gate, and began to climb the hill that lay between the house and the river.

"Well, that's not very fair," said Laurie to himself, "to have a picnic and not invite me! They can't be going in the boat, because they haven't got the key. Maybe they forgot it. I'll take it to them and see what's going on."

Although he had half a dozen hats, it took him some time to find one, then there was a search for the key, which was finally found in his pocket, so that the girls were well out of sight when he jumped over the fence and ran after them. Taking the fastest way to the boathouse, he waited for them to appear, but no one came, and he went up the hill to look around. A group of pine trees covered one part of it, and from the center of this green spot came a sound clearer than the soft sound of the pines or the sleepy chirping of the crickets.

"What a lovely scene!" thought Laurie, peeking through the bushes, looking wide awake and in good spirits already.

It was a rather pretty little picture, because the sisters sat together in the shaded spot, with sun and shadow flickering over them, the fragrant breeze lifting their hair and cooling their warm cheeks, and all the little woodland creatures going about their business as if these were not strangers but old friends. Meg sat on her cushion, sewing neatly with her white hands, looking as fresh and sweet as a rose in her pink dress among the green leaves. Beth was sorting the pine cones that lay thick under a nearby hemlock tree, because she made pretty things with them. Amy was sketching a group of ferns, and Jo was knitting as she read aloud. A shadow passed over the boy's face as he watched them, feeling that he ought to go away since he hadn't been invited, yet staying because home felt very lonely and this quiet group in the woods was very appealing to his restless spirit. He stood so still that a squirrel busy with its nut-gathering ran down a nearby pine, suddenly saw him, and jumped back, scolding so loudly that Beth looked up, saw the wistful face behind the birch trees, and beckoned to him with an encouraging smile.

"May I come in, please? Or will I be in the way?" he asked, moving forward slowly.

Meg raised her eyebrows, but Jo looked at her defiantly and said immediately, "Of course you may. We should have invited you before, only we thought you wouldn't want to join in a quiet girls' activity like this."

"I always like your activities, but if Meg doesn't want me, I'll go away."

"I have no objection, as long as you do something. It's against the rules to be idle here," replied Meg seriously but kindly.

"Many thanks. I'll do anything if you'll let me stay a little while, because it's as dull as a desert down there. Shall I sew, read, sort cones, draw, or do all of them at once? I'm ready for whatever you want." And Laurie sat down with a humble expression that was delightful to see.

"Finish this story while I turn the heel of my sock," said Jo, handing him the book.

"Yes, ma'am," was his humble answer, as he began reading, doing his best to show his gratitude for being allowed to join the 'Busy Bee Society'.

The story wasn't a long one, and when it was finished, he felt he had earned the right to ask a few questions.

"Excuse me, ma'am, could I ask if this highly interesting and charming organization is a new one?"

"Should we tell him?" asked Meg, looking at her sisters.

"He'll laugh," warned Amy.

"Who cares?" said Jo.

"I think he'll like it," added Beth.

"Of course I will! I give you my word I won't laugh. Go ahead and tell, Jo, and don't be afraid."

"Afraid of you! Please. Well, you see, we used to play Pilgrim's Progress, and we've been doing it seriously all winter and summer."

"Yes, I know," said Laurie, nodding wisely.

"Who told you?" demanded Jo.

"Spirits."

"No, I did. I wanted to entertain him one evening when you were all away and he was rather sad. He did enjoy it, so don't be angry, Jo," said Beth gently.

"You can't keep a secret. Never mind, it makes things easier now."

"Please go on," said Laurie, as Jo became absorbed in her work, looking slightly annoyed.

"Oh, didn't she tell you about this new plan of ours? Well, we have tried not to waste our vacation time, and each of us has had a personal goal and worked on it seriously. The vacation is almost over, the goals are all achieved, and we're very glad we didn't waste our time."

"Yes, I imagine so," and Laurie thought regretfully of his own wasted days.

"Mother likes us to be outside as much as possible, so we bring our work here and have lovely times. For the fun of it, we carry our things in these bags, wear the old hats, use the sticks to help us climb the hill, and play pilgrims as we used to do years ago. We call this hill the Delectable Mountain, because from here we can see far away and look at the country where we hope to live someday."

Jo pointed, and Laurie sat up to look, because through an opening in the trees one could see across the wide blue river, the meadows on the other side, far across the edges of the great city, to the green hills that rose up to meet the sky. The sun was low, and the sky glowed with the beauty of an autumn sunset. Gold and purple clouds rested on the hilltops, and rising high into the reddish light were silvery white peaks that shone like the graceful towers of some heavenly city.

"How beautiful that is!" said Laurie softly, because he was quick to notice and appreciate beauty of any kind.

"It often looks like this, and we love to watch it, because it is never quite the same but always magnificent," replied Amy, wishing she could paint it.

"Jo talks about the country where we hope to live someday—the real country, she means, with animals and farming and all of that. It would be nice, but I wish the beautiful country up there was real and we could go to it," said Beth thoughtfully.

"There is a more beautiful country than that, where we will all go eventually, when we are good enough," answered Meg in her gentlest voice.

"It feels like such a long time to wait, and so hard to achieve. I want to fly away immediately, like those swallows, and go through that magnificent gate."

"You'll get there, Beth, sooner or later—there's no doubt of that," said Jo. "I'm the one who will have to fight and work and try hard and wait, and maybe never get in after all."

"You'll have me for company, if that's any comfort. I'll have to do a great deal of growing before I can see your Heavenly City. If I arrive late, will you say a good word for me, Beth?"

Something in the boy's face troubled his gentle little friend, but she said cheerfully, with her calm eyes on the changing clouds, "If people really want to get there and truly try all their lives, I believe they will make it, because I don't think there are any locks on that door or any guards at the gate. I always imagine it as it is in the picture, where the shining figures stretch out their hands to welcome poor Christian as he comes up from the river."

"Wouldn't it be wonderful if all the dreams we have could come true and we could live in them?" said Jo, after a short pause.

"I've made so many of them that it would be hard to choose which I'd want," said Laurie, lying on his back and throwing pine cones at the squirrel who had given him away earlier.

"You'd have to choose your favorite one. What is it?" asked Meg.

"If I tell you mine, will you tell me yours?"

"Yes, if the girls will too."

"We will. Go ahead, Laurie."

"After I've seen as much of the world as I want to, I'd like to settle in Germany and have as much music as I choose. I'm to be a famous musician myself, and everyone will rush to hear me. And I'm never to be bothered about money or business, but just enjoy myself and live for what I love. That's my favorite dream. What's yours, Meg?"

Meg seemed to find it a little difficult to share hers, and waved a fern in front of her face as if to push away imaginary insects, while she said slowly, "I would like a beautiful house, full of all kinds of comfortable things—good food, pretty clothes, beautiful furniture, pleasant people, and plenty of money. I would be the mistress of it and run it as I pleased, with plenty of servants so I would never need to work. How I would enjoy that! Because I wouldn't be lazy, but would do good and make everyone love me very much."

"Wouldn't your dream home have a master?" asked Laurie with a sly smile.

"I said 'pleasant people', you know," and Meg carefully tied up her shoe as she spoke, so no one could see her face.

"Why don't you say you'd have a wonderful, wise, good husband and some lovely children? You know your dream wouldn't be complete without them," said blunt Jo, who hadn't yet developed tender romantic feelings and found love rather silly, except in books.

"And yours would have nothing but horses, writing materials, and novels," replied Meg, a little annoyed.

"Oh, wouldn't it though? I'd have a stable full of beautiful horses, rooms filled with books, and I'd write using a magical pen, so that my works would be as famous as Laurie's music. I want to do something great before I settle into my dream home, something heroic or wonderful that won't be forgotten after I'm dead. I don't know what yet, but I'm watching for the opportunity and plan to surprise you all one day. I think I shall write books and become rich and famous—that would suit me perfectly—so that is my favorite dream."

"Mine is to stay at home safely with Father and Mother and help look after the family," said Beth contentedly.

"Don't you wish for anything else?" asked Laurie.

"Since I got my little piano, I am completely satisfied. I only wish we may all stay well and be together—nothing else."

"I have so many wishes, but my most important one is to be an artist, go to Rome, make great paintings, and be the best artist in the whole world," was Amy's modest desire.

"We're an ambitious group, aren't we? Every one of us, except Beth, wants to be rich and famous and wonderful in every way. I wonder if any of us will ever get what we wish for," said Laurie, chewing on a piece of grass thoughtfully.

"I've got the key to my dream, but whether I can unlock the door remains to be seen," said Jo mysteriously.

"I've got the key to mine, but I'm not allowed to use it yet. I'm tired of waiting for college!" muttered Laurie with an impatient sigh.

"Here's mine!" and Amy held up her pencil.

"I haven't got any," said Meg sadly.

"Yes, you have," said Laurie immediately.

"Where?"

"In your face."

"That's nonsense—that's not useful at all."

"Wait and see if it doesn't bring you something worth having," replied the boy, laughing at the thought of a charming little secret he believed he knew.

Meg turned red behind the fern, but asked no questions and looked across the river with the same hopeful expression that Mr. Brooke had worn when he told the story of the knight.

"If we are all still alive in ten years, let's meet and see how many of us have achieved our wishes, or how much closer we are then than we are now," said Jo, always ready with a plan.

"Goodness! How old I'll be—twenty-seven!" exclaimed Meg, who already felt quite grown up, having just turned seventeen.

"You and I will be twenty-six, Teddy, Beth twenty-four, and Amy twenty-two. What a very old group!" said Jo.

"I hope I'll have done something to be proud of by then, but I'm so lazy I'm afraid I'll just waste time, Jo."

"You need a good reason to work hard, Mother says, and when you find it, she's sure you'll work wonderfully."

"Does she? Really, then I will, if I only get the chance!" cried Laurie, sitting up with sudden energy. "I should be satisfied with pleasing Grandfather, and I do try, but it goes against what I want, you see, and it's difficult. He wants me to be a merchant dealing in Asian goods, as he was, and I'd rather do anything else. I hate tea and silk and spices and all the things his old ships carry, and I don't care if they sink when I own them. Going to college should satisfy him, because if I give him four years he ought to let me make my own choices after that. But he's determined, and I'll have to do exactly as he did, unless I break away and follow my own path, as my father did. If there were someone else to keep the old gentleman company, I'd leave tomorrow."

Laurie spoke with excitement and looked ready to carry out his threat at the first opportunity, because he was growing up quickly and, despite his lazy ways, had a young man's dislike of being controlled and a young man's restless desire to try the world for himself.

"I think you should sail away in one of your ships and never come home until you've tried your own way," said Jo, whose imagination was excited by the thought of such a bold adventure, and who felt sympathy for what she called 'Teddy's troubles'.

"That's not right, Jo. You shouldn't say things like that, and Laurie shouldn't follow your bad advice. You should do exactly what your grandfather wishes, my dear boy," said Meg in her most motherly tone. "Do your best at college, and when he sees that you're trying to please him, I'm sure he won't be hard on you or unfair. As you say, there is no one else to stay with him and keep him company, and you'd never forgive yourself if you left without his permission. Don't be miserable or worried, but do your duty and you'll get your reward, as good Mr. Brooke has, by being respected and loved."

"What do you know about him?" asked Laurie, grateful for the good advice but not wanting a lecture, and glad to change the subject from himself after his unusual outburst.

"Only what your grandfather told us about him—how he took good care of his own mother until she died and refused to go abroad as a tutor to some wealthy family because he wouldn't leave her. And how he now looks after an old woman who nursed his mother, and never tells anyone, but is just as generous and patient and kind as possible."

"So he is, dear old fellow!" said Laurie warmly, as Meg paused, looking flushed and earnest from her story. "It's just like Grandpa to find out all about him without letting him know, and to tell everyone about his goodness so that they'd like him. Brooke couldn't understand why your mother was so kind to him, inviting him over with me and treating him in her wonderful, friendly way. He thought she was absolutely perfect and talked about it for days and days, and said wonderful things about all of you. If I ever do get my wish, you'll see what I'll do for Brooke."

"Begin by not making his life miserable," said Meg sharply.

"How do you know I do that?"

"I can always tell by his expression when he leaves. If you've been good, he looks satisfied and walks quickly. If you've been difficult, he looks serious and walks slowly, as if he wishes he could go back and do his work better."

"Well, I like that! So you keep track of my good and bad behavior by watching Brooke's face, do you? I see him bow and smile as he passes your window, but I didn't know you'd worked out a way to communicate."

"We haven't. Don't be angry, and please don't tell him I said anything! I was only saying it to show I care about how you're doing, and what's said here is said in confidence, you know," cried Meg, very worried about what might follow from her careless words.

"I don't tell stories," replied Laurie, with his proud manner that Jo had given a name to. "Only if Brooke is going to be my indicator, I'll have to make sure I give him pleasant things to report."

"Please don't be offended. I didn't mean to lecture or tell stories or be foolish. I only thought Jo was encouraging you in a feeling you'd regret later. You're so kind to us that we feel as if you're our brother and we say exactly what we think. Forgive me—I meant it kindly." And Meg held out her hand with a gesture that was both warm and hesitant.

Ashamed of his brief irritation, Laurie squeezed the kind little hand and said honestly, "I'm the one who should be forgiven. I'm being difficult and have been out of sorts all day. I like you to point out my faults and be sisterly to me, so don't mind if I'm grumpy sometimes. I'm grateful all the same."

Determined to show that he wasn't offended, he made himself as pleasant as possible, wound thread for Meg, recited poetry to please Jo, shook down pine cones for Beth, and helped Amy with her ferns, proving himself a worthy member of the 'Busy Bee Society'. In the middle of a lively discussion about the home habits of turtles—one of these friendly creatures having walked up from the river—the faint sound of a bell reminded them that Hannah had put the tea on, and they had just enough time to get home for supper.

"May I come again?" asked Laurie.

"Yes, if you are good and love your books, as the boys in the reader are told to do," said Meg, smiling.

"I'll try."

"Then you may come, and I'll teach you to knit as the Scottish men do. There's a great need for warm socks right now," added Jo, waving hers like a large blue woolen flag as they said goodbye at the gate.

That night, when Beth played for Mr. Laurence in the soft evening light, Laurie, standing in the shadow of the curtain, listened to the gentle music that always calmed his troubled spirit, and watched the old man, who sat with his gray head in his hand, thinking tender thoughts of the child he had loved so much and lost. Remembering the conversation of the afternoon, the boy said to himself, with the resolve to accept the sacrifice cheerfully, "I'll let my dream go, and stay with the dear old gentleman while he needs me, for I am all he has."
