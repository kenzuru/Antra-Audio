When Mowgli was old enough, the wolves brought him to the Council Rock for the first time. This was where all the wolves gathered once every so often to discuss pack matters, choose leaders, and decide the future of the cubs. Every wolf born that season had to be presented to the pack.

Mowgli wasn’t nervous at first. He thought it was just another gathering, and he was used to being around the wolves. But when he saw how many wolves there were—dozens, maybe more—crowded around the stone platform, he realized this was more serious than he expected.

The wolf cubs were brought forward one by one. The older wolves sniffed them, nodded, and accepted them into the pack.

When it was Mowgli’s turn, the wolves around him went quiet. He could feel every eye on him. He tried to stand tall, but he felt small compared to all the wolves watching him.

Some wolves muttered to each other.
“A man-cub?”
“He doesn’t even have claws.”
“Why should we accept him?”

Akela, the leader of the pack, stood on top of the rock. He was old but strong, with a calm voice everyone respected. He looked at Mowgli for a long moment before speaking.

“This child has lived in our den,” Akela said. “But he is not a wolf. The law says every cub must be accepted by the pack. Speak now.”

A few wolves immediately objected. They said humans caused trouble, humans grew up to make weapons, humans were dangerous. Some were simply unsure what it meant for the pack to raise one.

Mowgli listened without reacting. He didn’t understand all the arguments, but he understood the tone. They were deciding whether he could stay.

Bagheera stepped forward. His voice was quiet but strong enough for everyone to hear.

“The child has lived with the wolves,” Bagheera said. “He has learned the laws like the others. I owe the pack much, and I offer a gift. Accept the man-cub.”

The wolves murmured. Bagheera rarely offered gifts. When he did, it meant he was serious.

Baloo spoke next. “I have taught him myself. He listens better than some of your own cubs. The jungle will be safer with him learning the right ways.”

Mowgli stood silently between Mother Wolf and Father Wolf. He looked at the pack, waiting. He didn’t realize he was holding his breath until he let it out slowly.

But not everyone agreed.

An older wolf barked, “Shere Khan says the child is dangerous. Why challenge the tiger? Why bring trouble to the pack?”

Bagheera’s eyes narrowed. “Shere Khan is not part of this council. And fear of him is no reason to abandon a harmless cub.”

The pack began to argue again—some for Mowgli, some against. The noise grew louder. Even Akela had trouble calming them.

Then Mother Wolf stepped forward.

She almost never spoke at gatherings, so the pack went quiet.

“I found him alone,” she said. “He came to us helpless. If the pack rejects him, Shere Khan will kill him. That is the truth. If you want to give the tiger a victory, then drive the boy away. But I say he is brave and learns quickly. He is one of us.”

The silence that followed felt heavy, like the whole jungle was listening.

Finally Akela raised his head. “The law allows any two animals besides the parents to speak for a cub. Bagheera has spoken. Baloo has spoken. That is enough. The child stays.”

The decision settled over the gathering. Some wolves were still unsure, but the rules were clear. Mowgli had been accepted.

Later, when the wolves began leaving the Council Rock, Mowgli stayed close to Bagheera.

“Why did you help me?” he asked.

Bagheera looked down at him. “You will understand when you’re older. For now, know this—you belong to the pack, but the jungle will not always be kind. You must learn quickly.”

Mowgli nodded. He didn’t fully understand, but he respected Bagheera too much to argue.

When they returned to the cave, the pups jumped on Mowgli as if nothing unusual had happened. To them, he had always been part of the family.

And now, officially, he was part of the pack.
