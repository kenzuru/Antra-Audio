

The detective and Passepartout met often on deck after this conversation, though Fix was careful and did not try to make his companion reveal any more information about Mr. Fogg. He caught a glimpse of that mysterious gentleman once or twice, but Mr. Fogg usually stayed in his cabin, where he kept Aouda company, or, following his usual habit, played whist.

Passepartout began to wonder seriously about what strange chance kept Fix following the same route as his master. It was really worth thinking about why this clearly very pleasant and agreeable person, whom he had first met at Suez, then encountered again on the "Mongolia," who had got off at Bombay, which he had named as his destination, and who had now turned up so unexpectedly on the "Rangoon," was following Mr. Fogg's path step by step. What was Fix trying to do? Passepartout was ready to bet his Indian shoes — which he kept very carefully — that Fix would also leave Hong Kong at the same time as them, and probably on the same ship.

Passepartout could have thought about it for a hundred years without guessing the real reason why the detective was following them. He could never have imagined that Phileas Fogg was being followed as a criminal around the world. But, as people naturally try to explain every mystery, Passepartout suddenly came up with an explanation for Fix's movements — one that was actually not unreasonable at all. Fix, he thought, must be an agent sent by Mr. Fogg's friends at the Reform Club to follow him and check that he really did travel around the world as agreed.

"Of course!" the good servant repeated to himself, pleased with his clever thinking. "He's a spy sent to watch us! That's not quite right, following Mr. Fogg, who is such an honourable man! Ah, gentlemen of the Reform, this will cost you dearly!"

Passepartout, delighted with his discovery, decided to say nothing about it to his master, in case Mr. Fogg would be rightly offended by such distrust from his opponents. But he decided to tease Fix, whenever he had the chance, with mysterious hints — which would not, however, give away his real suspicions.

On the afternoon of Wednesday October 30th, the "Rangoon" entered the Strait of Malacca, which separates the peninsula of that name from Sumatra. The mountainous and rocky small islands blocked the view of this beautiful large island from the travellers. The "Rangoon" stopped at Singapore the next day at four in the morning to take on coal, having arrived half a day earlier than the scheduled time. Phileas Fogg noted this gain in his diary, and then, accompanied by Aouda, who expressed a wish to walk on shore, got off the ship.

Fix, suspicious of Mr. Fogg's every movement, followed them carefully without being noticed himself, while Passepartout, laughing to himself at Fix's behaviour, went about his usual activities.

The island of Singapore is not impressive in appearance, since there are no mountains. Yet it is attractive in its own way. It is like a park with pleasant roads and streets. A fine carriage, pulled by two smooth New Holland horses, carried Phileas Fogg and Aouda through rows of palm trees with bright leaves and clove trees, where the cloves form the centre of a half-open flower. Pepper plants had replaced the thorny hedges of European fields. Sago bushes, large ferns with beautiful branches, added variety to the scenery of this tropical place, while nutmeg trees in full leaf filled the air with a strong, sweet smell. Quick and grinning groups of monkeys jumped about in the trees, and tigers could be found in the forests.

After a two-hour drive through the countryside, Aouda and Mr. Fogg returned to the town, which is a large collection of heavy, irregular houses surrounded by lovely gardens full of tropical fruits and plants. At ten o'clock they got back on the ship, closely followed by the detective, who had kept them in sight the whole time.

Passepartout, who had been buying several dozen mangoes — a fruit as large as a good-sized apple, dark brown on the outside and bright red inside, with white flesh that melts in the mouth and gives a wonderful feeling of pleasure — was waiting for them on deck. He was very happy to offer some mangoes to Aouda, who thanked him very warmly.

At eleven o'clock the "Rangoon" left Singapore harbour, and a few hours later the high mountains of Malacca, with their forests home to the most beautifully coloured tigers in the world, disappeared from view. Singapore is about thirteen hundred miles from the island of Hong Kong, which is a small English colony near the Chinese coast. Phileas Fogg hoped to complete the journey in six days, so as to be in time for the steamer leaving on November 6th for Yokohama, the main Japanese port.

The "Rangoon" carried a large number of passengers, many of whom got off at Singapore. These included Indians, people from Ceylon, Chinese, Malays, and Portuguese, most of them second-class travellers.

The weather, which had been fine until then, changed with the last quarter of the moon. The sea became rough, and the wind at times rose almost to a storm. Fortunately it blew from the south-west and therefore helped the steamer's progress. The captain raised his sails as often as possible, and under both steam and sail the ship moved quickly along the coasts of Anam and Cochin China. However, because of problems with the "Rangoon's" construction, unusual care was needed in bad weather. The time lost because of this nearly drove Passepartout mad, though it did not seem to bother his master at all. Passepartout blamed the captain, the engineer, and the crew, and wished all those connected with the ship would go somewhere very far away. Perhaps the thought of the gas burning uselessly in his master's house in Saville Row had something to do with his strong impatience.

"You are in a great hurry to reach Hong Kong?" Fix said to him one day.

"A very great hurry!"

"Mr. Fogg, I suppose, is anxious to catch the steamer for Yokohama?"

"Terribly anxious."

"You believe in this journey around the world, then?"

"Absolutely. Don't you, Mr. Fix?"

"I? I don't believe a word of it."

"You're a clever one!" said Passepartout, giving him a knowing look.

This expression made Fix a little uncomfortable, though he did not know quite why. Had the Frenchman guessed his real purpose? He did not know what to think. But how could Passepartout have found out that he was a detective? Yet, in speaking as he did, the man clearly meant more than he was saying.

Passepartout went even further the next day. He simply could not keep quiet.

"Mr. Fix," he said in a playful tone, "will we have the misfortune of losing you when we get to Hong Kong?"

"Well," replied Fix, a little embarrassed, "I'm not sure. Perhaps—"

"Ah, if only you would come with us! An agent of the Peninsular Company, you know, cannot stop halfway! You were only going to Bombay, and here you are in China. America is not far off, and from America to Europe is just one more step."

Fix looked carefully at his companion, whose face was perfectly calm, and laughed along with him. But Passepartout kept teasing him by asking whether he made much money from his current occupation.

"Yes and no," replied Fix. "There is good luck and bad luck in such things. But you must understand that I do not travel at my own expense."

"Oh, I am quite sure of that!" cried Passepartout, laughing heartily.

Fix, thoroughly confused, went down to his cabin and thought deeply. He was clearly suspected. Somehow or other the Frenchman had found out that he was a detective. But had he told his master? What role was he playing in all this — was he an ally or not? Was the plan finished? Fix spent several hours turning these questions over, sometimes thinking that everything was lost, then persuading himself that Fogg knew nothing about his presence, and then unable to decide what was the best course of action.

Despite this, he kept his calm and finally decided to speak honestly to Passepartout. If he could not arrest Fogg at Hong Kong, and if Fogg made plans to leave that last English territory, he, Fix, would tell Passepartout everything. Either the servant was helping his master, in which case the master already knew of Fix's activities and everything would fail. Or the servant knew nothing about the robbery, in which case it would be in his own interest to stop helping the criminal.

This was the situation between Fix and Passepartout. Meanwhile Phileas Fogg moved calmly above them all, completely unaware. He was moving steadily in his path around the world, paying no attention to the smaller concerns around him. Yet there was nearby something which, in the language of astronomers, might be called a troubling star — something that might have caused movement in this gentleman's heart. But no! The beauty of Aouda seemed to have no effect on him, to Passepartout's great surprise. And whatever feelings might have existed would have been more difficult to measure than the movements of Uranus that led to the discovery of Neptune.

It was a daily growing wonder to Passepartout, who could see in Aouda's eyes the depth of her gratitude to his master. Phileas Fogg, though brave and kind, must be, he thought, completely without feeling. As for any emotion that this journey might have awakened in him, there was clearly no sign of it. Meanwhile poor Passepartout lived in constant daydreams.

One day he was leaning on the railing of the engine room, watching the machinery, when a sudden roll of the steamer lifted the propeller out of the water. Steam came hissing out of the valves, and this made Passepartout furious.

"The valves are not carrying enough pressure!" he exclaimed. "We are not moving fast enough! Oh, these English! If this were an American ship, we might blow up, perhaps, but we would at least go faster!"
