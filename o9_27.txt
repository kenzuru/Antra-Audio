
  During the night of the 5th of December, the train went south-east for about fifty miles, then rose an equal distance in a north-easterly direction, towards the Great Salt Lake.

Passepartout went out onto the platform around nine o'clock to get some fresh air. The weather was cold, the sky grey, but it was not snowing. The disc of the sun, made larger by the mist, looked like an enormous ring of gold. Passepartout was amusing himself by working out its value in pounds sterling, when he was distracted from this interesting activity by a strange-looking person who appeared on the platform.

This person, who had boarded the train at Elko, was tall and dark, with a black moustache, black stockings, a black silk hat, a black waistcoat, black trousers, a white tie, and leather gloves. He could have been taken for a clergyman. He walked from one end of the train to the other and attached to the door of each carriage a notice written by hand.

Passepartout went closer and read one of these notices, which said that Elder William Hitch, Mormon missionary, taking advantage of his presence on train number 48, would give a talk about Mormonism in carriage number 117, between eleven o'clock and midday. He invited all who wished to learn about the mysteries of the religion of the "Latter Day Saints" to attend.

"I'll go," Passepartout said to himself. He knew nothing about Mormonism except the practice of having multiple wives, which is its main feature.

The news quickly spread through the train, which carried about one hundred passengers. Thirty of them at most, attracted by the notice, settled themselves in carriage number 117. Passepartout took one of the front seats. Neither Mr. Fogg nor Fix wanted to attend.

At the agreed time, Elder William Hitch stood up and said in an irritated voice, as if he had already been contradicted, "I tell you that Joe Smith is a martyr, that his brother Hiram is a martyr, and that the actions of the United States Government against the prophets will also make a martyr of Brigham Young. Who dares say the opposite?"

No one challenged the missionary, whose excited tone contrasted strangely with his naturally calm face. No doubt his anger came from the difficulties the Mormons were actually experiencing. The government had just managed, with some difficulty, to bring these independent and strong-willed people under its control. It had taken over Utah and made it follow the laws of the Union, after putting Brigham Young in prison on charges of rebellion and having multiple wives. The followers of the prophet had since doubled their efforts and resisted, at least in words, the authority of Congress. Elder Hitch, as we can see, was trying to gain new followers on the very railway trains.

Then, speaking with a loud voice and frequent gestures, he told the history of the Mormons starting from Biblical times: how a Mormon prophet of the tribe of Joseph in Israel wrote the records of the new religion and left them to his son Mormon. How, many centuries later, a translation of this precious book, which was written in Egyptian, was made by Joseph Smith Junior, a farmer from Vermont, who presented himself as a spiritual prophet in 1825. And how, in short, a heavenly messenger appeared to him in a lit forest and gave him the records of God.

Several members of the audience, not finding the missionary's story very interesting, left the carriage at this point. But Elder Hitch continued his talk, explaining how Smith Junior, with his father, two brothers, and a few followers, founded the church of the "Latter Day Saints." This church had members not only in America but also in England, Norway, Sweden, and Germany, including many workers as well as professionals. He described how a community was established in Ohio, how a church was built there at a cost of two hundred thousand dollars, and a town built at Kirkland. He explained how Smith became an active banker and received from a simple mummy showman a paper scroll said to have been written by Abraham and several famous Egyptians.

The Elder's story was becoming somewhat boring, and his audience slowly became smaller, until only twenty passengers remained. But this did not discourage the enthusiastic preacher, who went on with the story of Joseph Smith's bankruptcy in 1837, and how his angry lenders covered him in tar and feathers. He continued with Smith's return some years later, more respected than ever, at Independence, Missouri, as the leader of a growing community of three thousand followers. He described how Smith was chased away by angry non-Mormons and forced to move further west.

Only ten listeners remained now, including honest Passepartout, who was listening very carefully. He learned that after long persecution, Smith reappeared in Illinois and in 1839 founded a community at Nauvoo, on the Mississippi River, with twenty-five thousand people, of which he became mayor, chief judge, and military leader. He announced himself in 1843 as a candidate for President of the United States, and finally, after being trapped, was thrown into prison and killed by a group of masked men.

Passepartout was now the only person left in the carriage, and the Elder, looking him directly in the face, reminded him that two years after the killing of Joseph Smith, the inspired prophet Brigham Young, his successor, left Nauvoo for the banks of the Great Salt Lake. There, in the middle of this fertile area, directly on the route of the settlers crossing Utah on their way to California, the new community — thanks to the multiple marriages practised by the Mormons — had grown beyond all expectations.

"And this," added Elder William Hitch, "is why Congress has become jealous of us! Why have the soldiers of the Union entered Utah? Why has Brigham Young, our leader, been put in prison, against all justice? Shall we give in to force? Never! Driven from Vermont, driven from Illinois, driven from Ohio, driven from Missouri, driven from Utah — we shall yet find some independent land on which to set up our homes. And you, my brother," continued the Elder, fixing his angry eyes on his single listener, "will you not set yours there too, in the shadow of our flag?"

"No!" replied Passepartout bravely, in his turn leaving the carriage, leaving the Elder to preach to an empty room.

During the talk the train had been making good progress, and around half past twelve it reached the north-west edge of the Great Salt Lake. From there the passengers could see the wide surface of this inland sea, also called the Dead Sea. An American river, like the Jordan, flows into it. It is a beautiful stretch of water, surrounded by tall rocky ridges covered in large layers of white salt — a wonderful sheet of water, which was once larger than it is now. Its shores have gradually moved inward over time, reducing its width while increasing its depth.

The Salt Lake, seventy miles long and thirty-five miles wide, is three miles and eight hundred feet above sea level. Unlike Lake Asphaltite, which is twelve hundred feet below sea level, it contains a large amount of salt. A quarter of the weight of its water is solid matter. Its density is 1,170, and after being made pure, 1,000. Fish, of course, cannot live in it, and those which swim in through the Jordan or the Weber or other rivers die quickly.

The land around the lake was well cultivated, for the Mormons are mostly farmers. Farms and enclosures for animals, fields of wheat, corn, and other crops, rich meadows, hedges of wild roses, clusters of acacia and other plants would have been seen six months earlier. Now the ground was covered with a light layer of snow.

The train arrived at Ogden at two o'clock, where it rested for six hours. Mr. Fogg and his group had time to visit Salt Lake City, connected to Ogden by a branch line. They spent two hours in this strongly American city, built in the pattern of other cities of the Union, like a grid — "with the gloomy sadness of right angles," as Victor Hugo expressed it. The founder of the City of the Saints could not escape the love for order and symmetry that is typical of the English-speaking peoples. In this unusual country, where the people are certainly not at the level of their own institutions, everything is done in a very structured way — cities, houses, and even their mistakes.

The travellers were walking, at three o'clock, through the streets of the town built between the banks of the Jordan and the lower slopes of the Wahsatch mountains. They saw few or no churches, but the prophet's house, the court building, and the weapons store — blue brick buildings with covered walkways and front porches, surrounded by gardens with acacias, palm trees, and locust trees. A wall of clay and stone, built in 1853, surrounded the town. In the main street were the market and several hotels decorated with canopies. The place did not seem very full of people. The streets were almost empty, except near the temple, which they only reached after crossing several areas surrounded by fences. There were many women, which was easily explained by the "special practice" of the Mormons. But it should not be supposed that all Mormons practise polygamy. They are free to marry or not, as they choose. It is worth noting, however, that it is mainly the female citizens of Utah who want to marry, since, according to the Mormon religion, unmarried women are not allowed to enjoy its greatest rewards. These poor women seemed to be neither well off nor happy. Some — the wealthier ones, no doubt — wore short, open, black silk dresses under a hood or modest shawl. Others wore clothes in the local style.

Passepartout could not look without a certain worry at these women, gathered in groups to bring happiness to a single Mormon husband. His common sense felt most sorry for the husband. It seemed to him a terrible thing to have to guide so many wives through all the difficulties of life, and to lead them all together to the Mormon heaven, with the chance of seeing them there in the company of the glorious Smith, who was certainly the greatest figure of that wonderful place, for all time. He felt clearly against such a life, and he imagined — perhaps mistakenly — that the women of Salt Lake City were looking at him with rather alarming interest. Happily, his stay there was brief. At four o'clock the group found themselves back at the station, took their seats in the train, and the whistle blew for departure. Just at that moment, however, cries of "Stop! Stop!" were heard.

Trains, like time and tide, stop for no one. The gentleman shouting these words was clearly a Mormon who was almost too late. He was out of breath from running. Luckily for him, the station had neither gates nor barriers. He ran along the track, jumped onto the back platform of the train, and fell, exhausted, into one of the seats.

Passepartout, who had been watching this amateur gymnast with great interest, went up to him curiously, and learned that the man had run away after an unpleasant argument at home.

When the Mormon had got his breath back, Passepartout politely asked how many wives he had. From the way the man had run away, one might have thought he had at least twenty.

"One, sir," replied the Mormon, raising his arms to the sky — "one, and that was already too many!"
