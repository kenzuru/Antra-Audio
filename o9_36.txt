
  Now is the time to describe the change that took place in English public opinion when it became known that the real bank robber — a man named James Strand — had been arrested on the 17th of December in Edinburgh. Three days before, Phileas Fogg had been considered a criminal, chased desperately by the police. Now he was seen as an honourable gentleman, carefully completing his unusual journey around the world.

The newspapers returned to their discussions about the bet. All those who had placed bets, for or against him, came back to life as if by magic. The "Phileas Fogg bonds" became worth trading again, and many new bets were made. Phileas Fogg's name was once again highly valued on the market.

His five friends from the Reform Club spent those three days in a state of nervous anxiety. Would Phileas Fogg — whom they had almost forgotten — reappear before them? Where was he at that moment? The 17th of December, the day of James Strand's arrest, was the seventy-sixth day since Fogg's departure from London, and no news of him had arrived. Was he dead? Had he given up, or was he still continuing his journey on the agreed route? And would he appear on Saturday, the 21st of December, at a quarter before nine in the evening, at the entrance of the Reform Club?

The worry that gripped London society during those three days cannot be described. Telegrams were sent to America and Asia for news of Phileas Fogg. Messengers were sent to the house in Saville Row morning and evening. No news. The police did not know what had become of detective Fix, who had so unfortunately chased the wrong person. Bets continued to grow, however, in number and value. Phileas Fogg, like a racehorse approaching the last corner, was being watched more closely than ever. The bonds were no longer quoted at a hundred below their value, but at twenty below, then ten, then five. And old paralysed Lord Albemarle bet in his favour.

On Saturday evening, a large crowd had gathered in Pall Mall and the nearby streets — it looked like a permanent crowd of traders surrounding the Reform Club. Circulation was difficult, and everywhere there were arguments, discussions, and money deals. The police had great difficulty controlling the crowd, and as the hour when Phileas Fogg was expected drew closer, the excitement reached its highest point.

The five opponents of Phileas Fogg had met in the main saloon of the club. John Sullivan and Samuel Fallentin, the bankers, Andrew Stuart, the engineer, Gauthier Ralph, the director of the Bank of England, and Thomas Flanagan, the beer maker — all of them were waiting anxiously.

When the clock showed twenty minutes past eight, Andrew Stuart stood up and said: "Gentlemen, in twenty minutes the time we agreed upon with Mr. Fogg will have passed."

"What time did the last train arrive from Liverpool?" asked Thomas Flanagan.

"At twenty-three minutes past seven," replied Gauthier Ralph. "And the next does not arrive until ten minutes after midnight."

"Well, gentlemen," resumed Andrew Stuart, "if Phileas Fogg had come on the 7:23 train, he would have been here by now. We can therefore consider the bet won."

"Let us wait and not be too quick," replied Samuel Fallentin. "You know that Mr. Fogg is very precise. He never arrives too early or too late. I would not be surprised to see him appear before us at the very last moment."

"As for me," said Andrew Stuart nervously, "if I were to see him, I would not believe it was really him."

"The truth is," resumed Thomas Flanagan, "Mr. Fogg's plan was completely foolish. However precise he was, he could not prevent the delays that were certain to happen. A delay of only two or three days would be enough to ruin his trip."

"And notice," added John Sullivan, "that we have had no word from him at all, even though there are telegraph lines along his entire route."

"He has lost, gentlemen," said Andrew Stuart. "He has lost a hundred times over! And you know that the 'China' — the only steamer he could have taken from New York in time — arrived yesterday. I have seen the passenger list, and the name Phileas Fogg is not on it. Even if we say that luck was with him, he could hardly have reached America. I think he will be at least twenty days late, and Lord Albemarle will lose a cool five thousand."

"That is clear," replied Gauthier Ralph. "All we need to do is present Mr. Fogg's cheque at Barings tomorrow."

At that moment, the club clock showed twenty minutes to nine.

"Five minutes more," said Andrew Stuart.

The five gentlemen looked at each other. Their worry was growing intense. Not wanting to show it, however, they all agreed to Mr. Fallentin's suggestion of a card game.

"I would not give up my four thousand from the bet," said Andrew Stuart, sitting down, "for three thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine."

The clock showed eighteen minutes to nine.

The players picked up their cards, but could not keep their eyes off the clock. Certainly, however confident they felt, the minutes had never seemed so long.

"Seventeen minutes to nine," said Thomas Flanagan, as he cut the cards that Ralph handed to him.

Then there was a moment of silence. The large saloon was completely quiet. Outside, the sound of the crowd could be heard, with the occasional sharp cry. The clock ticked out the seconds, which each player eagerly counted while listening, with perfect regularity.

"Sixteen minutes to nine!" said John Sullivan, in a voice that showed how tense he felt.

One more minute, and the bet would be won. Andrew Stuart and his companions stopped their game. They put down their cards and counted the seconds.

At the fortieth second — nothing. At the fiftieth — still nothing.

At the fifty-fifth second, a loud cry was heard in the street, followed by applause, cheering, and some angry shouts.

The players rose from their seats.

At the fifty-seventh second, the door of the saloon opened. The clock had not yet beaten the sixtieth second when Phileas Fogg appeared, followed by an excited crowd that had pushed its way through the club doors. In his calm voice, he said: "Here I am, gentlemen!"
