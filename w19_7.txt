There was a long table set out under a tree in front of the house, and the March Hare and the Hatter were seated at it, having tea. A Dormouse was sitting between them, fast asleep, and the other two were using it as a cushion, resting their elbows on it and talking right over its head. “That looks very uncomfortable for the Dormouse,” thought Alice; “only, as it’s sound asleep, I suppose it doesn’t mind.”

The table itself was a large one, but the three creatures were all crowded together at just one corner of it. “No room! No room!” they cried out loudly when they saw Alice approaching. “There’s plenty of room!” said Alice indignantly, and she sat down firmly in a large arm-chair at the opposite end of the table.

“Have some wine,” the March Hare said in a seemingly encouraging tone.

Alice looked all around the table, but there was nothing on it except various types of tea. “I don’t see any wine,” she remarked.

“There isn’t any,” said the March Hare.

“Then it was not very polite of you to offer it,” said Alice angrily.

“It wasn’t very polite of you to sit down without being properly invited,” said the March Hare.

“I didn’t know it was your table,” said Alice. “It’s set for a great many more people than just three.”

“Your hair needs cutting,” said the Hatter. He had been staring at Alice with great curiosity for some time, and this was the very first thing he said.

“You should learn not to make personal remarks,” Alice said with some severity. “It’s extremely rude.”

The Hatter opened his eyes very wide when he heard this, but all he said was, “Why is a raven like a writing-desk?”

“Good! We are going to have some fun now!” thought Alice excitedly. “I’m glad they’ve started asking riddles. I believe I can guess that one,” she added out loud.

“Do you mean that you think you can figure out the answer to it?” said the March Hare.

“Exactly so,” said Alice.

“Then you should say what you mean,” the March Hare went on, arguing.

“I do,” Alice hastily replied. “At least—at least I mean what I say—that’s the same thing, you know.”

“Not the same thing in the slightest!” said the Hatter immediately. “You might just as well say that ‘I see what I eat’ is the exact same thing as saying ‘I eat what I see’!”

“You might just as well say,” added the March Hare, “that ‘I like what I get’ is the exact same thing as saying ‘I get what I like’!”

“You might just as well say,” added the Dormouse, who seemed to be talking while still asleep, “that ‘I breathe when I sleep’ is the exact same thing as saying ‘I sleep when I breathe’!”

“It is the same thing with you, then,” said the Hatter, concluding the matter. At this point, the conversation stopped, and the party sat in silence for a minute while Alice thought over everything she could remember about ravens and writing-desks, which was not very much.

The Hatter was the first to break the silence. “What day of the month is it?” he said, turning to Alice. He had taken his large pocket-watch out and was looking at it with concern, shaking it every now and then, and holding it close to his ear.

Alice thought for a moment and then said, “The fourth.”

“Two days wrong!” sighed the Hatter. “I told you that butter wouldn’t be good for the works inside!” he added, looking angrily at the March Hare.

“It was the best butter,” the March Hare replied meekly.

“Yes, but some crumbs must have gotten inside as well,” the Hatter grumbled. “You shouldn’t have put it in with the bread-knife.”

The March Hare took the watch and looked at it sadly. Then he dipped it into his cup of tea and looked at it again, but he could think of nothing better to say than his first remark, “It was the best butter, you know.”

Alice had been looking over his shoulder with some curiosity. “What a funny watch!” she remarked. “It tells you the day of the month, but it doesn’t tell you what time it is!”

“Why should it?” muttered the Hatter. “Does your watch tell you what year it is?”

“Of course not,” Alice replied very quickly. “But that’s because it stays the same year for such a long time.”

“Which is exactly the case with mine,” said the Hatter.

Alice felt dreadfully confused. The Hatter’s remark seemed to have absolutely no meaning in it, and yet it was certainly English words he was speaking. “I don’t quite understand you,” she said, as politely as she could manage.

“The Dormouse is asleep again,” said the Hatter, and he poured a little stream of hot tea onto its nose.

The Dormouse shook its head impatiently and said, without opening its eyes, “Of course, of course; that is exactly what I was about to remark myself.”

“Have you solved the riddle yet?” the Hatter said, turning back to Alice.

“No, I give up,” Alice replied. “What is the answer?”

“I haven’t the slightest idea,” said the Hatter.

“Nor I,” said the March Hare.

Alice sighed wearily. “I think you might do something better with your time,” she said, “than waste it asking riddles that don’t even have answers.”

“If you knew Time as well as I do,” said the Hatter, with sudden intensity, “you wouldn’t talk about wasting it. Time is a him.”

“I don’t know what you mean,” said Alice.

“Of course you don’t!” the Hatter said, tossing his head contemptuously. “I dare say you’ve never even spoken to Time!”

“Perhaps not,” Alice replied cautiously, “but I know I have to beat time when I learn music.”

“Ah! That explains everything,” said the Hatter. “He won’t tolerate being beaten. Now, if you only stayed on good terms with him, he would do almost anything you liked with the clock. For instance, suppose it were nine o’clock in the morning, which is exactly the time to begin lessons: you’d only have to whisper a suggestion to Time, and round goes the clock in a flash! Half-past one, time for dinner!”

(“I only wish it was,” the March Hare whispered quietly to himself.)

“That would be wonderful, certainly,” said Alice thoughtfully. “But then—I wouldn’t be hungry for it, you know.”

“Not at first, perhaps,” said the Hatter. “But you could keep it at half-past one for as long as you liked.”

“Is that the way you manage things?” Alice asked.

The Hatter shook his head mournfully. “Not me!” he replied. “We had a falling out last March—just before he went mad, you know—” (He pointed with his teaspoon at the March Hare.) “—it was at the great concert given by the Queen of Hearts, and I had to sing

‘Twinkle, twinkle, little bat! How I wonder what you’re at!’

You know the song, perhaps?”

“I’ve heard something rather similar to it,” said Alice.

“It continues, you see,” the Hatter went on, “in this manner:—

‘Up above the world you fly, Like a tea-tray in the sky. Twinkle, twinkle—’”

Here the Dormouse shook itself and began singing in its sleep, “Twinkle, twinkle, twinkle, twinkle—” and went on for so long that they had to pinch it hard to make it stop.

“Well, I had barely finished the first verse,” said the Hatter, “when the Queen leaped up and shouted, ‘He’s murdering the Time! Off with his head!’”

“How dreadfully savage!” exclaimed Alice.

“And ever since that day,” the Hatter went on in a sad, mournful tone, “Time won’t do a single thing I ask! It’s always six o’clock now.”

A sudden idea came into Alice’s head. “Is that the reason why so many tea-things are set out here?” she asked.

“Yes, that’s precisely it,” said the Hatter with a sigh. “It’s always tea-time, and we have no time to wash the dirty things in between.”

“Then you keep moving around the table, I suppose?” said Alice.

“Exactly so,” said the Hatter. “We move as the places get used up.”

“But what happens when you get back to the beginning place again?” Alice ventured to ask.

“Suppose we quickly change the subject,” the March Hare interrupted, yawning widely. “I am getting tired of this topic. I propose that the young lady tells us a story.”

“I’m afraid I don’t know a single story,” said Alice, feeling rather alarmed at the suggestion.

“Then the Dormouse must tell one!” they both cried. “Wake up, Dormouse!” And they pinched it simultaneously on both sides.

The Dormouse slowly opened its eyes. “I wasn’t asleep,” he said in a hoarse, weak voice. “I heard every single word you fellows were saying.”

“Tell us a story!” said the March Hare.

“Yes, please do!” pleaded Alice.

“And be quick about it,” added the Hatter, “or you’ll fall asleep again before you finish.”

“Once upon a time there were three little sisters,” the Dormouse began in a great rush. “And their names were Elsie, Lacie, and Tillie; and they lived at the bottom of a well—”

“What did they live on?” said Alice, who always found questions of eating and drinking fascinating.

“They lived on treacle,” said the Dormouse, after pausing to think for a minute or two.

“They couldn’t possibly have done that, you know,” Alice gently remarked. “They would have been very ill.”

“So they were,” said the Dormouse. “Very ill.”

Alice tried to imagine what such an extraordinary way of living would be like, but it confused her too much, so she went on: “But why did they live at the bottom of a well?”

“Take some more tea,” the March Hare said to Alice, with great sincerity.

“I haven’t had anything yet,” Alice replied in an offended tone, “so I can’t exactly take more.”

“You mean you can’t take less,” said the Hatter, correcting her. “It’s very easy to take more than nothing.”

“Nobody asked for your opinion,” said Alice sharply.

“Who is making personal remarks now?” the Hatter asked triumphantly.

Alice did not quite know what to say to this. So, she helped herself to some tea and bread-and-butter, and then turned back to the Dormouse and repeated her question. “Why did they live at the bottom of a well?”

The Dormouse again took a minute or two to consider it, and then said, “It was a treacle-well.”

“There’s no such thing!” Alice began to object very angrily, but the Hatter and the March Hare went “Sh! sh!” and the Dormouse sulkily remarked, “If you can’t manage to be polite, you had better finish telling the story yourself.”

“No, please go on!” Alice said very humbly. “I promise I won’t interrupt again. I dare say there may be one.”

“One, indeed!” said the Dormouse indignantly. However, he agreed to continue. “And so these three little sisters—they were learning to draw, you know—”

“What exactly did they draw?” said Alice, completely forgetting her promise not to interrupt.

“Treacle,” said the Dormouse, this time answering immediately without thinking.

“I want a clean cup,” interrupted the Hatter. “Let’s all move one place over.”

He moved on as he spoke, and the Dormouse followed him. The March Hare moved into the Dormouse’s place, and Alice rather unwillingly took the place of the March Hare. The Hatter was the only one who gained any benefit from the change. Alice was much worse off than before, as the March Hare had just carelessly tipped the milk-jug into his plate.

Alice did not want to offend the Dormouse again, so she began very cautiously: “But I still don’t understand. Where did they draw the treacle from?”

“You can draw water out of a water-well,” said the Hatter, answering for the Dormouse. “So I should certainly think you could draw treacle out of a treacle-well—eh, stupid?”

“But they were in the well,” Alice said to the Dormouse, choosing to ignore the Hatter's rude final word.

“Of course they were,” said the Dormouse. “—Well in.”

This answer confused poor Alice so much that she allowed the Dormouse to continue its story for some time without interrupting it again.

“They were learning to draw,” the Dormouse went on, yawning widely and rubbing its eyes, for it was becoming extremely sleepy. “And they drew all sorts of things—everything that begins with an M—”

“Why with an M?” said Alice.

“Why not?” said the March Hare.

Alice remained silent.

The Dormouse had closed its eyes by this time and was drifting off into a doze. But, on being pinched by the Hatter, it woke up again with a little shriek and went on: “—that begins with an M, such as mouse-traps, and the moon, and memory, and muchness—you know you say things are ‘much of a muchness’—did you ever actually see such a thing as a drawing of a muchness?”

“Really, now that you ask me,” said Alice, feeling very confused, “I don’t think—”

“Then you shouldn’t talk,” said the Hatter.

This final piece of rudeness was more than Alice could bear. She got up in great disgust and walked off abruptly. The Dormouse fell asleep instantly, and neither of the others took the slightest notice of her departure, although she looked back once or twice, half hoping that they would call after her. The last time she saw them, they were trying to forcibly put the Dormouse into the teapot.

“At any rate, I will never go there again!” said Alice as she picked her way carefully through the wood. “It’s the most utterly stupid tea-party I have ever been to in all my life!”

Just as she said this, she noticed that one of the trees right next to her had a door leading straight into its trunk. “That is very curious!” she thought. “But everything is curious today. I think I might as well go in right now.” And in she went.

Once more, she found herself back in the long hall, standing close to the little glass table. “Now, I’ll manage things much better this time,” she said to herself, and she started by taking the little golden key and unlocking the door that led into the garden. Then, she set to work nibbling at the mushroom (she had kept a piece of it safely in her pocket) until she was exactly about a foot high. Then, she walked down the small passage; and then—she found herself at last in the beautiful garden, standing among the bright, colorful flower-beds and next to the cool, splashing fountains.
