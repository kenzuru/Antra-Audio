

  "Laurie!"

"Hello!" He was halfway up the stairs, but when he turned round and saw Laura he suddenly puffed out his cheeks and opened his eyes wide at her. "Wow, Laura! You look absolutely wonderful," said Laurie. "What a fantastic hat!"

Laura said weakly "Is it?" and smiled up at Laurie, and did not tell him after all.

Soon after that, guests began arriving in large numbers. The band started playing. The hired waiters ran back and forth between the house and the tent. Everywhere you looked there were couples walking slowly, bending towards the flowers, greeting each other, moving on across the lawn. They were like bright birds that had landed in the Sheridans' garden for this one afternoon, on their way to — where? Ah, what happiness it is to be with people who are all happy, to shake hands, press cheeks together, smile into each other's eyes.

"Darling Laura, how well you look!"

"What a lovely hat, child!"

"Laura, you look almost Spanish. I have never seen you look so striking."

And Laura, glowing with happiness, answered softly, "Have you had tea? Would you like an ice cream? The passion fruit ice creams are really rather special." She ran to her father and asked him. "Daddy darling, can the band have something to drink?"

And the perfect afternoon slowly reached its height, slowly faded, slowly its petals closed.

"Never a more wonderful garden party..." "The greatest success..." "Quite the most..."

Laura helped her mother say goodbye to the guests. They stood side by side at the entrance until it was all over.

"All over, all over, thank goodness," said Mrs. Sheridan. "Find the others, Laura. Let's go and have some fresh coffee. I'm exhausted. Yes, it was very successful. But oh, these parties, these parties! Why will you children keep insisting on giving parties!" And they all sat down in the empty tent.

"Have a sandwich, daddy dear. I wrote the label."

"Thank you." Mr. Sheridan took a bite and the sandwich was gone. He took another. "I don't suppose you heard about the awful accident that happened today?" he said.

"My dear," said Mrs. Sheridan, holding up her hand, "we did. It almost ruined the party. Laura insisted we should cancel it."

"Oh, mother!" Laura did not want to be made fun of about it.

"It was a horrible thing all the same," said Mr. Sheridan. "The man was married too. Lived just below in the lane, and has left a wife and about six children, they say."

A slightly awkward silence followed. Mrs. Sheridan fiddled with her cup. Really, it was very thoughtless of father...

Suddenly she looked up. There on the table were all those sandwiches, cakes and cream puffs — all uneaten, all going to be wasted. She had one of her brilliant ideas.

"I know," she said. "Let's put together a basket. Let's send some of this perfectly good food to that poor woman. It will certainly be a wonderful treat for the children. And she is sure to have neighbours coming to visit. How useful to have the food already prepared. Laura!" She jumped up. "Get me the big basket from the cupboard under the stairs."

"But, mother, do you really think it's a good idea?" said Laura.

Again, strangely, she felt different from all of them. To take leftovers from their party. Would the poor woman really want that?

"Of course! What is wrong with you today? An hour or two ago you were insisting that we show sympathy, and now —"

Oh well! Laura ran to get the basket. Her mother filled it and piled it high.

"Take it yourself, darling," she said. "Just run down as you are. No, wait, take the arum lilies too. People of that kind are always so impressed by arum lilies."

"The stems will ruin her lace dress," said practical Jose.

They would. Just in time. "Just the basket then. And, Laura!" — her mother followed her out of the tent — "whatever you do, don't —"

"What, mother?"

No, better not to put such thoughts in the child's head! "Nothing! Run along."

It was just getting dark as Laura closed their garden gate behind her. A large dog ran past like a shadow. The road shone white, and down below in the hollow the small cottages were in deep shadow. How quiet it seemed after the busy afternoon. Here she was, walking down the hill towards a place where a man lay dead, and she could not make it feel real. Why could she not? She stopped for a moment. And it seemed to her that kisses, voices, the clinking of teaspoons, laughter, and the smell of crushed grass were all somehow inside her. There was no room for anything else. How strange! She looked up at the pale sky, and all she could think was, "Yes, it was the most successful party."

Now she had crossed the wide road. The lane began, dark and smoky. Women in shawls and men wearing flat caps hurried past. Men leaned over the fences. Children played in doorways. A low humming sound came from the small poor cottages. In some of them there was a small flickering light, and a shadow moved like a crab across a window. Laura bent her head and hurried on. She now wished she had put on a coat. How her dress stood out! And the big hat with the velvet ribbon — if only it were a different hat! Were people looking at her? They must be. It was a mistake to have come. She had known all along it was a mistake. Should she go back even now?

No, too late. This was the house. It had to be. A small dark group of people stood outside. Beside the gate a very old woman sat in a chair with a crutch, watching. She had her feet resting on a newspaper. The voices stopped as Laura came near. The group moved apart. It was as though she was expected, as though they had known she was coming.

Laura felt very nervous. She tossed the velvet ribbon over her shoulder and said to a woman standing nearby, "Is this Mrs. Scott's house?" and the woman, with a strange smile, said, "It is, my dear."

Oh, to be away from this! She actually said quietly, "Help me, God," as she walked up the small path and knocked on the door. To be away from those staring eyes, or to be covered up by something, even one of those women's shawls. I'll just leave the basket and go, she decided. I won't even wait for it to be emptied.

Then the door opened. A small woman dressed in black appeared in the dark entrance.

Laura said, "Are you Mrs. Scott?" But to her horror the woman answered, "Please come in, miss," and Laura found herself standing inside the entrance passage.

"No," said Laura. "I don't want to come in. I only want to leave this basket. Mother sent —"

The small woman in the dark passage seemed not to have heard her. "This way, please, miss," she said in a smooth voice, and Laura followed her.

She found herself in a small, poor kitchen, lit by a smoky lamp. There was a woman sitting in front of the fire.

"Em," said the small woman who had let her in. "Em! It's a young lady." She turned to Laura. She said meaningfully, "I'm her sister, miss. You'll excuse her, won't you?"

"Oh, but of course!" said Laura. "Please, please don't disturb her. I — I only want to leave —"

But at that moment the woman at the fire turned round. Her face, swollen and red, with puffy eyes and lips, looked terrible. She seemed unable to understand why Laura was there. What did it mean? Why was this stranger standing in the kitchen with a basket? What was it all about? And the poor face crumpled up again.

"All right, my dear," said the other woman. "I'll thank the young lady."

And again she said, "You'll excuse her, miss, I'm sure," and her own swollen face tried to make a smooth smile.

Laura only wanted to leave, to get away. She was back in the passage. The door opened. She walked straight through into the bedroom, where the dead man was lying.

"You'd like to look at him, wouldn't you?" said Em's sister, and she moved past Laura towards the bed. "Don't be afraid, my dear," — and now her voice sounded gentle and quiet, and gently she pulled down the sheet — "he looks peaceful. There is nothing wrong to see. Come, my dear."

Laura came.

There lay a young man, fast asleep — sleeping so soundly, so deeply, that he was far, far away from them both. Oh, so still, so peaceful. He was dreaming. Never to be woken again. His head was resting deeply in the pillow, his eyes were closed — blind behind the closed eyelids. He had been given over to his dream. What did garden parties and baskets and lace dresses matter to him? He was far from all those things. He was wonderful, beautiful. While they were laughing and while the band was playing, this extraordinary thing had happened in the lane. Happy... happy... All is well, said that sleeping face. This is exactly how it should be. I am at peace.

But all the same you had to cry, and she could not leave the room without saying something to him. Laura let out a loud, childish sob.

"Forgive my hat," she said.

And this time she did not wait for Em's sister. She found her way out through the door, down the path, past all those dark figures. At the corner of the lane she met Laurie.

He stepped out of the shadow. "Is that you, Laura?"

"Yes."

"Mother was getting worried. Was everything all right?"

"Yes, quite. Oh, Laurie!" She took his arm and pressed close against him.

"You're not crying, are you?" asked her brother.

Laura shook her head. She was.

Laurie put his arm around her shoulder. "Don't cry," he said in his warm, loving voice. "Was it terrible?"

"No," sobbed Laura. "It was simply wonderful. But Laurie —" She stopped and looked at her brother. "Isn't life," she said with difficulty, "isn't life —" But what life was, she could not explain. It did not matter. He understood completely.

"Isn't it, darling?" said Laurie.
