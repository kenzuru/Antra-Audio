

  The sun was still shining on the garden when the back door of the Burnells' house shut with a bang, and a very brightly dressed figure walked down the path to the gate. It was Alice, the servant girl, dressed for her afternoon off. She wore a white cotton dress with such large red spots on it, and so many spots, that they made you uncomfortable. She also wore white shoes and a straw hat turned up at the brim and decorated with poppies. Of course she wore gloves—white ones, stained at the fastenings with rust marks—and in one hand she carried a rather worn-looking umbrella which she called her "parasol."

Beryl, sitting at the window, drying her freshly washed hair, thought she had never seen such an odd-looking sight. If Alice had only blackened her face before going out, the picture would have been complete. And where did a girl like that go in a place like this? The heart-shaped Fijian fan moved with contempt through that lovely bright hair. She supposed Alice had met some unpleasant common young man and they'd go off into the bush together. It was a pity to have made herself so noticeable. They'd have a hard time hiding with Alice dressed like that.

But no, Beryl was being unfair. Alice was going to tea with Mrs. Stubbs, who had sent her an invitation through the little boy who came to take orders. She had become very fond of Mrs. Stubbs ever since the first time she went to the shop to get something for her mosquito bites.

"Good heavens!" Mrs. Stubbs had put her hand to her side. "I've never seen anyone so bitten. You look like you've been attacked by cannibals."

Alice did wish there had been a little more life on the road though. It made her feel so strange, having nobody behind her. It made her feel weak in her back. She couldn't believe that someone wasn't watching her. And yet it was silly to turn around. It gave you away. She pulled up her gloves, hummed to herself and said to the distant gum tree, "Won't be long now." But that was hardly company.

Mrs. Stubbs's shop sat on a small hill just off the road. It had two big windows for eyes, a wide veranda for a hat, and the sign on the roof, written roughly as MRS. STUBBS'S, was like a small card stuck carelessly in the hat.

On the veranda there hung a long line of swimming costumes, clinging together as though they had just been rescued from the sea rather than waiting to go in. Beside them hung a collection of beach shoes so completely mixed up that to find one pair you had to pull apart and separate at least fifty. Even then it was extremely rare to find the left shoe that matched the right. So many people had given up and gone away with one shoe that fitted and one that was a little too big.... Mrs. Stubbs was proud of having a bit of everything. The two windows, arranged in shaky pyramid shapes, were packed so tight and piled so high that it seemed only a magician could stop them from falling over. In the left corner of one window, stuck to the glass with four gelatine sweets, there was—and there had been for as long as anyone could remember—a notice.

LOST! HANDSOME GOLD BROOCH
SOLID GOLD
ON OR NEAR BEACH
REWARD OFFERED

Alice pushed open the door. The bell rang, the red curtains parted, and Mrs. Stubbs appeared. With her wide smile and the long bacon knife in her hand, she looked like a friendly pirate. Alice was welcomed so warmly that she found it quite hard to maintain her "polite manners." These consisted of repeated little coughs and throat clearings, pulling at her gloves, straightening her skirt, and a strange difficulty in seeing what was placed in front of her or understanding what was said.

Tea was laid on the parlour table—ham, sardines, a whole pound of butter, and such a large flat cake that it looked like an advertisement for someone's baking powder. But the small gas stove roared so loudly that it was impossible to talk above the noise. Alice sat on the edge of a basket chair while Mrs. Stubbs pumped the stove even higher. Suddenly Mrs. Stubbs pulled the cushion off a chair and uncovered a large brown paper package.

"I've just had some new photos taken, my dear," she called out cheerfully to Alice. "Tell me what you think of them."

In a very delicate, careful way Alice made the tissue paper damp with her finger and peeled it back from the first one. Goodness! How many there were! There were at least three dozen. And she held it up to the light.

Mrs. Stubbs sat in an armchair, leaning very much to one side. There was a look of mild surprise on her large face, and well there might be. For though the armchair stood on a carpet, to the left of it, remarkably avoiding the carpet edge, there was a rushing waterfall. On her right stood a Greek column with a giant fern tree on either side of it, and in the background rose a tall mountain, pale with snow.

"It's a nice style, isn't it?" Mrs. Stubbs called out. And Alice had just shouted "Lovely!" when the roaring of the gas stove died down, spluttered out, stopped, and she said "Pretty" in a silence that felt frightening.

"Pull up your chair, my dear," said Mrs. Stubbs, beginning to pour the tea. "Yes," she said thoughtfully, as she passed the cup, "but I don't care for the size. I'm having an enlargement made. All very well for Christmas cards, but I never liked small photos myself. You get no real pleasure from them. To tell the truth, I find them discouraging."

Alice completely understood what she meant.

"Size," said Mrs. Stubbs. "Give me size. That was what my poor dear husband always said. He couldn't stand anything small. It made him uncomfortable. And, strange as it may seem, my dear"—here Mrs. Stubbs shifted and seemed to grow larger at the memory—"it was a condition causing water to build up in the body that carried him off at the end. Many times they drained one and a half pints from him at the hospital.... It seemed like a judgement from above."

Alice was burning to know exactly what it was that was drained from him. She tried, "I suppose it was water."

But Mrs. Stubbs fixed Alice with her eyes and replied with meaning, "It was liquid, my dear."

Liquid! Alice jumped away from the word like a cat and came back to it, carefully and cautiously.

"That's him!" said Mrs. Stubbs, and she pointed dramatically to the full-size head and shoulders of a heavily built man with a cold white rose in the buttonhole of his coat that made you think of a curl of cold fat. Just below, in silver letters on a red cardboard background, were the words, "Be not afraid, it is I."

"It's a very strong face," said Alice faintly.

The pale blue bow on the top of Mrs. Stubbs's fair fluffy hair trembled. She lifted her round neck proudly. What a neck she had! It was bright pink where it started and then changed to a warm apricot color, and that faded to the color of a brown egg and then to a deep cream.

"All the same, my dear," she said surprisingly, "freedom's best!" Her soft, fat little laugh sounded like a purr. "Freedom's best," said Mrs. Stubbs again.

Freedom! Alice gave a loud, awkward little laugh. She felt uncomfortable. Her mind flew back to her own kitchen. How very strange! She wanted to be back in it again.
