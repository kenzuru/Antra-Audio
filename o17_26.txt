ABOUT noon the next day the boys arrived at the dead tree again. They had a smoke and a chat in the shade, and then dug a little in their last hole, not with great hope, but just because Tom said there were many cases where people had given up on a treasure after getting down to within six inches of it, and then somebody else had come along and dug it up with a single push of a shovel. This failed again however, so the boys picked up their tools and walked away feeling that they had tried hard and done all the right things for the business of treasure hunting.

When they reached the haunted house there was something so strange and unsettling about the dead silence that lay there under the hot sun, and something so discouraging about the loneliness and emptiness of the place, that they were afraid, for a moment, to go inside. Then they crept to the door and took a shaking look. They saw a weed-covered, empty room, with no wall covering, an old fireplace, empty windows, a falling-apart staircase; and everywhere hung old forgotten cobwebs. They went inside quietly, with faster heartbeats, talking in whispers, ears ready to catch the slightest sound, and muscles tense and ready to run.

After a little while, getting used to the place reduced their fear and they looked around with interest, rather admiring their own courage, and wondering at it too. Next they wanted to look upstairs. This was something like blocking their escape route, but they challenged each other, and of course there could be only one result -- they threw their tools into a corner and went up. Up there were the same signs of age and damage. In one corner they found a closet that seemed mysterious, but the promise was empty -- there was nothing in it. Their courage was now firm and under control. They were about to go down and begin work when --

"Quiet!" said Tom.

"What is it?" whispered Huck, going pale with fright.

"Quiet! ... There! ... Hear it?"

"Yes! ... Oh, no! Let's run!"

"Stay still! Don't move! They're coming right toward the door."

The boys stretched themselves on the floor with their eyes at holes in the floorboards, and lay waiting, terrified.

"They've stopped.... No -- coming.... Here they are. Don't whisper another word, Huck. I really wish I was out of this!"

Two men entered. Each boy said to himself: "There's the old deaf and dumb Spaniard that's been around town once or twice lately -- never saw the other man before."

"The other" was a rough, messy-looking person, with nothing pleasant in his face. The Spaniard was wrapped in a blanket-like cloak; he had thick white whiskers; long white hair flowed from under his wide-brimmed hat, and he wore green glasses. When they came in, "the other" was talking in a low voice; they sat down on the ground, facing the door, with their backs to the wall, and the speaker continued his remarks. He became less careful and his words more clear as he went on:

"No," said he, "I've thought it all over, and I don't like it. It's dangerous."

"Dangerous!" muttered the "deaf and dumb" Spaniard -- to the great surprise of the boys. "Coward!"

This voice made the boys gasp and shake. It was Injun Joe's! There was silence for some time. Then Joe said:

"What's more dangerous than that job up the river -- but nothing's come of it."

"That's different. Way up the river like that, and not another house nearby. Nobody will ever know we tried, anyway, since we didn't succeed."

"Well, what's more dangerous than coming here in the daytime! -- anybody would be suspicious of us who saw us."

"I know that. But there wasn't any other convenient place after that foolish job. I want to leave this shed. I wanted to yesterday, only it wasn't possible to go out, with those annoying boys playing over there on the hill in full view."

"Those annoying boys" trembled again strongly at hearing this, and thought how lucky it was that they had remembered it was Friday and decided to wait a day. They wished with all their hearts they had waited a year.

The two men got out some food and had a meal. After a long and thoughtful silence, Injun Joe said:

"I'm very tired. It's your turn to watch."

He curled down in the weeds and soon began to snore. His companion shook him once or twice and he became quiet. Soon the watcher began to fall asleep; his head drooped lower and lower, both men began to snore now.

The boys breathed a long, thankful breath. Tom whispered:

"Now's our chance -- come!"

Huck said:

"I can't -- I'd die if they woke up."

Tom pushed -- Huck held back. At last Tom rose slowly and softly, and started alone. But the first step he took made such a terrible creak from the old floor that he sank down almost faint with fright. He never made a second attempt. The boys lay there counting the slowly passing moments until it seemed as if time must be over and eternity beginning; and then they were grateful to notice that at last the sun was setting.

Now one snore stopped. Injun Joe sat up, looked around -- smiled grimly upon his companion, whose head was drooping on his knees -- pushed him with his foot and said:

"Hey! YOU'RE supposed to be the watchman! All right, though -- nothing's happened."

"What! Have I been asleep?"

"Oh, partly. Nearly time for us to be moving, partner. What'll we do with the little bit of stolen goods we have left?"

"I don't know -- leave it here as we always do, I suppose. No use taking it away until we start south. Six hundred and fifty in silver is something to carry."

"Well -- all right -- it won't matter to come here once more."

"No -- but I'd say come at night as we used to do -- it's better."

"Yes: but look here; it may be a while before I get the right chance at that job; things could go wrong; it isn't hidden in a very safe place; we'll just bury it properly -- and bury it deep."

"Good idea," said the companion, who walked across the room, knelt down, lifted one of the hearthstones at the back and took out a bag that rattled pleasantly. He took out twenty or thirty dollars for himself and as much for Injun Joe, and passed the bag to the latter, who was on his knees in the corner, digging with his large knife.

The boys forgot all their fears, all their misery in an instant. With greedy eyes they watched every movement. What luck! -- the greatness of it was beyond all imagination! Six hundred dollars was enough money to make half a dozen boys rich! Here was treasure hunting under the best conditions -- there would be no bothersome uncertainty about where to dig. They nudged each other every moment -- expressive nudges and easily understood, for they simply meant -- "Oh, aren't we glad NOW we're here!"

Joe's knife struck upon something.

"Hello!" said he.

"What is it?" said his companion.

"A half-rotten board -- no, it's a box, I think. Here -- give me a hand and we'll see what it's here for. Never mind, I've broken a hole."

He reached his hand in and drew it out --

"Man, it's money!"

The two men looked at the handful of coins. They were gold. The boys above were as excited as the men below, and just as pleased.

Joe's companion said:

"We'll deal with this quickly. There's an old rusty pick over among the weeds in the corner the other side of the fireplace -- I saw it a minute ago."

He ran and brought the boys' pick and shovel. Injun Joe took the pick, looked it over carefully, shook his head, muttered something to himself, and then began to use it. The box was soon dug up. It was not very large; it was bound with metal and had been very strong before the slow years had weakened it. The men looked at the treasure for a while in happy silence.

"Partner, there are thousands of dollars here," said Injun Joe.

"It was always said that Murrel's gang used to be around here one summer," the stranger remarked.

"I know it," said Injun Joe; "and this looks like it, I would say."

"Now you won't need to do that job."

The half-breed frowned. Said he:

"You don't know me. At least you don't know everything about that thing. It isn't robbery only -- it's REVENGE!" and a wicked light flamed in his eyes. "I'll need your help in it. When it's finished -- then Texas. Go home to your family, and wait till you hear from me."

"Well -- if you say so; what'll we do with this -- bury it again?"

"Yes. [Great happiness above.] NO! by God, no! [Deep sadness above.] I almost forgot. That pick had fresh earth on it! [The boys felt sick with fear immediately.] What is a pick and a shovel doing here? What business with fresh earth on them? Who brought them here -- and where have they gone? Have you heard anybody? -- seen anybody? What! bury it again and let them come and see the disturbed ground? Certainly not. We'll take it to my hiding place."

"Why, of course! I should have thought of that before. You mean Number One?"

"No -- Number Two -- under the cross. The other place is bad -- too well known."

"All right. It's almost dark enough to start."

Injun Joe got up and went from window to window, carefully looking out. Then he said:

"Who could have brought those tools here? Do you think they could be upstairs?"

The boys stopped breathing. Injun Joe put his hand on his knife, paused a moment, uncertain, and then turned toward the stairway. The boys thought of the closet, but their strength was gone. The steps came creaking up the stairs -- the unbearable fear of the situation woke a desperate determination in the boys -- they were about to spring for the closet, when there was a crash of rotten wood and Injun Joe landed on the ground among the broken pieces of the ruined stairway. He picked himself up swearing, and his companion said:

"Now what's the use of all that? If it's anyone, and they're up there, let them STAY there -- who cares? If they want to jump down and get into trouble, who objects? It will be dark in fifteen minutes -- and then let them follow us if they want to. I'm willing. In my opinion, whoever threw those things in here saw us and ran away thinking we were ghosts or demons or something. I'll bet they're still running."

Joe grumbled for a while; then he agreed with his friend that what daylight was left ought to be used efficiently in getting ready to leave. Shortly afterward they slipped out of the house in the growing darkness, and moved toward the river with their precious box.

Tom and Huck stood up, weak but greatly relieved, and watched them through the cracks between the logs of the house. Follow? Not them. They were happy just to reach the ground without broken necks, and take the path toward town over the hill. They did not talk much. They were too focused on blaming themselves -- blaming the bad luck that had made them bring the spade and the pick there. Without those tools, Injun Joe would never have been suspicious. He would have left the silver with the gold to wait there until his "revenge" was completed, and then he would have had the bad luck of finding that money gone. Terrible, terrible luck that the tools were ever brought there!

They decided to keep watch for that Spaniard when he came secretly into town looking for a chance to carry out his revenge, and follow him to "Number Two," wherever that might be. Then a terrifying thought came to Tom.

"Revenge? What if he means US, Huck!"

"Oh, no!" said Huck, almost fainting.

They talked it all over, and as they entered town they agreed to believe that he probably meant somebody else -- at least that he might mean nobody but Tom, since only Tom had testified.

Very, very small comfort it was to Tom to be alone in danger! Company would clearly be better, he thought.
