TOM appeared before Aunt Polly, who was sitting by an open window in a pleasant back room, which was bedroom, breakfast room, dining room, and library, all in one. The gentle summer air, the calm quiet, the smell of the flowers, and the sleepy sound of the bees had had their effect, and she was falling asleep over her knitting -- for she had no company but the cat, and it was asleep in her lap. Her glasses were set up on her gray head for safety. She had thought that of course Tom had run off long ago, and she was surprised to see him come back to face her so bravely. He said: "Can I go and play now, aunt?"

"What, already? How much have you done?"

"It's all done, aunt."

"Tom, don't lie to me -- I can't stand it."

"I'm not, aunt; it IS all done."

Aunt Polly had little belief in such a claim. She went out to see for herself; and she would have been satisfied to find twenty percent of Tom's statement true. When she found the entire fence whitewashed, and not only whitewashed but thickly coated and recoated, and even a strip added at the ground, her surprise was almost too great to express. She said:

"Well, I never! There's no getting around it, you can work when you're willing, Tom." And then she softened the praise by adding, "But it's very rare you're willing, I have to say. Well, go along and play; but make sure you're back some time this week, or I'll whip you."

She was so overwhelmed by the greatness of his achievement that she took him into the closet and chose a special apple and gave it to him, along with a lesson about how a treat has better value and flavor when it comes honestly through good effort. And while she closed with a happy quotation from the Bible, he "stole" a doughnut.

Then he skipped out, and saw Sid just starting up the outside stairway that led to the back rooms on the second floor. Lumps of dirt were nearby and the air was full of them in a moment. They flew around Sid like a hailstorm; and before Aunt Polly could pull herself together and rush to the rescue, six or seven clods had hit him, and Tom was over the fence and gone. There was a gate, but as a general thing he was too much in a hurry to use it. His spirit was at peace now, since he had paid Sid back for telling about his black thread and getting him into trouble.

Tom went around the block, and came around into a muddy alley that led by the back of his aunt's cow stable. He soon got safely beyond the reach of capture and punishment, and hurried toward the main square of the village, where two "army" companies of boys had met for battle, according to an earlier plan. Tom was General of one of these armies, Joe Harper (a close friend) General of the other. These two great commanders did not lower themselves to fight in person -- that was better left to the smaller fighters -- but sat together on a high spot and directed the battle by giving orders through helpers. Tom's army won a great victory, after a long and hard-fought battle. Then the dead were counted, prisoners exchanged, the terms of the next fight agreed upon, and the day for the next battle decided; after which the armies fell into line and marched away, and Tom turned homeward alone.

As he was passing by the house where Jeff Thatcher lived, he saw a new girl in the garden -- a lovely little blue-eyed creature with yellow hair braided into two long pigtails, a white summer dress and embroidered trousers. The newly victorious hero fell for her without firing a shot. A certain Amy Lawrence vanished out of his heart and left not even a memory of herself behind. He had thought he loved her like crazy; he had thought his feelings were like worship; and now it was only a poor little quickly fading feeling. He had spent months winning her; she had admitted her feelings hardly a week ago; he had been the happiest and the proudest boy in the world only seven short days, and here in one instant of time she had gone out of his heart like a passing stranger whose visit is done.

He looked at this new angel in secret, until he saw that she had noticed him; then he pretended he did not know she was there, and began to "show off" in all sorts of silly boyish ways, in order to win her admiration. He kept up this strange foolishness for some time; but eventually, while he was in the middle of some dangerous acrobatics, he glanced aside and saw that the little girl was moving toward the house. Tom came up to the fence and leaned on it, sad, and hoping she would wait a little longer. She stopped for a moment on the steps and then moved toward the door. Tom breathed a great sigh as she put her foot on the doorstep. But his face lit up right away, for she threw a pansy over the fence a moment before she disappeared.

The boy ran around and stopped within a foot or two of the flower, and then shaded his eyes with his hand and began to look down the street as if he had noticed something interesting going on in that direction. Soon he picked up a straw and began trying to balance it on his nose, with his head tilted far back; and as he moved from side to side in his efforts, he moved closer and closer toward the pansy; finally his bare foot rested upon it, his toes closed around it, and he hopped away with the treasure and disappeared around the corner. But only for a minute -- only while he could button the flower inside his jacket, next to his heart -- or next to his stomach, possibly, for he did not know much about body structure, and was not very concerned with details, anyway.

He returned now, and hung around the fence until nightfall, "showing off," as before; but the girl never showed herself again, though Tom comforted himself a little with the hope that she had been near some window, meanwhile, and had seen his efforts. Finally he walked home unwillingly, with his poor head full of dreams.

All through supper his spirits were so high that his aunt wondered "what was wrong with the child." He received a good scolding about throwing dirt at Sid, and did not seem to mind it in the least. He tried to steal sugar right under his aunt's nose, and got his knuckles rapped for it. He said:

"Aunt, you don't hit Sid when he takes it."

"Well, Sid doesn't bother a body the way you do. You'd always be into that sugar if I wasn't watching you."

Soon she stepped into the kitchen, and Sid, feeling safe from punishment, reached for the sugar bowl -- a kind of boasting over Tom which was almost too much to stand. But Sid's fingers slipped and the bowl dropped and broke. Tom was overjoyed. So overjoyed that he even kept quiet and said nothing. He said to himself that he would not speak a word, even when his aunt came in, but would sit perfectly still until she asked who did the damage; and then he would tell, and there would be nothing so satisfying in the world as to see that favorite perfect boy "get it." He was so full of excitement that he could barely hold himself together when the old lady came back and stood above the broken pieces, glaring angrily from over her glasses. He said to himself, "Now it's coming!" And the next instant he was falling on the floor! The powerful hand was raised to strike again when Tom cried out:

"Hold on, now, what are you hitting ME for? -- Sid broke it!"

Aunt Polly paused, confused, and Tom looked for comforting sympathy. But when she got her words back, she only said:

"Well! You didn't get a hit that wasn't deserved, I guess. You've been into some other bold mischief when I wasn't around, most likely."

Then her inner sense of right and wrong bothered her, and she felt a strong need to say something kind and loving; but she thought that this would look like admitting she had been wrong, and her role as the person in charge of discipline would not allow that. So she kept silent, and went about her work with a troubled heart. Tom sulked in a corner and made his suffering seem bigger. He knew that in her heart his aunt was sorry, and he was sadly pleased by this knowledge. He would show no signs of forgiveness, he would accept none. He knew that a longing look fell upon him, now and then, through a film of tears, but he refused to notice it. He imagined himself lying sick about to die and his aunt bending over him begging for one little word of forgiveness, but he would turn his face to the wall, and die with that word unsaid. Ah, how would she feel then? And he imagined himself brought home from the river, dead, with his curls all wet, and his sore heart at rest. How she would throw herself upon him, and how her tears would fall like rain, and her lips would pray God to give her back her boy and she would never, never be cruel to him any more! But he would lie there cold and still and make no sign -- a poor little sufferer, whose troubles were at an end. He stirred up his feelings so much with the sad emotion of these dreams, that he had to keep swallowing, he was so close to crying; and his eyes swam in a blur of water, which overflowed when he blinked, and ran down and dripped from the end of his nose. And it was such a pleasure to him to dwell on his sorrows, that he could not stand to have any cheerfulness or any jarring happiness break in upon it; it was too precious for such contact; and so, soon, when his cousin Mary danced in, full of joy at seeing home again after a very long visit of one week to the countryside, he got up and moved in sadness out of one door as she brought song and sunshine in at the other.

He wandered far from the usual places where boys went, and sought lonely places that matched his mood. A log raft in the river attracted him, and he sat on its outer edge and thought deeply about the sad wide space of the river, wishing, meanwhile, that he could only drown, all at once and without knowing it, without going through the uncomfortable process that nature requires. Then he thought of his flower. He got it out, crushed and wilted, and it greatly added to his gloomy happiness. He wondered if she would feel sorry for him if she knew? Would she cry, and wish that she had a right to put her arms around his neck and comfort him? Or would she turn coldly away like all the empty world? This picture brought such an extreme pain of pleasurable sadness that he went over it again and again in his mind and set it up in new and different ways, until he had made it dull and boring. At last he rose up with a sigh and departed in the darkness.

About half past nine or ten o'clock he came along the empty street to where the loved unknown lived; he paused a moment; no sound fell upon his listening ear; a candle was casting a dull glow upon the curtain of a second-story window. Was the holy presence there? He climbed the fence, crept secretly through the plants, until he stood under that window; he looked up at it for a long time, with great emotion; then he lay down on the ground under it, placing himself on his back, with his hands crossed upon his chest and holding his poor wilted flower. And thus he would die -- out in the cold world, with no shelter over his homeless head, no friendly hand to wipe the sweat of death from his brow, no loving face to bend sadly over him when the great agony came. And thus SHE would see him when she looked out upon the bright morning, and oh! would she drop one little tear upon his poor, lifeless body, would she breathe one little sigh to see a bright young life so badly ruined, so early cut down?

The window went up, a servant's harsh voice ruined the holy calm, and a flood of water soaked the lying-down victim!

The shocked hero sprang up with a gasp. There was a sound as of something flying through the air, mixed with the sound of a curse, a sound as of breaking glass followed, and a small, dark form went over the fence and shot away in the darkness.

Not long after, as Tom, all undressed for bed, was looking at his soaked clothes by the light of a cheap candle, Sid woke up; but if he had any idea of making any remarks about past events, he thought better of it and kept quiet, for there was danger in Tom's eye.

Tom went to bed without the extra trouble of saying prayers, and Sid made a mental note of the thing left out.
