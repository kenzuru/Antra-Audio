A large rose-tree stood near the entrance of the garden. The roses growing on it were naturally white, but three gardeners were working busily, painting them red. Alice thought this was a very strange thing to be doing, and she moved closer to watch them. Just as she came up to them, she heard one of them say, “Watch what you’re doing now, Five! Don’t go splashing paint all over me like that!”

“I couldn’t help it,” said Five, in a sulky tone. “Seven jogged my elbow.”

Hearing this, Seven looked up and said, “That’s typical, Five! Always find a way to blame others!”

“You’d better not talk!” said Five sharply. “I heard the Queen say only yesterday that you deserved to be beheaded!”

“What for?” said the one who had spoken first.

“That’s none of your business, Two!” said Seven.

“Yes, it is his business!” said Five. “And I’ll tell him—it was for bringing the Cook tulip-roots instead of onions.”

Seven angrily flung down his brush and had just begun, “Well, of all the unfair things—” when his eye happened to fall upon Alice, who was standing there watching them. He checked himself immediately. The others looked around too, and all three of them bowed very low to her.

“Would you mind telling me,” said Alice, a little timidly, “why exactly you are painting those roses?”

Five and Seven said nothing, but looked nervously at Two. Two began to speak in a low voice, “Well, the fact is, you see, Miss, this here ought to have been a red rose-tree, and we put a white one in by mistake. And if the Queen were to find out, we should all have our heads cut off, you know. So you see, Miss, we’re doing our very best, before she arrives, to—” At that moment, Five, who had been anxiously looking across the garden, shouted out, “The Queen! The Queen!” and the three gardeners instantly threw themselves flat upon their faces. There was the sound of many approaching footsteps, and Alice looked around, eager to see the Queen.

First came ten soldiers carrying clubs. These were all shaped like the three gardeners, oblong and flat, with their hands and feet at the corners. Next came the ten courtiers. These were decorated all over with diamonds and walked two by two, just like the soldiers. After them came the royal children; there were ten of them, and the little darlings came jumping merrily along hand in hand, in pairs. They were all decorated with hearts. Next came the guests, mostly Kings and Queens, and among them, Alice immediately recognized the White Rabbit. He was talking in a hurried, nervous manner, smiling widely at everything that was said, and passed by without noticing her. Then followed the Knave of Hearts, carrying the King’s crown on a crimson velvet cushion. And, last of all in this grand procession, came THE KING AND QUEEN OF HEARTS.

Alice was rather uncertain whether she ought to lie down on her face like the three gardeners, but she couldn't remember ever having heard of such a rule during processions. “And besides, what would be the purpose of a procession,” she thought, “if everyone had to lie down on their faces, so they couldn’t see it?” So she stood still where she was and waited.

When the procession reached the spot opposite Alice, they all stopped and looked at her, and the Queen said severely, “Who is this?” She said it to the Knave of Hearts, who only bowed and smiled in reply.

“Idiot!” said the Queen, impatiently tossing her head. Then, turning to Alice, she went on, “What is your name, child?”

“My name is Alice, if it pleases your Majesty,” said Alice very politely. But she added to herself, “Why, they’re only a pack of cards, after all. I don’t need to be afraid of them!”

“And who are these?” said the Queen, pointing to the three gardeners who were lying flat around the rose-tree. For, you see, since they were lying on their faces, and the pattern on their backs was the same as the rest of the playing card pack, she could not tell if they were gardeners, soldiers, courtiers, or three of her own children.

“How should I know?” said Alice, surprised by her own sudden courage. “It’s no business of mine.”

The Queen turned bright crimson with rage, and after glaring at Alice for a moment like a wild animal, she screamed, “Off with her head! Off—”

“Nonsense!” said Alice, very loudly and decidedly, and the Queen immediately fell silent.

The King timidly placed his hand on the Queen’s arm and said softly, “Consider, my dear: she is only a child!”

The Queen angrily turned away from him and said to the Knave, “Turn them over!”

The Knave did so, very carefully, using one foot.

“Get up!” said the Queen in a shrill, loud voice, and the three gardeners instantly jumped up and began bowing frantically to the King, the Queen, the royal children, and everyone else.

“Stop doing that!” screamed the Queen. “You make me dizzy.” And then, turning her attention to the rose-tree, she went on, “What have you been doing here?”

“May it please your Majesty,” said Two, in a very humble tone, going down on one knee as he spoke, “we were merely trying—”

“I see!” said the Queen, who had in the meantime been closely examining the roses. “Off with their heads!” And the procession moved forward, with three of the soldiers remaining behind to execute the unfortunate gardeners, who immediately ran to Alice for protection.

“You shall not be beheaded!” said Alice, and she quickly put them into a large terracotta flower-pot that was standing nearby. The three soldiers wandered about the area for a minute or two, looking for them, and then quietly marched off to join the others.

“Are their heads off?” shouted the Queen.

“Their heads are gone, if it please your Majesty!” the soldiers shouted back in reply.

“That’s right!” shouted the Queen. “Can you play croquet?”

The soldiers were silent and all looked at Alice, as the question was obviously meant for her.

“Yes!” shouted Alice.

“Come on, then!” roared the Queen, and Alice joined the procession, wondering very much what would happen next.

“It’s—it’s a very fine day!” said a timid voice at her side. She was walking beside the White Rabbit, who was anxiously peering up into her face.

“Very,” said Alice. “—Where is the Duchess?”

“Hush! Hush!” said the Rabbit in a low, hurried tone. He looked anxiously over his shoulder as he spoke, then rose up on tiptoe, put his mouth close to her ear, and whispered, “She’s under sentence of execution.”

“What for?” said Alice.

“Did you say ‘What a pity!’?” the Rabbit asked.

“No, I didn’t,” said Alice. “I don’t think it’s at all a pity. I said, ‘What for?’”

“She boxed the Queen’s ears—” the Rabbit began. Alice let out a little suppressed scream of laughter. “Oh, hush!” the Rabbit whispered in a terrified tone. “The Queen will certainly hear you! You see, she arrived rather late, and the Queen said—”

“Get to your places!” shouted the Queen in a voice of thunder, and people began running about in all directions, tumbling up against each other. However, they got settled down in a minute or two, and the game began. Alice thought she had never seen such a strange croquet-ground in her life. It was all ridges and furrows; the balls were live hedgehogs, the mallets were live flamingoes, and the soldiers had to double themselves up and stand on their hands and feet to form the arches.

The main difficulty Alice had at first was controlling her flamingo. She managed to get its body tucked away comfortably enough under her arm, with its legs hanging down, but generally, just as she had gotten its long neck nicely straightened out and was about to hit the hedgehog with its head, the flamingo would twist itself right around and look up into her face with such a puzzled expression that she couldn't help bursting out laughing. And when she finally got its head pointed down again and was ready to try once more, it was very annoying to discover that the hedgehog had unrolled itself and was in the act of crawling away. Besides all this, there was usually a ridge or a furrow right in the way wherever she wanted to send the hedgehog, and, as the doubled-up soldiers were always standing up and walking off to other parts of the playing field, Alice quickly concluded that it was an extremely difficult game indeed.

The players all played at once without waiting for turns, quarreling furiously the whole time and fighting over the hedgehogs. In a very short time, the Queen was in a towering passion, stamping about and shouting “Off with his head!” or “Off with her head!” about once every minute.

Alice began to feel very uneasy. To be sure, she had not yet had any direct argument with the Queen, but she knew that it might happen at any second. “And then,” she thought, “what on earth would happen to me? They are dreadfully fond of beheading people here; the great wonder is that there is anyone left alive at all!”

She was anxiously looking around for some way to escape and wondering if she could slip away without being seen, when she noticed a strange shape in the air. It greatly puzzled her at first, but after watching it for a minute or two, she recognized it as a grin, and she said to herself, “It’s the Cheshire Cat. Now I shall have somebody I can actually talk to.”

“How are you getting on with the game?” said the Cat, as soon as there was enough mouth visible for it to speak with.

Alice waited until the eyes appeared, and then she nodded. “It’s no use trying to speak to it,” she thought, “until its ears have appeared, or at least one of them.” In another minute, the entire head materialized, and then Alice put down her flamingo and began to tell the Cat all about the game, feeling extremely glad she had someone to listen to her. The Cat seemed to think that there was enough of it visible now, and no more of its body appeared.

“I don’t think they play fairly at all,” Alice began, in a rather complaining tone, “and they all quarrel so dreadfully that one can’t hear oneself speak—and they don’t seem to have any specific rules; at least, if there are, nobody pays attention to them. And you have no idea how confusing it is with all the things being alive! For instance, the arch I have to go through next is walking about at the far end of the ground—and I would have croqueted the Queen’s hedgehog just now, only it ran away when it saw mine coming toward it!”

“How do you like the Queen?” said the Cat in a low, conspiratorial voice.

“Not at all,” said Alice. “She’s so extremely—” Just then, she noticed that the Queen was standing very close behind her, clearly listening. So she quickly finished her sentence, “—likely to win, that it’s hardly worth the effort of finishing the game.”

The Queen smiled faintly and walked on.

“Who are you talking to?” said the King, walking up to Alice and looking at the Cat’s head with great curiosity.

“It’s a friend of mine—a Cheshire Cat,” said Alice. “Allow me to introduce it.”

“I don’t like the look of it in the slightest,” said the King. “However, it may kiss my hand if it chooses.”

“I’d rather not,” the Cat remarked plainly.

“Don’t be rude,” said the King, getting angry, “and don’t look at me like that!” He quickly hid behind Alice as he spoke.

“A cat may look at a king,” said Alice. “I’ve read that in some book, but I don’t remember where.”

“Well, it must be removed immediately,” said the King very decisively, and he called the Queen, who was passing at that moment, “My dear! I wish you would have this Cat removed!”

The Queen had only one way of solving every difficulty, large or small. “Off with his head!” she said, without even turning to look.

“I’ll fetch the executioner myself right away,” said the King eagerly, and he hurried off.

Alice thought she might as well go back and see how the game was progressing, as she could hear the Queen’s voice in the distance, screaming in a furious passion. She had already heard the Queen sentence three more of the players to be executed for having missed their turns, and she did not like the look of things at all, since the game was in such confusion that she never knew whether it was her turn or not. So she went in search of her hedgehog.

The hedgehog was currently involved in a fight with another hedgehog, which seemed to Alice an excellent opportunity for her to use her flamingo to croquet one of them with the other. The only problem was that her flamingo had crossed over to the other side of the garden, where Alice could see it trying in a helpless sort of way to fly up into a tree.

By the time she had chased down the flamingo and brought it back, the fight was over, and both the hedgehogs were out of sight. “But it doesn’t matter very much,” thought Alice, “as all the arches are gone from this side of the ground anyway.” So she tucked the flamingo securely under her arm, so that it could not escape again, and went back for a little more conversation with her friend.

When she returned to the Cheshire Cat, she was surprised to find a quite large crowd gathered around it. There was a fierce dispute going on between the executioner, the King, and the Queen, who were all talking at once, while all the rest of the party remained completely silent and looked very anxious.

The moment Alice appeared, she was appealed to by all three of them to settle the question, and they all repeated their arguments to her, though, as they were all speaking simultaneously, she found it very hard indeed to make out exactly what they were saying.

The executioner’s argument was that you absolutely could not cut off a head unless there was a body to cut it off from; that he had never had to do such a thing before, and he certainly was not going to start at his time of life.

The King’s argument was that anything that had a head could be beheaded, and that the executioner was not to talk nonsense.

The Queen’s argument was that if something was not done about the situation in less than no time, she would have absolutely everybody executed, all around the ground. (It was this last remark that had made the whole party look so serious and worried.)

Alice could think of nothing else to say but, “It belongs to the Duchess: you’d better ask her about it.”

“She’s in prison,” the Queen said to the executioner. “Fetch her here.” And the executioner ran off like an arrow.

The Cat’s head began to fade away the moment he was gone, and by the time the executioner had come back with the Duchess, the head had entirely disappeared. So the King and the executioner ran wildly up and down looking for it, while the rest of the party went back to the game.
