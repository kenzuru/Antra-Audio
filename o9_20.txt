

While these events were happening at the opium den, Mr. Fogg, with no idea of the danger he was in of missing the steamer, was calmly walking Aouda around the streets of the English part of the city, making the purchases needed for the long voyage ahead. It was perfectly reasonable for an Englishman like Mr. Fogg to travel around the world with just a carpet bag. But a lady could not be expected to travel comfortably under such conditions. He carried out this task with his usual calm, and always replied politely to his companion's protests — she was embarrassed by his patience and generosity:

"It is in the interest of my journey — a part of my plan."

Having finished the shopping, they returned to the hotel, where they had dinner at a table that was richly and carefully set. Afterwards Aouda, shaking hands with her protector in the English manner, went to her room to rest. Mr. Fogg spent the whole evening reading the Times and Illustrated London News.

Had he been the kind of person who could be surprised by anything, it would have been by his servant's failure to return at bedtime. But, knowing that the steamer was not due to leave for Yokohama until the next morning, he was not troubled about the matter. When Passepartout still had not appeared the next morning to answer his master's bell, Mr. Fogg, without showing the least annoyance, simply took his carpet bag, called for Aouda, and sent for a carriage.

It was eight o'clock. At half past nine, when the tide would be high, the "Carnatic" would leave the harbour. Mr. Fogg and Aouda got into the carriage, with their luggage brought after them on a wheelbarrow, and half an hour later stepped onto the dock from which they were to set sail. Mr. Fogg then learned that the "Carnatic" had left the previous evening. He had expected to find not only the steamer but his servant, and was now without both. But no sign of disappointment appeared on his face. He simply remarked to Aouda, "It is an accident, madam. Nothing more."

At that moment a man who had been watching him carefully came forward. It was Fix, who, bowing, addressed Mr. Fogg: "Were you not, like me, sir, a passenger on the 'Rangoon,' which arrived yesterday?"

"I was, sir," replied Mr. Fogg coldly. "But I do not have the pleasure of—"

"Excuse me. I thought I would find your servant here."

"Do you know where he is, sir?" asked Aouda anxiously.

"What!" replied Fix, pretending to be surprised. "Is he not with you?"

"No," said Aouda. "He has not been seen since yesterday. Could he have gone on board the 'Carnatic' without us?"

"Without you, madam?" the detective replied. "Excuse me — did you plan to sail on the 'Carnatic'?"

"Yes, sir."

"So did I, madam, and I am greatly disappointed. The 'Carnatic,' its repairs being finished, left Hong Kong twelve hours ahead of schedule, without any warning being given. Now we must wait a week for another steamer."

As he said "a week," Fix felt his heart jump with joy. Fogg stuck in Hong Kong for a week! There would be time for the warrant to arrive, and fortune was at last smiling on the representative of the law. His shock may be imagined when he heard Mr. Fogg say, in his calm voice, "But there are other vessels besides the 'Carnatic,' it seems to me, in the harbour of Hong Kong."

And, offering his arm to Aouda, he walked towards the docks looking for some ship about to depart. Fix, stunned, followed — he seemed to be attached to Mr. Fogg by an invisible string. Luck, however, seemed to have truly abandoned the man it had served so well until now. For three hours Phileas Fogg walked around the docks, determined, if necessary, to hire a vessel to take him to Yokohama. But he could only find ships that were loading or unloading, and which could not therefore set sail. Fix began to hope again.

But Mr. Fogg, far from being discouraged, kept searching, determined not to give up even if he had to go all the way to Macao. Then a sailor on one of the docks came up to him.

"Is your honour looking for a boat?"

"Have you a boat ready to sail?"

"Yes, your honour — a pilot boat, number 43 — the best in the harbour."

"Does she go fast?"

"Between eight and nine miles an hour. Would you like to see her?"

"Yes."

"Your honour will be satisfied with her. Is it for a short trip at sea?"

"No, for a voyage."

"A voyage?"

"Yes. Will you take me to Yokohama?"

The sailor leaned on the railing, opened his eyes wide, and said, "Is your honour joking?"

"No. I have missed the 'Carnatic,' and I must reach Yokohama by the 14th at the latest, to catch the boat for San Francisco."

"I am sorry," said the sailor. "But it is impossible."

"I will pay you a hundred pounds per day, and an extra two hundred pounds if I reach Yokohama in time."

"Are you serious?"

"Very much so."

The pilot walked a short distance away and looked out to sea, clearly struggling between the desire to earn a large sum and the fear of sailing so far. Fix was in terrible suspense.

Mr. Fogg turned to Aouda and asked, "You would not be frightened, would you, madam?"

"Not with you, Mr. Fogg," was her reply.

The pilot returned, turning his hat in his hands.

"Well, pilot?" said Mr. Fogg.

"Well, your honour," he replied, "I could not risk my own life, the lives of my men, or my little boat of barely twenty tons on such a long voyage at this time of year. Besides, we could not reach Yokohama in time, since it is sixteen hundred and sixty miles from Hong Kong."

"Only sixteen hundred," said Mr. Fogg.

"It's the same thing."

Fix breathed more easily.

"But," added the pilot, "it might be arranged another way."

Fix stopped breathing again.

"How?" asked Mr. Fogg.

"By going to Nagasaki, at the southern tip of Japan, or even to Shanghai, which is only eight hundred miles from here. By going to Shanghai we would not have to sail away from the Chinese coast, which would be a great advantage, as the sea currents run northward and would help us."

"Pilot," said Mr. Fogg, "I must take the American steamer at Yokohama, not at Shanghai or Nagasaki."

"Why not?" returned the pilot. "The San Francisco steamer does not start from Yokohama. It stops at Yokohama and Nagasaki, but it actually starts from Shanghai."

"You are certain of that?"

"Completely."

"And when does the boat leave Shanghai?"

"On the 11th, at seven in the evening. We therefore have four days, that is ninety-six hours. In that time, if we had good luck and a south-west wind and a calm sea, we could cover those eight hundred miles to Shanghai."

"And you could leave—"

"In an hour, as soon as food could be put on board and the sails put up."

"It's a deal. Are you the master of the boat?"

"Yes. John Bunsby, master of the 'Tankadere.'"

"Would you like some payment in advance?"

"If it would not trouble your honour—"

"Here are two hundred pounds as a deposit." Then, turning to Fix, Mr. Fogg said, "If you would like to take advantage—"

"Thank you, sir. I was just about to ask."

"Very well. In half an hour we shall go on board."

"But poor Passepartout?" said Aouda, who was very troubled by the servant's disappearance.

"I will do everything I can to find him," replied Phileas Fogg.

While Fix, in a restless and nervous state, went to the pilot boat, the others headed for the police station in Hong Kong. Phileas Fogg gave them Passepartout's description and left a sum of money to be spent on searching for him. After going through the same process at the French consulate, and after the carriage had stopped at the hotel to pick up the luggage that had been sent back there, they returned to the dock.

It was now three o'clock, and pilot boat number 43, with its crew on board and its supplies stored away, was ready to depart.

The "Tankadere" was a neat little vessel of twenty tons, built as beautifully as if she were a racing yacht. Her shining copper casing, her metal fittings, and her deck, white as ivory, showed the care that John Bunsby had taken to keep her looking smart. Her two masts leaned slightly backwards. She carried several different sails and was well set up for sailing before the wind. She was clearly capable of great speed, as she had already proved by winning several prizes in pilot boat races. The crew of the "Tankadere" consisted of John Bunsby, the master, and four strong sailors who knew the Chinese seas well. John Bunsby himself, a man of about forty-five, vigorous and sun-darkened, with bright eyes and a confident, self-reliant face, would have inspired trust in even the most nervous person.

Phileas Fogg and Aouda went on board, where they found Fix already settled in. Below deck was a square cabin, whose walls curved out in the shape of beds, above a round seat that went all the way around. In the centre was a table with a hanging lamp. The space was small but tidy.

"I am sorry to have nothing better to offer you," said Mr. Fogg to Fix, who bowed without answering.

The detective felt something close to shame at accepting the kindness of Mr. Fogg.

"It is certain," he thought, "that whatever else he may be, he is a polite one!"

The sails and the English flag were raised at ten minutes past three. Mr. Fogg and Aouda, seated on deck, took one last look at the dock, hoping to catch sight of Passepartout. Fix was not without his fears that chance might bring the unfortunate servant — whom he had treated so badly — to this spot. In that case, an explanation very unfavourable to the detective would surely have followed. But the Frenchman did not appear, and without doubt was still lying unconscious under the effects of the opium.

John Bunsby, master, finally gave the order to depart, and the "Tankadere," catching the wind in her sails, leapt forward quickly over the waves.
