

The "Rangoon" — one of the ships of the Peninsular and Oriental Company, sailing in the Chinese and Japanese seas — was a metal steamship with a propeller, weighing about seventeen hundred and seventy tons, with engines of four hundred horse-power. She was as fast as the "Mongolia," but not as well equipped, and Aouda was not as comfortably looked after on board as Phileas Fogg would have wished. However, the journey from Calcutta to Hong Kong was only about three thousand five hundred miles, taking ten to twelve days, and the young woman was not hard to please.

During the first days of the journey, Aouda got to know her protector better, and she constantly showed how deeply grateful she was for what he had done for her. The quiet gentleman listened to her, at least on the surface, with coldness. Neither his voice nor his manner showed the slightest emotion. But he seemed always to be watching carefully to make sure that nothing Aouda needed was missing. He visited her regularly each day at set times, not so much to talk himself, but to sit and listen to her talk. He treated her with complete politeness, but with the exact regularity of a machine that had been set up for this purpose. Aouda was not quite sure what to think of him, although Passepartout had given her some idea of his master's unusual ways, and had made her smile by telling her about the bet that was sending him around the world. After all, she owed Phileas Fogg her life, and she always saw him through the warm light of her gratitude.

Aouda confirmed the Parsee guide's story of her sad history. She did indeed belong to the highest of the native peoples of India. Many Parsee merchants had made great fortunes in India through the cotton trade. One of them, Sir Jametsee Jeejeebhoy, had been made a knight by the English government. Aouda was a relative of this important man, and it was his cousin, Jeejeeh, whom she hoped to find in Hong Kong. Whether she would find a protector in him, she could not say. But Mr. Fogg tried to calm her worries, assuring her that everything would be arranged mathematically — he used that exact word. Aouda fixed her large eyes — "clear as the sacred lakes of the Himalaya" — upon him. But the firm Fogg, as private as ever, did not seem in the least ready to throw himself into those eyes.

The first few days of the voyage went well, with good weather and helpful winds. They soon came in sight of the large Andaman island, the main island in the Bay of Bengal, with its beautiful Saddle Peak, two thousand four hundred feet high, rising above the water. The steamer passed close to the shore, but the native Papuans, who are among the most basic people in the world, but who are not cannibals as some have wrongly claimed, did not appear.

The view of the islands as they sailed past was wonderful. Vast forests of palms, areca, bamboo, teak, giant mimosa, and tree-like ferns filled the foreground, while behind them the graceful shapes of mountains were drawn against the sky. Along the coasts, thousands of valuable swallows flew, whose nests provide a rich dish for the tables of China. But the Andaman Islands were soon left behind, and the "Rangoon" moved quickly towards the Strait of Malacca, which leads into the Chinese seas.

What was detective Fix, so unluckily pulled from country to country, doing all this time? He had managed to get onto the "Rangoon" at Calcutta without being seen by Passepartout. He had left instructions that if the warrant arrived, it should be sent on to him in Hong Kong. He hoped to stay hidden until the end of the voyage. It would have been difficult to explain why he was on board without making Passepartout suspicious, since the servant thought he had stayed in Bombay. But he was forced, despite this, to renew his acquaintance with the good servant, as will be seen.

All of Fix's hopes were now focused on Hong Kong. The stop at Singapore would be too short for him to do anything there. The arrest had to be made in Hong Kong, or the man he was chasing would probably escape him forever. Hong Kong was the last English place he would reach. Beyond it, China, Japan, and America offered Fogg an almost certain place to hide. If the warrant finally arrived at Hong Kong, Fix could arrest him and hand him to the local police, and there would be no more trouble. But beyond Hong Kong, a simple arrest warrant would be useless. An extradition warrant would be needed, which would cause delays and problems that a clever criminal would use to escape from justice.

Fix thought over these possibilities during the long hours he spent in his cabin, repeating to himself: "Either the warrant will be at Hong Kong, in which case I will arrest my man. Or it will not be there, and this time I must absolutely prevent his departure. I have failed at Bombay, and I have failed at Calcutta. If I fail at Hong Kong, my reputation is finished. Whatever the cost, I must succeed! But how can I stop his departure, if that turns out to be my only option?"

Fix decided that if the worst came to the worst, he would share everything with Passepartout and tell him what kind of man his master really was. He was quite certain that Passepartout was not Fogg's partner in crime. The servant, once told the truth, and afraid of being caught up in the crime himself, would no doubt become an ally of the detective. But this approach was dangerous and should only be used when everything else had failed. A single word from Passepartout to his master would ruin everything. The detective was therefore in a very difficult position. But suddenly a new idea came to him. The presence of Aouda on the "Rangoon," travelling with Phileas Fogg, gave him new things to think about.

Who was this woman? What series of events had made her Fogg's travel companion? They had clearly met somewhere between Bombay and Calcutta, but where? Had they met by chance, or had Fogg deliberately gone into the interior to find this attractive young woman? Fix was very confused. He wondered whether there had not been a secret escape together, and this thought became so strong in his mind that he decided to use this supposed relationship. Whether the young woman was married or not, he would be able to create such difficulties for Mr. Fogg at Hong Kong that he could not escape by paying any amount of money.

But could he even wait until they reached Hong Kong? Fogg had an annoying habit of jumping from one ship to another, and before anything could be done, he might already be on his way to Yokohama.

Fix decided he must warn the English authorities and alert them about the "Rangoon" before she arrived. This was easy to do, since the steamer stopped at Singapore, from where there was a telegraph line to Hong Kong. He also decided, before taking stronger action, to speak with Passepartout. It would not be hard to make him talk. And since there was no time to lose, Fix prepared to make himself known.

It was now October 30th, and the next day the "Rangoon" was due at Singapore.

Fix came out of his cabin and went up on deck. Passepartout was walking back and forth in the front part of the steamer. The detective rushed forward, looking very surprised, and exclaimed, "You here, on the 'Rangoon'?"

"What, Mr. Fix, you are on board?" returned the truly surprised Passepartout, recognising his friend from the "Mongolia." "Why, I last saw you in Bombay, and here you are on the way to Hong Kong! Are you also going around the world?"

"No, no," replied Fix. "I will stop at Hong Kong — at least for a few days."

"I see," said Passepartout, who seemed confused for a moment. "But how is it that I haven't seen you on board since we left Calcutta?"

"Oh, a little seasickness — I have been staying in my cabin. The Bay of Bengal does not suit me as well as the Indian Ocean. And how is Mr. Fogg?"

"As well and as punctual as ever, not a day behind schedule! But, Mr. Fix, you don't know that we have a young lady with us."

"A young lady?" replied the detective, pretending not to understand.

Passepartout then told Fix about Aouda's story: the incident at the Bombay temple, the purchase of the elephant for two thousand pounds, the rescue, the arrest, the sentence at the Calcutta court, and how Mr. Fogg and himself had been freed on bail. Fix, who already knew about the last events, pretended to know nothing about any of what Passepartout described. The servant was happy to have such an interested listener.

"But does your master plan to take this young woman to Europe?"

"Not at all. We are simply going to place her under the protection of one of her relatives, a rich merchant in Hong Kong."

"Nothing to be done there," Fix said to himself, hiding his disappointment. "A glass of gin, Mr. Passepartout?"

"Gladly, Mr. Fix. We must at least have a friendly drink on board the 'Rangoon.'"
