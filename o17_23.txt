AT last the quiet mood of the town was stirred -- and strongly: the murder trial began in court. It immediately became the most talked about topic in the village. Tom could not get away from it. Every mention of the murder sent a shudder through his heart, for his troubled conscience and his fears almost convinced him that these remarks were made in his hearing as tests; he could not see how anyone could suspect him of knowing anything about the murder, but still he could not feel comfortable in the middle of all this talk. It kept him in a cold shiver all the time. He took Huck to a lonely place to have a talk with him. It would be some relief to speak freely for a little while; to share his burden of distress with another who suffered too. Moreover, he wanted to make sure that Huck had stayed quiet.

"Huck, have you ever told anybody about -- that?"

"About what?"

"You know what."

"Oh -- of course I haven't."

"Never a word?"

"Never a single word, I promise. What makes you ask?"

"Well, I was worried."

"Why, Tom Sawyer, we wouldn't be alive two days if that got found out. YOU know that."

Tom felt more comfortable. After a pause:

"Huck, they couldn't get you to tell, could they?"

"Get me to tell? Why, if I wanted that half-breed devil to drown me they could get me to tell. There's no other way."

"Well, that's all right then. I reckon we're safe as long as we keep quiet. But let's swear again, anyway. It's more certain."

"Agreed."

So they swore again with great seriousness.

"What is the talk around, Huck? I've heard a lot of it."

"Talk? Well, it's just Muff Potter, Muff Potter, Muff Potter all the time. It keeps me nervous constantly, so that I want to hide somewhere."

"That's just the same way they talk around me. I reckon he's done for. Don't you feel sorry for him sometimes?"

"Almost always -- almost always. He isn't much; but he's never done anything to hurt anybody. Just fishes a little, to get money to get drunk on -- and goes about; but we all do that -- at least most of us -- preachers and such like. But he's kind of good -- he gave me half a fish once, when there wasn't enough for two; and many times he's been kind to me when I was having bad luck."

"Well, he's fixed kites for me, Huck, and tied hooks on to my fishing line. I wish we could get him out of there."

"My! We couldn't get him out, Tom. And besides, it wouldn't do any good; they'd catch him again."

"Yes -- so they would. But I hate to hear them talk badly about him when he never did -- that."

"I do too, Tom. Lord, I hear them say he's the most bloodthirsty looking criminal in this area, and they wonder he was never hanged before."

"Yes, they talk like that all the time. I've heard them say that if he were to go free they'd lynch him."

"And they'd do it, too."

The boys had a long talk, but it brought them little comfort. As the evening drew on, they found themselves near the small lonely jail, perhaps with an unclear hope that something would happen that might clear away their difficulties. But nothing happened; there seemed to be no angels or fairies interested in this unlucky prisoner.

The boys did as they had done before -- went to the cell window and gave Potter some tobacco and matches. He was on the ground floor and there were no guards.

His gratitude for their gifts had always made their consciences hurt -- it hurt deeper than ever this time. They felt cowardly and disloyal to the highest degree when Potter said:

"You've been very good to me, boys -- better than anybody else in this town. And I don't forget it, I don't. Often I say to myself, 'I used to fix all the boys' kites and things, and show them where the good fishing places were, and be friendly to them as much as I could, and now they've all forgotten old Muff when he's in trouble; but Tom doesn't, and Huck doesn't -- THEY don't forget him,' I say, 'and I don't forget them.' Well, boys, I did a terrible thing -- drunk and out of my mind at the time -- that's the only way I can explain it -- and now I've got to die for it, and it's right. Right, and BEST, too, I think -- I hope so anyway. Well, we won't talk about that. I don't want to make YOU feel bad; you've been good to me. But what I want to say is, don't YOU ever get drunk -- then you won't ever end up here. Stand a little further west -- so -- that's it; it's a wonderful comfort to see friendly faces when a person's in such a bad situation, and nobody comes here but yours. Good friendly faces -- good friendly faces. Get up on each other's backs and let me touch them. That's it. Shake hands -- yours will come through the bars, but mine are too big. Little hands, and weak -- but they've helped Muff Potter a lot, and they'd help him more if they could."

Tom went home miserable, and his dreams that night were full of horrors. The next day and the day after, he stayed near the courtroom, pulled by an almost irresistible urge to go in, but forcing himself to stay out. Huck was having the same experience. They carefully avoided each other. Each wandered away from time to time, but the same sad attraction always brought them back. Tom kept his ears open when people came out of the courtroom, but always heard bad news -- the case was closing more and more tightly around poor Potter. At the end of the second day the village talk was that Injun Joe's testimony stood firm and unshaken, and that there was no doubt about what the jury's decision would be.

Tom was out late that night, and came to bed through the window. He was in a state of great excitement. It was hours before he got to sleep. All the village gathered at the courthouse the next morning, for this was to be the great day. Both men and women were about equally represented in the packed audience. After a long wait the jury came in and took their places; shortly afterward, Potter, pale and worn, frightened and without hope, was brought in with chains on him, and seated where all the curious eyes could stare at him; just as noticeable was Injun Joe, as unmoved as ever. There was another pause, and then the judge arrived and the sheriff announced the opening of the court. The usual whispering among the lawyers and gathering of papers followed. These details and the delays that went with them built up an atmosphere of preparation that was both impressive and fascinating.

Now a witness was called who said he found Muff Potter washing in the stream at an early hour of the morning that the murder was discovered, and that he immediately slipped away. After some further questioning, the lawyer for the prosecution said:

"Take the witness."

The prisoner looked up for a moment, but dropped his eyes again when his own lawyer said:

"I have no questions to ask him."

The next witness showed the knife found near the body. The lawyer for the prosecution said:

"Take the witness."

"I have no questions to ask him," Potter's lawyer replied.

A third witness said he had often seen the knife in Potter's possession.

"Take the witness."

Potter's lawyer refused to question him. The faces of the audience began to show frustration. Did this lawyer mean to throw away his client's life without any effort?

Several witnesses testified about Potter's guilty behavior when brought to the scene of the murder. They were allowed to leave without being questioned.

Every detail of the damaging events that took place in the graveyard that morning, which everyone present remembered so well, was brought out by believable witnesses, but none of them were questioned by Potter's lawyer. The confusion and dissatisfaction of the crowd expressed itself in murmurs and received a warning from the judge. The lawyer for the prosecution now said:

"By the sworn words of citizens whose honest word is beyond question, we have proven this terrible crime, beyond all possibility of doubt, against the unhappy prisoner at the bar. We rest our case here."

A cry escaped from poor Potter, and he put his face in his hands and rocked his body gently back and forth, while a painful silence filled the courtroom. Many men were moved, and many women's sympathy showed itself in tears. The defense lawyer rose and said:

"Your honor, in our opening remarks at the start of this trial, we said we would prove that our client committed this terrible act while under the influence of a complete and uncontrollable confusion produced by drink. We have changed our mind. We will not offer that argument." [Then to the clerk:] "Call Thomas Sawyer!"

A confused amazement appeared on every face in the room, not even excluding Potter's. Every eye fixed itself with wondering interest upon Tom as he rose and took his place on the witness stand. The boy looked very shaken, for he was badly scared. The oath was given.

"Thomas Sawyer, where were you on the seventeenth of June, around midnight?"

Tom glanced at Injun Joe's hard face and his tongue stopped working. The audience listened without breathing, but the words would not come. After a few moments, however, the boy recovered a little of his strength, and managed to say loudly enough for part of the room to hear:

"In the graveyard!"

"A little bit louder, please. Don't be afraid. You were --"

"In the graveyard."

A look of contempt crossed Injun Joe's face.

"Were you anywhere near Horse Williams' grave?"

"Yes, sir."

"Speak up -- just a little louder. How near were you?"

"As near as I am to you."

"Were you hidden, or not?"

"I was hidden."

"Where?"

"Behind the elm trees at the edge of the grave."

Injun Joe gave a barely noticeable start.

"Anyone with you?"

"Yes, sir. I went there with --"

"Wait -- wait a moment. Don't mention your companion's name. We will bring him forward at the right time. Did you take anything there with you?"

Tom hesitated and looked confused.

"Speak up, my boy -- don't be shy. The truth is always respectable. What did you take there?"

"Only a -- a -- dead cat."

There was a ripple of laughter, which the court stopped.

"We will show the skeleton of that cat. Now, my boy, tell us everything that happened -- tell it in your own way -- don't leave anything out, and don't be afraid."

Tom began -- hesitantly at first, but as he got into the story his words flowed more and more easily; in a little while every sound stopped except his own voice; every eye focused on him; with parted lips and held breath the audience hung on his words, forgetting time, caught up in the horrible fascination of the story. The tension on the held-back emotions reached its peak when the boy said:

"-- and as the doctor picked up the board and hit Muff Potter, and Potter fell, Injun Joe jumped with the knife and --"

Crash! Quick as lightning the half-breed jumped for a window, forced his way through all who stood in the way, and was gone!
