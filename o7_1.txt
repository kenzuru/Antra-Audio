

  And after all, the weather was perfect. They could not have had a better day for a garden party if they had chosen it. There was no wind, it was warm, and the sky had no clouds. Only the blue colour had a soft golden mist over it, the way it sometimes looks in early summer. The gardener had been up since early morning, cutting the grass and cleaning it, until the lawn and the flat dark circles where the daisy plants had been seemed to shine. As for the roses, you could not help feeling that they knew roses are the only flowers that impress people at garden parties — the only flowers that everyone recognises. Hundreds, yes, truly hundreds, had opened in a single night. The green bushes bent down as if great angels had visited them.

Breakfast was not yet finished before the men came to put up the large tent.

"Where do you want the tent put, mother?"

"My dear child, there is no point asking me. I have decided to leave everything to you children this year. Forget that I am your mother. Treat me like an honoured guest."

But Meg could not possibly go and watch over the men. She had washed her hair before breakfast, and she sat drinking her coffee wearing a green head wrap, with a dark wet curl pressed flat on each cheek. Jose, the butterfly, always came downstairs in a silk petticoat and a kimono jacket.

"You'll have to go, Laura. You're the artistic one."

Laura hurried away, still holding her piece of bread and butter. It is so nice to have a reason to eat outside, and besides, she loved organising things. She always felt she could do it better than anyone else.

Four men in their shirt sleeves stood together on the garden path. They were carrying long poles wrapped with rolls of canvas, and they had large tool bags on their backs. They looked impressive. Laura now wished she did not have the bread and butter, but there was nowhere to put it, and she could not throw it away. She went red and tried to look serious and even a little short-sighted as she walked up to them.

"Good morning," she said, copying her mother's voice. But that sounded so unnatural that she felt embarrassed, and she spoke like a small girl instead. "Oh — er — have you come — is it about the tent?"

"That's right, miss," said the tallest of the men, a thin, freckled man. He moved his tool bag, pushed back his straw hat and smiled down at her. "That's about it."

His smile was so relaxed and friendly that Laura felt better. What nice eyes he had — small, but so dark blue! And when she looked at the others, they were smiling too. "Don't worry, we won't hurt you," their smiles seemed to say. How very nice workmen were! And what a beautiful morning! She must not mention the morning. She must be professional. The tent.

"Well, what about the lily lawn? Would that work?"

She pointed to the lily lawn with the hand that was not holding the bread and butter. They turned and looked in that direction. A short, fat man pushed out his lower lip, and the tall man looked unsure.

"I don't like it," he said. "Not easy enough to see. You see, with something like a tent," and he turned to Laura in his easy way, "you want to put it somewhere where it really stands out and catches your eye, if you understand me."

The way Laura had been raised made her wonder for a moment whether it was quite respectful for a workman to speak to her that way. But she did understand him.

"A corner of the tennis court," she suggested. "But the band is going to be in one corner."

"Oh, you're going to have a band, are you?" said one of the other workmen. He was pale and looked tired as his dark eyes looked around the tennis court. What was he thinking?

"Only a very small band," said Laura gently. Perhaps he would not mind so much if the band was quite small. But the tall man interrupted.

"Look here, miss, that's the spot. Against those trees. Over there. That will work perfectly."

Against the karaka trees. Then the karaka trees would be hidden. And they were so beautiful, with their wide, shiny leaves and their groups of yellow fruit. They were like trees you imagined growing on a deserted island — proud and alone, holding their leaves and fruit up to the sun in a kind of quiet beauty. Did they have to be hidden by a tent?

They did. Already the men had picked up their poles and were walking towards the spot. Only the tall man was left. He bent down, picked a small piece of lavender, put his thumb and finger to his nose and breathed in the smell. When Laura saw that, she forgot all about the karaka trees because she was so surprised that he cared about something like that — caring about the smell of lavender. How many men she knew would have done such a thing? Oh, how wonderfully nice workmen were, she thought. Why could she not have workmen as friends instead of the silly boys she danced with and who came to Sunday evening dinner? She would get along much better with men like these.

It is all because of these silly class differences, she decided, as the tall man drew something on the back of an envelope — something that needed to be folded up or left to hang. Well, for her part, she did not feel those differences. Not at all, not even a little. And now came the knocking sound of wooden hammers. Someone whistled, someone called out, "Are you ready there, mate?" "Mate!" The friendliness of it. Just to show how happy she was, just to show the tall man how comfortable she felt, and how little she cared about silly social rules, Laura took a big bite of her bread and butter while she looked at the small drawing. She felt just like a working girl.

"Laura, Laura, where are you? Telephone, Laura!" a voice called from the house.

"Coming!" She ran quickly across the lawn, up the path, up the steps, across the veranda and into the entrance. In the hall her father and Laurie were brushing their hats, getting ready to go to the office.

"I say, Laura," said Laurie quickly, "could you take a look at my jacket before this afternoon? See if it needs to be pressed."

"I will," she said. Suddenly she could not stop herself. She ran to Laurie and gave him a small, quick hug. "Oh, I do love parties, don't you?" Laura said excitedly.

"Yes, absolutely," said Laurie in his warm, young voice, and he hugged his sister back and gave her a gentle push. "Now hurry to the telephone, old girl."

The telephone. "Yes, yes. Oh yes. Kitty? Good morning, dear. Come to lunch? Please do, dear. I'd love it of course. It will only be a very simple meal — just leftover sandwich pieces and broken meringue shells and whatever is left. Yes, isn't it a perfect morning? Your white one? Oh, I definitely would. One moment — stay on the line. Mother's calling." And Laura sat back. "What, mother? I can't hear."
