

  Like bees following their queen, mother and daughters hovered around Mr. March the next day, forgetting everything else to look at, serve, and listen to the new patient, who was in danger of being overwhelmed with too much kindness. As he sat propped up in a big chair next to Beth's sofa, with the other three close by, and Hannah putting her head in now and then to peek at the dear man, nothing seemed to be missing from their happiness. But something was missing, and the older ones felt it, though no one said so. Mr. and Mrs. March looked at each other with a worried expression, whenever their eyes followed Meg. Jo had sudden moments of seriousness, and was seen to shake her fist at Mr. Brooke's umbrella, which had been left in the hall. Meg was distracted, shy, and quiet, jumped when the doorbell rang, and went red when John's name was mentioned. Amy said that everyone seemed to be waiting for something and could not settle down, which was strange, since Father was safely home. And Beth innocently wondered why their neighbors had not come over as usual.

Laurie walked by in the afternoon, and seeing Meg at the window, seemed suddenly inspired by a dramatic idea, because he fell down on one knee in the snow, hit his chest, pulled at his hair, and put his hands together in a begging gesture, as if asking for something. And when Meg told him to behave himself and go away, he pretended to wipe away imaginary tears with his handkerchief, and moved unsteadily around the corner as if in complete despair.

"What does that silly person mean?" said Meg, laughing and trying to look as if she did not understand.

"He is showing you how your John will behave before long. Touching, is it not?" answered Jo with a scornful tone.

"Do not say my John, it is not appropriate or true," but Meg's voice stayed on the words as if they sounded nice to her. "Please do not tease me, Jo, I have told you I do not care much about him, and nothing is supposed to be said, but we should all be friendly, and go on as before."

"We cannot, because something has been said, and Laurie's trick has changed things between us. I see it, and so does Mother. You are not like your old self at all, and you seem so far away from me. I do not plan to tease you and I will accept it like a grown-up, but I do wish it was all decided. I hate waiting, so if you ever plan to do something about it, do it quickly and get it over with," said Jo with irritation.

"I cannot say anything until he speaks, and he will not, because Father said I was too young," began Meg, bending over her work with a small smile, which suggested that she did not quite agree with her father on that point.

"If he did speak, you would not know what to say, but would cry or go red, or let him have his way, instead of giving a clear, firm no."

"I am not as silly and weak as you think. I know exactly what I should say, because I have planned it all, so I will not be caught off guard. There is no knowing what may happen, and I wanted to be prepared."

Jo could not help smiling at the confident air that Meg had taken on without realizing it, which looked very nice with the pretty color rising in her cheeks.

"Would you mind telling me what you would say?" asked Jo, with more respect.

"Not at all. You are sixteen now, old enough to be told my private thoughts, and my experience may be useful to you someday, in your own situations like this."

"I do not plan to have any. It is fun to watch other people fall in love, but I would feel like a fool doing it myself," said Jo, looking alarmed at the thought.

"I think not, if you liked someone very much, and he liked you." Meg spoke as if to herself, and looked out at the lane where she had often seen couples walking together in the summer evenings.

"I thought you were going to tell me your speech to that man," said Jo, interrupting her sister's daydream.

"Oh, I would simply say, quite calmly and firmly, 'Thank you, Mr. Brooke, you are very kind, but I agree with Father that I am too young to make any commitment at present, so please say no more, but let us be friends as we were.'"

"Hmm, that is quite cold and formal! I do not believe you will ever actually say it, and I know he will not be satisfied if you do. If he goes on like the rejected lovers in books, you will give in, rather than hurt his feelings."

"No, I will not. I shall tell him I have made up my mind, and walk out of the room with dignity."

Meg stood up as she spoke, and was just about to practice the dignified exit, when footsteps in the hall made her rush back to her seat and begin to sew as fast as if her life depended on finishing that particular seam before a certain time. Jo hid a laugh at the sudden change, and when someone knocked quietly, opened the door with a serious face that was anything but welcoming.

"Good afternoon. I came to get my umbrella — that is, to see how your father is today," said Mr. Brooke, becoming a little confused as his eyes moved from one expressive face to the other.

"He is quite well, he is in the other room. I will get him and tell him you are here." And having mixed up her father and the umbrella in her answer, Jo slipped out of the room to give Meg a chance to say her speech and show her dignity. But the instant she disappeared, Meg began to move quietly toward the door, saying,

"Mother will like to see you. Please sit down, I will call her."

"Do not go. Are you afraid of me, Margaret?" and Mr. Brooke looked so hurt that Meg thought she must have done something very rude. She went red right up to the little curls on her forehead, because he had never called her Margaret before, and she was surprised to find how natural and sweet it felt to hear him say it. Wanting to seem friendly and comfortable, she put out her hand in a trusting way, and said gratefully,

"How could I be afraid when you have been so kind to Father? I only wish I could thank you for it."

"Shall I tell you how?" asked Mr. Brooke, holding the small hand firmly in both of his own, and looking down at Meg with so much love in the brown eyes that her heart began to flutter, and she both wanted to run away and to stay and listen.

"Oh no, please do not, I would rather not," she said, trying to take her hand back, and looking frightened despite what she said.

"I will not trouble you. I only want to know if you care for me even a little, Meg. I love you so much, dear," added Mr. Brooke tenderly.

This was the moment for the calm, proper speech, but Meg did not say it. She forgot every word of it, looked down, and answered, "I do not know," so softly that John had to lean down to hear the foolish little reply.

He seemed to think it was worth the effort, because he smiled to himself as if quite satisfied, held the hand gratefully, and said in his most gentle voice, "Will you try and find out? I want to know so much, because I cannot do my work with any enthusiasm until I know whether I am to have my reward in the end or not."

"I am too young," said Meg quietly, wondering why she felt so nervous, yet rather enjoying it.

"I will wait, and in the meantime, you could be learning to like me. Would that be a very difficult lesson, dear?"

"Not if I chose to learn it, but..."

"Please choose to learn, Meg. I love to teach, and this is easier than German," said John, taking her other hand, so that she had no way of hiding her face as he leaned forward to look into it.

His tone was perfectly respectful and caring, but stealing a quick look at him, Meg saw that his eyes were cheerful as well as tender, and that he wore the satisfied smile of someone who had no doubt about how things would go. This annoyed her. Annie Moffat's silly lessons about playing with someone's feelings came into her mind, and the love of power, which exists in even the kindest of people, woke up all of a sudden and took hold of her. She felt excited and strange, and not knowing what else to do, followed a sudden impulse, and, taking her hands back, said with irritation, "I do not choose. Please go away and let me be!"

Poor Mr. Brooke looked as if his lovely dream was falling apart, because he had never seen Meg in such a mood before, and it confused him.

"Do you really mean that?" he asked anxiously, following her as she walked away.

"Yes, I do. I do not want to be bothered about such things. Father says I need not, it is too soon and I would rather not."

"May I not hope you will change your mind later? I will wait and say nothing until you have had more time. Do not play games with me, Meg. I did not think that of you."

"Do not think of me at all. I would rather you did not," said Meg, taking a playful but not entirely kind pleasure in testing her admirer's patience and her own power over him.

He was serious and pale now, and looked more like the heroes in novels that she admired, but he did not hit his forehead or walk about the room as they did. He just stood looking at her so hopefully, so tenderly, that she found her heart softening in spite of herself. What might have happened next, I cannot say, if Aunt March had not come walking in with her cane at this important moment.

The old lady could not resist her wish to see her nephew, because she had met Laurie while she was out, and hearing of Mr. March's arrival, had driven straight over to see him. The family were all busy in the back part of the house, and she had walked in quietly, hoping to surprise them. She did surprise two of them very much, so that Meg jumped as if she had seen a ghost, and Mr. Brooke disappeared into the study.

"What is all this?" cried the old lady, tapping her cane as she looked from the pale young man to the very red young lady.

"He is Father's friend. I am so surprised to see you!" said Meg, feeling that she was about to get a lecture.

"That is obvious," replied Aunt March, sitting down. "But what is Father's friend saying to make you look like that? Something is going on, and I insist on knowing what it is," with another tap of her cane.

"We were only talking. Mr. Brooke came for his umbrella," began Meg, wishing that Mr. Brooke and the umbrella were safely out of the house.

"Brooke? That boy's teacher? Oh! I understand now. I know all about it. Jo gave the wrong message in one of your Father's letters, and I made her tell me. You have not gone and agreed to anything with him, child?" cried Aunt March, looking shocked.

"Quiet! He will hear. Should I call Mother?" said Meg, very worried.

"Not yet. I have something to say to you, and I must say it at once. Tell me, do you plan to marry this Cook? If you do, not one penny of my money will ever go to you. Remember that, and be a sensible girl," said the old lady firmly.

Now Aunt March had a perfect talent for making even the gentlest people feel like doing the opposite of what she told them, and she enjoyed doing it. The best of us have a small stubborn side, especially when we are young and in love. If Aunt March had begged Meg to agree to marry John Brooke, she would probably have said she could not think of it, but since she was firmly ordered not to like him, she immediately made up her mind that she would. Her own feelings as well as her stubbornness made the decision easy, and being already quite worked up, Meg stood up to the old lady with unusual spirit.

"I shall marry whom I please, Aunt March, and you can leave your money to anyone you like," she said, nodding her head firmly.

"Well, well! Is that how you take my advice, miss? You will be sorry for it eventually, when you have tried living on love alone and found it does not work."

"It cannot be worse than what some people find in big houses," replied Meg.

Aunt March put on her glasses and took a good look at the girl, because she did not know her in this new mood. Meg hardly knew herself, she felt so brave and independent, so glad to defend John and claim her right to love him if she chose. Aunt March saw that she had gone about it the wrong way, and after a short pause, tried again, saying as gently as she could, "Now, Meg, my dear, be reasonable and take my advice. I mean it kindly, and I do not want you to ruin your whole life by making a mistake at the start. You should marry well and help your family. It is your duty to make a financially good match and that should be made clear to you."

"Father and Mother do not think so. They like John even though he is not rich."

"Your parents, my dear, have no more practical wisdom than a pair of babies."

"I am glad of it," cried Meg firmly.

Aunt March took no notice, but continued with her lecture. "This Rook is poor and has no rich relatives, has he?"

"No, but he has many good friends."

"You cannot live on friends — try it and see how quickly they lose interest. He has no job, has he?"

"Not yet. Mr. Laurence is going to help him."

"That will not last. James Laurence is an unpredictable old man and not to be counted on. So you plan to marry a man without money, position, or a job, and go on working harder than you do now, when you could be comfortable all your days by listening to me and making a better choice? I thought you had more sense, Meg."

"I could not do better if I waited half my life! John is good and wise, he has great talent, he is willing to work and sure to succeed, he is so energetic and brave. Everyone likes and respects him, and I am proud to think he cares for me, even though I am so poor and young and silly," said Meg, looking prettier than ever in her passion.

"He knows you have rich relatives, child. That is the secret reason he likes you, I suspect."

"Aunt March, how dare you say such a thing? John would never think in such a low way, and I will not listen to you for another minute if you talk like that," cried Meg angrily, forgetting everything but how unfair the old lady's suspicions were. "My John would not marry for money, any more than I would. We are willing to work and we plan to wait. I am not afraid of being poor, because I have been happy so far, and I know I shall be with him because he loves me, and I..."

Meg stopped there, suddenly remembering that she had not made up her mind, that she had told 'her John' to go away, and that he might be listening to her contradictory words.

Aunt March was very angry, because she had set her heart on having her pretty niece make a great match, and something in the girl's happy young face made the lonely old woman feel both sad and bitter.

"Well, I have nothing more to do with it! You are a stubborn child, and you have lost more than you know by this act of foolishness. No, I will not stay. I am disappointed in you, and I have no heart to see your father now. Do not expect anything from me when you are married. Your Mr. Brooke's friends must look after you. I am done with you forever."

And slamming the door in Meg's face, Aunt March drove away in great anger. She seemed to take all the girl's courage with her, because when left alone, Meg stood for a moment, not sure whether to laugh or cry. Before she could decide, she was approached by Mr. Brooke, who said all at once, "I could not help hearing, Meg. Thank you for defending me, and thank Aunt March for proving that you do care for me a little bit."

"I did not know how much until she spoke badly of you," began Meg.

"And I need not go away, but may stay and be happy, may I, dear?"

Here was another perfect chance to make the impressive speech and the dignified exit, but Meg did not think of doing either, and embarrassed herself completely in Jo's eyes by quietly whispering, "Yes, John," and hiding her face against Mr. Brooke's chest.

Fifteen minutes after Aunt March had left, Jo came softly downstairs, paused for a moment at the sitting room door, and hearing no sound inside, nodded and smiled with a satisfied expression, saying to herself, "She has sent him away as we planned, and that business is settled. I will go in and hear the details, and have a good laugh about it."

But poor Jo never got her laugh, because she was frozen in the doorway by something that made her stand there, staring with her mouth nearly as wide open as her eyes. Going in to celebrate over someone she had hoped to see defeated, and to praise a strong-minded sister for getting rid of an unwanted admirer, it was certainly a shock to find the said admirer sitting calmly on the sofa, with the strong-minded sister sitting on his knee wearing an expression of complete contentment. Jo made a kind of sound, as if a cold shower had suddenly come down on her, because such an unexpected change of events truly took her breath away. At the strange sound the two lovers turned and saw her. Meg jumped up, looking both proud and embarrassed, but 'that man', as Jo called him, actually laughed and said coolly, as he kissed the amazed newcomer, "Sister Jo, congratulate us!"

That was adding insult to injury — it was altogether too much, and making some wild gesture with her hands, Jo disappeared without a word. Running upstairs, she shocked the patients by crying out dramatically as she burst into the room, "Oh, do somebody go down quickly! John Brooke is acting terribly, and Meg likes it!"

Mr. and Mrs. March left the room quickly, and throwing herself on the bed, Jo cried and complained dramatically as she told the shocking news to Beth and Amy. The little girls, however, thought it was a most enjoyable and interesting event, and Jo got little comfort from them, so she went up to her private spot in the attic, and shared her troubles with the rats.

Nobody ever found out what went on in the sitting room that afternoon, but a great deal of talking was done, and quiet Mr. Brooke surprised his friends by the energy and warmth with which he talked about his feelings, explained his plans, and convinced them to arrange everything just as he wanted.

The tea bell rang before he had finished describing the wonderful life he planned to make for Meg, and he proudly took her in to supper, both looking so happy that Jo did not have the heart to be jealous or miserable. Amy was very impressed by John's devotion and Meg's dignity. Beth smiled at them from a distance. While Mr. and Mrs. March watched the young couple with such gentle satisfaction that it was completely clear that Aunt March was right in calling them as 'unworldly as a pair of babies'. Nobody ate much, but everyone looked very happy, and the old room seemed to brighten up remarkably when the first love story of the family began there.

"You cannot say nothing pleasant ever happens now, can you, Meg?" said Amy, trying to decide how she would draw the two lovers in a sketch she was planning.

"No, I am sure I cannot. How much has happened since I said that! It seems a year ago," answered Meg, who was in a blissful dream far above ordinary things like bread and butter.

"The happy moments come right after the sad ones this time, and I rather think the changes have begun," said Mrs. March. "In most families there comes, now and then, a year full of important events. This has been such a year, but it ends well, after all."

"I hope the next will end better," said Jo quietly, who found it very hard to see Meg so absorbed in a new person right in front of her, because Jo loved a few people deeply and was afraid of losing any part of their affection.

"I hope the third year from now will end better. I intend it shall, if I live to work out my plans," said Mr. Brooke, smiling at Meg, as if everything had become possible to him now.

"Does it not seem like a very long time to wait?" asked Amy, who was eager for the wedding.

"I have so much to learn before I am ready, it seems quite short to me," answered Meg, with a sweet seriousness in her face that had never been there before.

"You have only to wait, while I am to do the work," said John, starting his efforts by picking up Meg's napkin, with an expression which made Jo shake her head, and then say to herself with some relief as the front door opened, "Here comes Laurie. Now we shall have some sensible conversation."

But Jo was wrong, because Laurie came in excitedly, full of good spirits, carrying a large wedding-style bouquet for 'Mrs. John Brooke', and clearly under the impression that the whole thing had been brought about by his own excellent planning.

"I knew Brooke would get his way, he always does, because when he decides to achieve something, it gets done no matter what," said Laurie, when he had presented his flowers and his congratulations.

"Thank you for that good word. I take it as a good sign for the future and invite you to my wedding right now," answered Mr. Brooke, who felt at peace with everyone, even his troublesome student.

"I will be there even if I am at the ends of the earth, because just seeing Jo's face on that occasion would be worth a long trip. You do not look very cheerful, madam, what is wrong?" asked Laurie, following her into a corner of the sitting room, where everyone had gathered to welcome Mr. Laurence.

"I do not approve of the match, but I have made up my mind to accept it, and shall not say a word against it," said Jo seriously. "You cannot know how hard it is for me to give up Meg," she continued with a little tremble in her voice.

"You do not give her up. You only share her," said Laurie with comfort.

"It can never be the same again. I have lost my dearest friend," sighed Jo.

"You have got me, anyway. I am not worth very much, I know, but I will be there for you, Jo, all my life. I truly mean it!" and Laurie meant what he said.

"I know you will, and I am very grateful. You are always a great comfort to me, Teddy," said Jo, gratefully shaking his hand.

"Well now, do not be sad, there's a good friend. It's all fine, you see. Meg is happy, Brooke will be busy and get settled right away, Grandfather will help him, and it will be wonderful to see Meg in her own little house. We will have great times after she goes, because I shall finish college before long, and then we will go abroad on some nice trip or other. Would not that make you feel better?"

"I rather think it would, but there is no knowing what may happen in three years," said Jo thoughtfully.

"That is true. Do you not wish you could look into the future and see where we will all be then? I do," said Laurie.

"I think not, because I might see something sad, and everyone looks so happy now, I do not believe things could be much better." And Jo's eyes moved slowly around the room, brightening as they looked, because what she saw was a very pleasant sight.

Father and Mother sat together, quietly reliving the first chapter of their own love story, which for them had begun about twenty years ago. Amy was drawing the lovers, who sat apart in their own beautiful world, the light of which touched their faces with a glow that the little artist could not capture. Beth lay on her sofa, talking happily with her old friend, who held her little hand as if he felt that it had the power to guide him along the peaceful path she walked. Jo sat in her favorite low chair, with the calm, quiet look which suited her best, and Laurie, leaning on the back of her chair, his chin level with her curly head, smiled with his friendliest expression, and nodded at her in the long mirror which reflected them both.

So the curtain falls on Meg, Jo, Beth, and Amy. Whether it ever rises again, depends on how the first act of the family story called Little Women is received.
