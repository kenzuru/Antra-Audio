

  Phileas Fogg, having shut the door of his house at half past eleven, and having put his right foot in front of his left five hundred and seventy-five times, and his left foot in front of his right five hundred and seventy-six times, arrived at the Reform Club, an impressive building in Pall Mall that could not have cost less than three million pounds. He went directly to the dining room, whose nine windows opened onto a tasteful garden where the trees were already turning golden with autumn colours. He took his usual seat at the table, which had already been set for him. His breakfast consisted of a side dish, a grilled fish with Reading sauce, a bright red slice of roast beef garnished with mushrooms, a rhubarb and gooseberry tart, and a small piece of Cheshire cheese, all washed down with several cups of tea, for which the Reform Club is famous. He rose at thirteen minutes to one and walked to the large hall, a grand room decorated with richly framed paintings. A servant handed him an uncut copy of The Times, which he proceeded to cut open with a skill that showed he was used to this delicate task. Reading this paper kept Phileas Fogg busy until a quarter to four, while the Standard, his next task, occupied him until dinner time. Dinner passed as breakfast had done, and Mr. Fogg reappeared in the reading room and sat down with the Pall Mall at twenty minutes before six. Half an hour later, several members of the Reform came in and gathered around the fireplace, where a coal fire was burning steadily. They were Mr. Fogg's usual whist partners: Andrew Stuart, an engineer; John Sullivan and Samuel Fallentin, bankers; Thomas Flanagan, a brewer; and Gauthier Ralph, one of the Directors of the Bank of England — all wealthy and highly respected men, even in a club that included the leading figures of English trade and finance.

"Well, Ralph," said Thomas Flanagan, "what about that robbery?"

"Oh," replied Stuart, "the Bank will lose the money."

"On the contrary," broke in Ralph, "I hope we may catch the robber. Skilled detectives have been sent to all the main ports of America and Europe, and he'll have to be very clever to get away from them."

"But do you have a description of the robber?" asked Stuart.

"In the first place, he is not a robber at all," replied Ralph firmly.

"What! A man who takes fifty-five thousand pounds is not a robber?"

"No."

"Perhaps he's a businessman, then."

"The Daily Telegraph says he is a gentleman."

It was Phileas Fogg, whose head now appeared from behind his newspapers, who made this last remark. He bowed to his friends and joined in the conversation. The matter being discussed, which was the talk of the town, had happened three days earlier at the Bank of England. A package of banknotes worth fifty-five thousand pounds had been taken from the chief cashier's desk, while that official was busy recording the receipt of three shillings and sixpence. Of course, he could not keep his eyes on everything. It should be noted that the Bank of England places a trusting confidence in the honesty of the public. There are neither guards nor barriers to protect its money. Gold, silver, and banknotes are openly displayed, available to anyone who passes by. A careful observer of English customs once described how, while visiting one of the rooms of the Bank, he was curious enough to pick up a gold bar weighing about seven or eight pounds. He picked it up, examined it, passed it to his neighbour, who passed it to the next person, and so on, until the bar, going from hand to hand, was carried to the end of a dark passageway. It did not return to its place for half an hour, and all the while the cashier had not even raised his head. But in the present case things had not gone so smoothly. The package of notes could not be found when five o'clock sounded on the heavy clock in the accounts office, and the amount was recorded as a loss. As soon as the theft was discovered, skilled detectives hurried off to Liverpool, Glasgow, Havre, Suez, Brindisi, New York, and other ports, driven by the promise of a reward of two thousand pounds plus five percent of whatever money was recovered. Detectives were also instructed to watch closely all those who arrived at or left London by train, and an official investigation was immediately begun.

There were real reasons to believe, as the Daily Telegraph reported, that the thief did not belong to a professional criminal gang. On the day of the robbery, a well-dressed gentleman of polished manners and a confident appearance had been noticed going back and forth in the payment room where the crime took place. A description of him was easily obtained and sent to the detectives. Some hopeful people, including Ralph, had not given up hope of catching him. The newspapers and clubs were full of the story, and everywhere people were debating whether the pursuit would succeed. The Reform Club was especially excited, since several of its members were Bank officials.

Ralph would not agree that the detectives' work was likely to fail. He believed that the reward offered would greatly encourage their enthusiasm and energy. But Stuart was far from sharing this confidence, and as they sat down at the whist table, they continued to argue the matter. Stuart and Flanagan played together, while Phileas Fogg had Fallentin as his partner. As the game went on the conversation stopped, though it started up again between the rounds of cards.

"I maintain," said Stuart, "that the chances are in the thief's favour. He must be a clever man."

"Well, but where can he run to?" asked Ralph. "No country is safe for him."

"Nonsense!"

"Where could he go, then?"

"Oh, I don't know. The world is big enough."

"It was once," said Phileas Fogg quietly. "Cut, sir," he added, handing the cards to Thomas Flanagan.

The discussion stopped during the round of cards, after which Stuart picked up where he had left off.

"What do you mean by 'once'? Has the world grown smaller?"

"Certainly," replied Ralph. "I agree with Mr. Fogg. The world has grown smaller, since a man can now travel around it ten times faster than he could a hundred years ago. And that is why the search for this thief is more likely to succeed."

"And also why the thief can escape more easily."

"Please play your card, Mr. Stuart," said Phileas Fogg.

But the doubtful Stuart was not convinced, and when the hand was finished, he said eagerly: "You have a strange way, Ralph, of proving that the world has grown smaller. So, because you can travel around it in three months—"

"In eighty days," interrupted Phileas Fogg.

"That is true, gentlemen," added John Sullivan. "Only eighty days, now that the section between Rothal and Allahabad on the Great Indian Peninsula Railway has been opened. Here is the estimate published by the Daily Telegraph:—

From London to Suez via Mont Cenis and Brindisi, by rail and steamboat ................. 7 days
From Suez to Bombay, by steamer .................... 13 "
From Bombay to Calcutta, by rail ................... 3 "
From Calcutta to Hong Kong, by steamer ............. 13 "
From Hong Kong to Yokohama (Japan), by steamer ..... 6 "
From Yokohama to San Francisco, by steamer ......... 22 "
From San Francisco to New York, by rail ............. 7 "
From New York to London, by steamer and rail ........ 9 "
-------
Total ............................................ 80 days."

"Yes, in eighty days!" exclaimed Stuart, who in his excitement made a wrong deal. "But that doesn't take into account bad weather, strong winds against the ship, shipwrecks, railway accidents, and so on."

"All included," replied Phileas Fogg, continuing to play despite the discussion.

"But suppose the Hindus or Indians pull up the railway tracks," replied Stuart. "Suppose they stop the trains, rob the luggage wagons, and attack the passengers!"

"All included," calmly replied Fogg, and as he put down his cards he added, "Two trumps."

Stuart, whose turn it was to deal, gathered the cards and continued: "You are right in theory, Mr. Fogg, but in practice—"

"In practice also, Mr. Stuart."

"I would like to see you do it in eighty days."

"It depends on you. Shall we go?"

"Heaven forbid! But I would bet four thousand pounds that such a journey, made under these conditions, is impossible."

"Quite possible, on the contrary," replied Mr. Fogg.

"Well, do it then!"

"The journey around the world in eighty days?"

"Yes."

"I would like nothing better."

"When?"

"Right away. But I warn you that I shall do it at your expense."

"This is absurd!" cried Stuart, who was beginning to be annoyed by his friend's persistence. "Come, let's continue the game."

"Deal the cards again," said Phileas Fogg. "There was a wrong deal."

Stuart picked up the pack of cards with a nervous hand, then suddenly put them down again.

"Well, Mr. Fogg," he said, "so be it. I will bet the four thousand pounds on it."

"Calm yourself, my dear Stuart," said Fallentin. "It's only a joke."

"When I say I'll bet," replied Stuart, "I mean it."

"Very well," said Mr. Fogg, and turning to the others he continued: "I have a deposit of twenty thousand at Baring's which I will willingly risk on it."

"Twenty thousand pounds!" cried Sullivan. "Twenty thousand pounds, which you would lose because of a single unexpected delay!"

"The unexpected does not exist," quietly replied Phileas Fogg.

"But, Mr. Fogg, eighty days is only the estimate of the minimum possible time in which the journey can be made."

"A well-used minimum is enough for everything."

"But, in order not to go over that time, you would have to move perfectly from trains onto steamers, and from steamers onto trains again."

"I will move — perfectly."

"You are joking."

"A true Englishman does not joke when he is talking about something as serious as a bet," replied Phileas Fogg solemnly. "I will bet twenty thousand pounds against anyone who wishes, that I will travel around the world in eighty days or less — in nineteen hundred and twenty hours, or a hundred and fifteen thousand two hundred minutes. Do you accept?"

"We accept," replied Messrs. Stuart, Fallentin, Sullivan, Flanagan, and Ralph, after consulting each other.

"Good," said Mr. Fogg. "The train leaves for Dover at a quarter to nine. I will take it."

"This very evening?" asked Stuart.

"This very evening," replied Phileas Fogg. He took out and looked at a small calendar, and added, "As today is Wednesday, the 2nd of October, I shall be due back in London in this very room of the Reform Club, on Saturday, the 21st of December, at a quarter to nine in the evening. Otherwise, the twenty thousand pounds now deposited in my name at Baring's will belong to you, in fact and by right, gentlemen. Here is a cheque for the amount."

A written record of the bet was immediately drawn up and signed by all six parties, during which Phileas Fogg remained perfectly calm. He certainly did not bet to win, and had only risked the twenty thousand pounds — half of his fortune — because he understood that he might need to spend the other half to complete this difficult, if not impossible, plan. As for the others, they seemed quite agitated — not so much because of the amount they had bet, but because they felt slightly uneasy about betting under conditions so difficult for their friend.

The clock struck seven, and the group offered to pause the game so that Mr. Fogg could prepare for his departure.

"I am quite ready now," was his calm reply. "Diamonds are trumps. Please play, gentlemen."
