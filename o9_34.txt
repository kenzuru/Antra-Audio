
  Phileas Fogg was in prison. He had been locked up in the Customs building and was to be taken to London the next day.

Passepartout, when he saw his master arrested, would have attacked Fix if he had not been held back by some police officers. Aouda was shocked by the suddenness of an event she could not understand. Passepartout explained to her how the honest and brave Fogg had been arrested as a criminal. The young woman's heart refused to believe such a terrible accusation, and when she saw there was nothing she could do to help her protector, she cried bitterly.

As for Fix, he had arrested Mr. Fogg because it was his duty — whether Mr. Fogg was guilty or not.

The thought then came to Passepartout that he was the cause of this new disaster. Had he not hidden Fix's true purpose from his master? When Fix had revealed his real identity and intentions, why had Passepartout not told Mr. Fogg? If his master had been warned, he would no doubt have shown Fix proof of his innocence and convinced him of his mistake. At the very least, Fix would not have continued his journey at his master's expense and following close behind him, only to arrest him the moment he set foot on English soil. Passepartout cried until he could not see, and felt like destroying himself.

Aouda and he had stayed near the entrance to the Customs building, despite the cold. Neither wanted to leave the place — both were desperate to see Mr. Fogg again.

That gentleman was truly ruined, and at the very moment he was about to reach his goal. This arrest was the end. Having arrived in Liverpool at twenty minutes before twelve on the 21st of December, he had until a quarter before nine that evening to reach the Reform Club — nine hours and a quarter. The journey from Liverpool to London was six hours.

If anyone had entered the Customs building at that moment, they would have found Mr. Fogg sitting on a wooden bench — still, calm, and showing no signs of anger. He was not, it was true, accepting defeat — but this final blow failed to make him show any emotion on the outside. Was he burning inside with one of those secret rages that stay hidden and then explode with unstoppable force at the very last moment? No one could say. There he sat, waiting quietly — for what? Did he still hold onto hope? Did he still believe, now that the prison door was shut behind him, that he would succeed?

Whatever the case, Mr. Fogg carefully placed his watch on the table and watched the hands moving forward. Not a word left his lips, but his expression was strangely fixed and serious. The situation, whatever way you looked at it, was terrible. If Phileas Fogg was honest, he was ruined. If he was a criminal, he was caught.

Did the idea of escape come to him? Did he look around to see if there was any way out of his prison? Did he think of breaking free? Perhaps — for once he walked slowly around the room. But the door was locked and the window was covered with heavy iron bars. He sat down again and took his journal from his pocket. On the line where the words "21st December, Saturday, Liverpool" were written, he added, "80th day, 11.40 a.m.," and waited.

The Customs building clock struck one. Mr. Fogg noticed that his watch was two hours fast.

Two hours! If he were getting on an express train at that very moment, he could reach London and the Reform Club by a quarter before nine in the evening. His forehead creased slightly.

At thirty-three minutes past two, he heard a strange noise outside, then the quick opening of doors. He could hear Passepartout's voice, and immediately after that of Fix. For a moment, Phileas Fogg's eyes brightened.

The door swung open, and he saw Passepartout, Aouda, and Fix, who rushed towards him.

Fix was out of breath, and his hair was messy. He could not speak. "Sir," he managed to say, "sir — forgive me — a terrible likeness — the real criminal arrested three days ago — you are free!"

Phileas Fogg was free! He walked up to the detective, looked him directly in the face, and with the only fast movement he had ever made in his life, or ever would make, drew back his arm and, with the precision of a machine, knocked Fix to the floor.

"Well done!" cried Passepartout. "That is what you call a proper English punch!"

Fix, who found himself on the floor, said nothing. He had only received what he deserved. Mr. Fogg, Aouda, and Passepartout left the Customs building without delay, got into a carriage, and a few minutes later arrived at the station.

Phileas Fogg asked if there was an express train about to leave for London. It was forty minutes past two. The express train had left thirty-five minutes ago. Phileas Fogg then ordered a special private train.

Several fast engines were available, but the railway rules did not allow the special train to leave until three o'clock.

At that hour, Phileas Fogg — after encouraging the driver with the offer of a generous reward — finally set out towards London with Aouda and his loyal servant.

The journey needed to be completed in five and a half hours. This would have been simple on a clear, uninterrupted route. But there were unavoidable delays, and when Mr. Fogg stepped off the train at the London station, all the clocks in the city were striking ten minutes to nine.[1]

Having gone all the way around the world, he was five minutes too late. He had lost the bet!

[1] A rather strange situation for all the London clocks to agree on! — TRANSLATOR.
