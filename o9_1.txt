

  Mr. Phileas Fogg lived, in 1872, at No. 7, Saville Row, Burlington Gardens, the house where Sheridan died in 1814. He was one of the most noticeable members of the Reform Club, though he always seemed to avoid drawing attention to himself. He was a mysterious person, and very little was known about him, except that he was a very polished and refined man. People said that he looked like Byron — at least that his head looked Byronic; but he was a calm, bearded Byron, who could live a thousand years without growing old.

He was certainly English, but it was less certain whether Phileas Fogg was really a Londoner. He was never seen at the Stock Exchange, nor at the Bank, nor in the business offices of the City. No ships coming into London docks belonged to him. He had no public job. He had never been registered at any of the Inns of Court — not at the Temple, Lincoln's Inn, or Gray's Inn. His voice had never been heard in the Court of Chancery, the Exchequer, the Queen's Bench, or the Ecclesiastical Courts. He was certainly not a manufacturer, nor a merchant, nor a farmer. His name was unknown to scientific and learned societies, and he had never been known to take part in the serious discussions of the Royal Institution, the London Institution, the Artisan's Association, or the Institution of Arts and Sciences. In fact, he did not belong to any of the many societies that exist in the English capital, from the Harmonic to the Entomologists, which was founded mainly to fight against harmful insects.

Phileas Fogg was a member of the Reform Club, and that was all.

The way he was accepted into this exclusive club was simple enough.

He was recommended by the Barings bank, with whom he had an open line of credit. His cheques were regularly paid immediately from his current account, which always had plenty of money in it.

Was Phileas Fogg rich? Without doubt. But those who knew him best could not imagine how he had made his fortune, and Mr. Fogg was the last person you would ask for that information. He was not wasteful with money, but he was not mean either; whenever he knew that money was needed for a good, useful, or charitable purpose, he gave it quietly and sometimes without giving his name. In short, he was the least talkative of men. He spoke very little, and this quiet manner made him seem even more mysterious. His daily habits were easy to observe, but whatever he did was always exactly the same as what he had always done before, which made curious people quite puzzled.

Had he travelled? It seemed likely, because no one appeared to know the world better than he did. There was no place so hidden that he did not seem to know it well. He often corrected, with a few clear words, the many guesses made by club members about lost or unknown travellers, pointing out what was most probably true, and seeming almost to have a sixth sense, since events so often proved his predictions right. He must have travelled everywhere, at least in his mind.

What was certain was that Phileas Fogg had not left London for many years. Those who had the honour of knowing him better than the rest said that nobody could claim to have ever seen him anywhere else. His only hobbies were reading the newspapers and playing whist. He often won at this game, which, being a silent one, suited his nature well. But his winnings never went into his own pocket — they were kept as a fund for his charitable giving. Mr. Fogg played not to win, but for the pleasure of playing. The game was, in his eyes, a contest, a struggle against difficulty, yet a still and tireless struggle that suited his character perfectly.

Phileas Fogg was not known to have either a wife or children, which can happen to the most respectable people. He also had no relatives or close friends, which was certainly more unusual. He lived alone in his house in Saville Row, which nobody ever entered. One single servant was enough to look after him. He had breakfast and dinner at the club, at times that were fixed to the minute, in the same room, at the same table, never eating with other members, and certainly never bringing a guest. He went home at exactly midnight, only to go straight to bed. He never used the comfortable rooms that the Reform Club provides for its preferred members. He spent ten hours out of every twenty-four at Saville Row, either sleeping or getting dressed. When he chose to take a walk, it was with a steady step in the entrance hall with its mosaic floor, or in the circular gallery whose dome was held up by twenty red stone Ionic columns and lit by blue painted windows. When he had breakfast or dinner, all the resources of the club — its kitchens and pantries, its food and dairy stores — helped to fill his table with the finest food. He was served by the most serious waiters in dress coats and shoes with soft soles, who offered him food on special china and the finest linen. Club decanters of a style no longer made held his sherry, port, and spiced claret, while his drinks were kept pleasantly cool with ice brought at great cost from the American lakes.

If living in this style means being eccentric, it must be admitted that there is something good about being eccentric.

The house in Saville Row, though not grand, was extremely comfortable. The habits of its owner asked very little of the one servant, but Phileas Fogg required him to be almost superhumanly prompt and regular. On that very 2nd of October he had dismissed James Forster, because that unlucky young man had brought him shaving water at eighty-four degrees Fahrenheit instead of eighty-six. He was now waiting for his replacement, who was expected at the house between eleven and half past eleven.

Phileas Fogg was sitting upright in his armchair, his feet close together like a soldier on parade, his hands resting on his knees, his body straight, his head held up. He was steadily watching a complex clock that showed the hours, minutes, seconds, days, months, and years. At exactly half past eleven, Mr. Fogg would, following his daily habit, leave Saville Row and go to the Reform Club.

At that moment there was a knock at the door of the comfortable room where Phileas Fogg was sitting, and James Forster, the dismissed servant, appeared.

"The new servant," he said.

A young man of thirty came forward and bowed.

"You are French, I believe," asked Phileas Fogg, "and your name is John?"

"Jean, if you please, sir," replied the newcomer, "Jean Passepartout, a nickname that has stayed with me because I have a natural ability for moving from one job to another. I believe I'm honest, sir, but to speak plainly, I've had several different jobs. I've been a travelling singer, a circus rider, when I could jump like Leotard and walk a tightrope like Blondin. Then I became a gymnastics teacher to make better use of my skills. After that I was a fireman in Paris and helped at many large fires. But I left France five years ago, and, wanting to enjoy a quiet home life, I took a job as a servant here in England. Finding myself without work, and hearing that Monsieur Phileas Fogg was the most orderly and settled gentleman in the United Kingdom, I have come to you, sir, hoping to live a peaceful life and even forget the name Passepartout."

"Passepartout suits me," replied Mr. Fogg. "You come with good recommendations. I have heard good things about you. You know my conditions?"

"Yes, sir."

"Good. What time is it?"

"Twenty-two minutes past eleven," replied Passepartout, pulling an enormous silver watch from deep inside his pocket.

"Your watch is slow," said Mr. Fogg.

"I beg your pardon, sir, that is impossible—"

"You are four minutes slow. No matter. It is enough to point out the mistake. Now, from this moment, twenty-nine minutes past eleven in the morning, this Wednesday, 2nd October, you are in my service."

Phileas Fogg stood up, took his hat in his left hand, placed it on his head in one automatic movement, and walked out without a word.

Passepartout heard the street door close once — that was his new master going out. He heard it close again — that was his predecessor, James Forster, leaving in his turn. Passepartout remained alone in the house in Saville Row.
