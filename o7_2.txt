

  Mrs. Sheridan's voice came floating down the stairs. "Tell her to wear that lovely hat she had on last Sunday."

"Mother says you should wear that lovely hat you had on last Sunday. Good. One o'clock. Goodbye."

Laura put down the phone, threw her arms above her head, took a deep breath, stretched and let them fall. "Huh," she sighed, and the moment after the sigh she sat up quickly. She was still, listening. All the doors in the house seemed to be open. The house was full of soft, quick footsteps and busy voices. The green door that led to the kitchen area swung open and shut with a soft thud. And now there came a long, strange rumbling sound. It was the heavy piano being moved on its stiff wheels. But the air! If you stopped to notice — was the air always like this? Small gentle breezes were playing, coming in through the tops of the windows and going out through the doors. And there were two tiny spots of sunlight, one on the ink pot and one on a silver photo frame, playing too. Lovely little spots. Especially the one on the lid of the ink pot. It was quite warm. A warm little silver star. She could have kissed it.

The front doorbell rang loudly, and there was the sound of Sadie's skirt brushing on the stairs. A man's voice said something quietly. Sadie answered carelessly, "I'm sure I don't know. Wait. I'll ask Mrs. Sheridan."

"What is it, Sadie?" Laura came into the hall.

"It's the florist, Miss Laura."

It was indeed. There, just inside the door, stood a wide, shallow tray full of pots of pink lilies. No other kind. Nothing but lilies — canna lilies, big pink flowers, wide open, bright and almost frighteningly full of life on dark red stems.

"Oh — oh, Sadie!" said Laura, and the sound was like a little cry. She bent down as if to warm herself at those bright lilies. She felt as if they were in her fingers, on her lips, growing in her chest.

"It's a mistake," she said quietly. "Nobody ever ordered so many. Sadie, go and find mother."

But at that moment Mrs. Sheridan joined them.

"It's perfectly right," she said calmly. "Yes, I ordered them. Aren't they lovely?" She pressed Laura's arm. "I was passing the shop yesterday and I saw them in the window. And I suddenly thought, just once in my life I will have enough canna lilies. The garden party will be a good reason."

"But I thought you said you did not want to get involved," said Laura. Sadie had gone. The florist's man was still outside at his van. She put her arm around her mother's neck and very gently bit her mother's ear.

"My darling child, you wouldn't want a perfectly logical mother, would you? Don't do that. Here's the man."

He carried more lilies still — another whole tray.

"Put them together, just inside the door, on both sides of the entrance, please," said Mrs. Sheridan. "Don't you agree, Laura?"

"Oh, I do, mother."

In the sitting room, Meg, Jose and good little Hans had at last managed to move the piano.

"Now, if we put this sofa against the wall and move everything out of the room except the chairs, don't you think?"

"Yes, quite right."
