

   The detective walked quickly along the dock and hurried to the consul's office, where he was immediately shown in to see that official.

"Consul," he said, without any introduction, "I have strong reasons for believing that my man is a passenger on the Mongolia." And he told him what had just happened concerning the passport.

"Well, Mr. Fix," replied the consul, "I won't be sorry to see this criminal's face. But perhaps he won't come here — that is, if he is the person you think he is. A robber doesn't like leaving traces of his escape behind him. Besides, he is not required to have his passport officially stamped."

"If he is as clever as I think he is, consul, he will come."

"To have his passport stamped?"

"Yes. Passports are only useful for causing trouble to honest people and helping criminals to escape. I assure you it will be exactly the right thing for him to do. But I hope you will not stamp the passport."

"Why not? If the passport is genuine I have no right to refuse."

"Nevertheless, I must keep this man here until I can get a warrant of arrest from London."

"Ah, that's your problem. But I cannot—"

The consul did not finish his sentence, because at that moment there was a knock at the door, and two strangers entered. One of them was the servant that Fix had met on the dock. The other, who was his master, held out his passport with the request that the consul would do him the favour of stamping it. The consul took the document and read it carefully, while Fix watched the stranger hungrily from a corner of the room.

"You are Mr. Phileas Fogg?" said the consul, after reading the passport.

"I am."

"And this man is your servant?"

"He is. A Frenchman, named Passepartout."

"You are from London?"

"Yes."

"And you are going—"

"To Bombay."

"Very well, sir. You know that an official stamp is not actually needed, and that no passport is required?"

"I know that, sir," replied Phileas Fogg. "But I wish to prove, by your stamp, that I came through Suez."

"Very well, sir."

The consul signed and dated the passport, then added his official seal. Mr. Fogg paid the usual fee, bowed coldly, and went out, followed by his servant.

"Well?" asked the detective.

"Well, he looks and behaves like a perfectly honest man," replied the consul.

"Perhaps. But that is not the question. Do you think, consul, that this calm gentleman looks exactly like the robber whose description I have received?"

"I agree with that. But you know, all descriptions—"

"I'll make certain of it," interrupted Fix. "The servant seems to me less mysterious than the master. Besides, he is French, and can't help talking. Please excuse me for a short while, consul."

Fix went off in search of Passepartout.

Meanwhile, Mr. Fogg, after leaving the consulate, went to the dock, gave some instructions to Passepartout, went out to the Mongolia in a small boat, and went down to his cabin. He opened his notebook, which contained the following notes:

"Left London, Wednesday, October 2nd, at 8.45 p.m.

"Reached Paris, Thursday, October 3rd, at 7.20 a.m.

"Left Paris, Thursday, at 8.40 a.m.

"Reached Turin via Mont Cenis, Friday, October 4th, at 6.35 a.m.

"Left Turin, Friday, at 7.20 a.m.

"Arrived at Brindisi, Saturday, October 5th, at 4 p.m.

"Sailed on the Mongolia, Saturday, at 5 p.m.

"Reached Suez, Wednesday, October 9th, at 11 a.m.

"Total hours spent: 158½; or, in days, six days and a half."

These dates were written in a travel record divided into columns, showing the month, the day of the month, and the day — with both the planned and actual arrival times at each main location: Paris, Brindisi, Suez, Bombay, Calcutta, Singapore, Hong Kong, Yokohama, San Francisco, New York, and London — from the 2nd of October to the 21st of December. There was space for recording any time gained or lost at each place. This organised record contained everything needed, and Mr. Fogg could always tell whether he was behind or ahead of his schedule. On that Friday, the 9th of October, he noted his arrival at Suez and observed that he had so far neither gained nor lost any time. He sat down quietly to have breakfast in his cabin, without once thinking of going to look at the town, being one of those Englishmen who prefer to see foreign countries through the eyes of their servants.
