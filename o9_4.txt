

   Having won twenty guineas at whist and said goodbye to his friends, Phileas Fogg left the Reform Club at twenty-five minutes past seven.

Passepartout, who had carefully studied the programme of his duties, was more than surprised to see his master doing the unusual thing of arriving home at this unexpected hour. According to the rules, he was not due back at Saville Row until exactly midnight.

Mr. Fogg went to his bedroom and called out, "Passepartout!"

Passepartout did not reply. It could not be him being called — it was not the right hour.

"Passepartout!" repeated Mr. Fogg, without raising his voice.

Passepartout appeared.

"I called you twice," said his master.

"But it is not midnight," replied the other, showing his watch.

"I know. I'm not blaming you. We leave for Dover and Calais in ten minutes."

A confused smile spread across Passepartout's round face. He clearly had not understood his master.

"Is monsieur going to leave home?"

"Yes," replied Phileas Fogg. "We are going around the world."

Passepartout's eyes opened wide, his eyebrows went up, his hands went up too, and he looked as if he might fall over, so completely overwhelmed was he with astonishment.

"Around the world!" he whispered.

"In eighty days," replied Mr. Fogg. "So we haven't a moment to lose."

"But the trunks?" gasped Passepartout, shaking his head from side to side without realising it.

"We'll have no trunks. Only a travel bag, with two shirts and three pairs of socks for me, and the same for you. We'll buy our clothes along the way. Bring down my raincoat and travel cloak, and some strong shoes, though we shall do little walking. Hurry!"

Passepartout tried to reply, but could not. He went out, climbed to his own room, fell into a chair, and muttered: "That's just great! And I, who wanted to stay quietly at home!"

He mechanically began making preparations for departure. Around the world in eighty days! Was his master a fool? No. Was this a joke, then? They were going to Dover — fine! To Calais — fine too! After all, Passepartout, who had been away from France for five years, would not be unhappy to set foot on his home soil again. Perhaps they would even go as far as Paris, and it would be good for his eyes to see Paris once more. But surely a gentleman so careful with his steps would stop there — surely — but then again, it was no less true that this man, who had always stayed so close to home, was actually going away!

By eight o'clock Passepartout had packed the modest travel bag containing both his master's clothes and his own. Then, still troubled in his mind, he carefully shut the door of his room and went down to Mr. Fogg.

Mr. Fogg was completely ready. Under his arm could be seen a red-covered copy of Bradshaw's Continental Railway Steam Transit and General Guide, with its timetables showing the arrival and departure of steamers and railways. He took the travel bag, opened it, and slid into it a large roll of Bank of England banknotes, which would be accepted anywhere he might go.

"Have you forgotten anything?" he asked.

"Nothing, sir."

"My raincoat and travel cloak?"

"Here they are."

"Good. Take this bag," handing it to Passepartout. "Look after it carefully, because it contains twenty thousand pounds."

Passepartout nearly dropped the bag, as if the twenty thousand pounds were in gold coins and weighed him down.

Master and servant then went downstairs, the front door was double-locked, and at the end of Saville Row they took a taxi and drove quickly to Charing Cross. The taxi stopped in front of the railway station at twenty minutes past eight. Passepartout jumped off the box seat and followed his master, who, after paying the driver, was about to enter the station, when a poor woman beggar, holding a child in her arms, with bare feet covered in mud, a worn-out hat on her head from which hung a torn feather, and a ragged shawl around her shoulders, came up and sadly asked for charity.

Mr. Fogg took out the twenty guineas he had just won at whist and handed them to the beggar, saying, "Here, my good woman. I'm glad that I met you," and walked on.

Passepartout felt his eyes go wet. His master's action touched his gentle heart.

Two first-class tickets for Paris were quickly purchased, and Mr. Fogg was crossing the station towards the train when he saw his five friends from the Reform Club.

"Well, gentlemen," he said, "I'm off, as you can see. And if you examine my passport when I return, you will be able to judge whether I have completed the agreed journey."

"Oh, that won't be necessary at all, Mr. Fogg," said Ralph politely. "We will trust your word, as a man of honour."

"You haven't forgotten when you are due back in London?" asked Stuart.

"In eighty days. On Saturday, the 21st of December, 1872, at a quarter to nine in the evening. Goodbye, gentlemen."

Phileas Fogg and his servant took their seats in a first-class carriage at twenty minutes before nine. Five minutes later the whistle blew loudly, and the train slowly moved out of the station.

The night was dark, and a light, steady rain was falling. Phileas Fogg, comfortably settled in his corner, did not open his lips. Passepartout, not yet recovered from his amazement, held on tightly to the travel bag with its enormous treasure.

Just as the train was rushing through Sydenham, Passepartout suddenly gave a cry of despair.

"What's the matter?" asked Mr. Fogg.

"Alas! In my hurry — I — I forgot—"

"What?"

"To turn off the gas light in my room!"

"Very well, young man," replied Mr. Fogg calmly. "It will burn — at your expense."
