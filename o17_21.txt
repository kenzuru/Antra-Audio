VACATION was getting near. The teacher, always strict, grew stricter and more demanding than ever, for he wanted the school to perform well on "Exam" day. His rod and his paddle were seldom idle now -- at least among the smaller students. Only the biggest boys, and young ladies of eighteen and twenty, escaped punishment. Mr. Dobbins' punishments were very painful ones, too; for although he wore a wig over a perfectly bald and shiny head, he had only reached middle age, and there was no sign of weakness in his muscles. As the important day came closer, all the harshness in him came out; he seemed to take a mean pleasure in punishing the smallest mistakes. The result was that the younger boys spent their days in fear and pain and their nights plotting revenge. They missed no opportunity to do the teacher a small harm. But he stayed ahead all the time. The punishment that followed every successful act of revenge was so complete and powerful that the boys always came away badly beaten. At last they planned together and came up with a plan that promised a spectacular victory. They got the help of the sign painter's son, told him the plan, and asked for his help. He had his own reasons for being happy about it, for the teacher stayed at his father's house and had given the boy plenty of reason to dislike him. The teacher's wife was going to visit the country for a few days, and there would be nothing to stop the plan; the teacher always prepared himself for important occasions by getting quite drunk, and the sign painter's son said that when the teacher had reached the right condition on Exam Evening he would "handle the situation" while he napped in his chair; then he would have him woken at the right time and hurried away to school.

In due course the exciting event arrived. At eight in the evening the schoolhouse was brightly lit, and decorated with wreaths and chains of leaves and flowers. The teacher sat like a king in his great chair on a raised platform, with his blackboard behind him. He was looking fairly drunk. Three rows of benches on each side and six rows in front of him were occupied by the important people of the town and by the parents of the students. To his left, behind the rows of citizens, was a large temporary platform on which were seated the students who would take part in the evening's program; rows of small boys, washed and dressed to an uncomfortable degree; rows of awkward bigger boys; rows of girls and young ladies dressed in light cotton and fabric and clearly aware of their bare arms, their grandmothers' old jewelry, their bits of pink and blue ribbon and the flowers in their hair. All the rest of the room was filled with other students.

The program began. A very little boy stood up and shyly said, "You'd scarce expect one of my age to speak in public on the stage," etc. -- accompanying himself with painfully exact and jerky movements that a machine might have made -- if the machine were a bit out of order. But he got through safely, though terribly scared, and got a fine round of applause when he made his practiced bow and sat down.

A little shy girl spoke with a childlike lisp, "Mary had a little lamb," etc., made a sweet little curtsy, got her reward of applause, and sat down flushed and happy.

Tom Sawyer stepped forward with proud confidence and began the well-known "Give me liberty or give me death" speech, with great energy and wild gestures, and broke down in the middle of it. A terrible stage fright seized him, his legs shook under him and he could hardly speak. He had the clear sympathy of the audience but also their silence, which was even worse than their sympathy. The teacher frowned, and this finished the disaster. Tom struggled for a while and then stepped back, completely defeated. There was a weak attempt at applause, but it died quickly.

"The Boy Stood on the Burning Deck" followed; also "The Assyrian Came Down," and other favorite dramatic speeches. Then there were reading exercises, and a spelling competition. The small Latin class did very well. The main part of the evening was next -- original written pieces by the young ladies. Each in her turn stepped forward to the edge of the platform, cleared her throat, held up her manuscript (tied with a pretty ribbon), and read it with great attention to expression. The topics were the same that had been used at similar occasions by their mothers before them, their grandmothers, and no doubt all their female ancestors back to ancient times. "Friendship" was one; "Memories of Other Days"; "Religion in History"; "Dream Land"; "The Advantages of Culture"; "Forms of Political Government Compared and Contrasted"; "Melancholy"; "Love for Parents"; "Heart Longings," etc., etc.

A common feature in these compositions was an overdone and much-loved sadness; another was a wasteful use of "fancy words"; another was a tendency to force in particularly liked words and phrases until they were worn out; and a quality that clearly marked and weakened them was the expected moral lesson that appeared at the end of each and every one of them. No matter what the subject might be, a great effort was made to connect it to some moral or religious point that the reader could benefit from. The obvious lack of sincerity of these moral endings was not enough to cause the practice to disappear from schools, and it is not enough today; it never will be, perhaps. There is no school in all our land where the young ladies do not feel forced to end their writing with a moral lesson; and the moral lesson of the silliest and least religious girl in the school is always the longest and most strongly religious. But enough of this. Simple truth is unpleasant.

Let us return to the "Examination." The first piece that was read was one called "Is this, then, Life?" Perhaps the reader can deal with an excerpt from it:

"In the common walks of life, with what delightful feelings does the youthful mind look forward to some expected celebration! Imagination is busy drawing rose-colored pictures of joy. In fancy, the pleasure-loving follower of fashion sees herself among the festive crowd, 'the observed of all observers.' Her graceful form, dressed in beautiful white, is moving through the turns of the joyous dance; her eye is brightest, her step is lightest in the gay gathering.

"In such pleasant dreams time quickly passes, and the welcome hour arrives for her entrance into the ideal world she has dreamed of so brightly. How magical does everything appear to her eyes! Each new scene is more wonderful than the last. But after a while she finds that beneath this pleasant surface, all is empty; the compliments which once delighted her soul now sound hollow; the ballroom has lost its charm; and with worn health and a bitter heart, she turns away with the firm belief that earthly pleasures cannot satisfy the deepest longings of the soul!"

And so on. There were sounds of pleased reaction from time to time during the reading, accompanied by whispered words of "How sweet!" "How expressive!" "So true!" etc., and after the piece ended with a particularly strong moral lesson the applause was enthusiastic.

Then a slim, sad girl rose, whose face had the "interesting" paleness that comes from too many pills and poor digestion, and read a "poem." Two verses of it will be enough:

"A MISSOURI MAIDEN'S FAREWELL TO ALABAMA
Alabama, good-bye! I love thee well!
But yet for a while do I leave thee now!
Sad, yes, sad thoughts of thee my heart doth fill,
And burning memories crowd my brow!
For I have wandered through thy flowery woods;
Have roamed and read near Tallapoosa's stream;
Have listened to Tallassee's rushing floods,
And sought on Coosa's side the morning's gleam.

"Yet I am not ashamed to bear an emotional heart,
Nor blush to turn behind my tearful eyes;
'Tis from no stranger land I now must part,
'Tis to no strangers left I give these sighs.
Welcome and home were mine within this State,
Whose valleys I leave -- whose buildings fade from me --
And cold must be mine eyes, and heart, and head,
When, dear Alabama! they turn cold on thee!"

There were very few there who knew what "tete" meant, but the poem was very well received, nevertheless.

Next appeared a dark-haired, dark-eyed young lady, who paused an important moment, took on a dramatic expression, and began to read in a measured, serious tone:

"A VISION
Dark and stormy was the night. Around the throne on high not a single star appeared; but the deep sounds of heavy thunder shook the air constantly; while the terrifying lightning moved in angry flashes through the cloudy sky, seeming to ignore the power once controlled over it by the great Franklin! Even the noisy winds came forth from their hiding places, and rushed about as if to add their strength to the wildness of the scene.

"At such a time, so dark, so gloomy, longing for human company my very spirit ached; but instead, 'My dearest friend, my counselor, my comforter and guide -- My joy in grief, my second happiness in joy,' came to my side. She moved like one of those bright beings painted in the sunny walks of imagination by romantic minds, a queen of beauty wearing no decoration but her own superior beauty. So soft was her step, it made no sound, and but for the gentle touch of her kind hand, as other quiet beauties, she would have moved away unnoticed -- unwanted. A strange sadness rested upon her face, like cold tears on the coat of December, as she pointed to the fighting forces outside, and asked me to look at the two beings shown to me."

This dream took up about ten pages of writing and ended with a moral lesson so discouraging to anyone not of the right religion that it received the first prize. This piece was considered to be the very finest effort of the evening. The mayor of the village, in giving the prize to its author, made a warm speech in which he said it was by far the most "expressive" thing he had ever heard, and that Daniel Webster himself might well be proud of it.

It may be noted, in passing, that the number of pieces in which the word "beautiful" was overused, and human experience was called "life's page," was up to the usual amount.

Now the teacher, drunk almost to the point of friendliness, pushed his chair aside, turned his back to the audience, and began to draw a map of America on the blackboard, to test the geography class upon. But he made a poor job of it with his unsteady hand, and a hidden laugh ran through the room. He knew what the matter was, and tried to fix it. He wiped out lines and redrew them; but he only made them look stranger than before, and the laughter grew more obvious. He put all his attention on his work now, as if determined not to be beaten by the amusement. He felt that all eyes were on him; he imagined he was succeeding, and yet the laughter continued; it even clearly increased. And with good reason. There was an attic above, with a small opening over his head; and down through this opening came a cat, hung around the hips by a string; she had a cloth tied around her head and jaws to keep her from meowing; as she slowly came down she twisted upward and clawed at the string, she swung downward and clawed at the air. The laughter rose higher and higher -- the cat was within six inches of the focused teacher's head -- down, down, a little lower, and she grabbed his wig with her desperate claws, held on to it, and was pulled up into the attic in an instant with her prize still in her possession! And how the light did shine out from the teacher's bald head -- for the sign painter's boy had PAINTED IT GOLD!

That ended the meeting. The boys were avenged. Vacation had come.
