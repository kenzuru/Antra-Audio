
  
  It was very early in the morning. The sun had not come up yet, and all of Crescent Bay was hidden under a thick white mist. The large hills covered with bushes at the back were hidden too. You could not see where the hills ended and where the fields and small houses began. The sandy road had disappeared, and so had the fields and houses on the other side of it. There were no white sand hills covered with reddish grass beyond them. There was nothing to show where the beach ended and the sea began. Heavy dew had fallen during the night. The grass looked blue. Large drops of water hung on the bushes but did not fall. The soft, feathery toi-toi plants drooped on their long stems, and all the marigolds and pinks in the cottage gardens bent down to the ground because they were so wet. The cold fuchsias were soaked with water. Round drops of dew rested on the flat nasturtium leaves. It looked as if the sea had come up quietly in the dark, as if one enormous wave had come moving slowly forward—how far? Maybe if you had woken up in the middle of the night you might have seen a large fish come in through the window and disappear again....

Ah-Aah! said the sleepy sea. And from the bush came the sound of small streams flowing quickly and lightly, slipping between smooth stones, rushing into small pools full of ferns and then flowing out again. There was the sound of large drops falling on big leaves, and something else—what was it?—a faint movement, the snap of a twig, and then such silence that it felt as if someone was listening.

Around the corner of Crescent Bay, between large piles of broken rock, a group of sheep came walking with quick little steps. They were pressed close together, a small, moving, woolly group, and their thin stick-like legs moved quickly as if the cold and the quiet had frightened them. Behind them an old sheepdog, his wet paws covered with sand, ran along with his nose to the ground, but in a relaxed way, as if he was thinking about something else. Then in the rocky entrance the shepherd himself appeared. He was a thin, straight old man, wearing a rough coat covered with tiny drops of water, trousers tied below the knee, and a wide-brimmed hat with a folded blue cloth around it. One hand was pushed into his belt, the other held a very smooth yellow stick. As he walked slowly along, he made a very soft, light whistling sound, a gentle, distant tune that sounded sad and tender. The old dog did a couple of playful jumps and then stopped suddenly, as if embarrassed by his own silliness, and walked a few proud steps beside his master. The sheep moved forward in small quick rushes and began to make noise, and ghost-like sheep answered them from under the sea. "Baa! Baaa!" For a while they seemed to stay on the same piece of ground. Ahead lay the sandy road with shallow puddles, and the same wet bushes showed on each side, and the same dark fences. Then something very large came into view—an enormous figure with untidy hair and arms stretched out. It was the big gum tree outside Mrs. Stubbs' shop, and as they passed there was a strong smell of eucalyptus. Now large spots of light shone through the mist. The shepherd stopped whistling. He rubbed his red nose and wet beard on his wet sleeve and, narrowing his eyes, looked towards the sea. The sun was rising. It was amazing how quickly the mist became thin, moved away, disappeared from the flat land, rolled up from the bush and was gone, as if in a hurry to leave. Big twists and curls of mist pushed against each other as the silver light spread wider. The distant sky—bright, pure blue—was reflected in the puddles, and the drops of water on the telegraph poles shone like points of light. Now the moving, shining sea was so bright it hurt your eyes to look at it. The shepherd took a pipe—the bowl as small as an acorn—out of his chest pocket, searched for a piece of dark tobacco, cut off a few small bits and filled the bowl. He was a serious, handsome old man. As he lit the pipe and blue smoke drifted around his head, the dog watched him with a proud look.

"Baa! Baaa!" The sheep spread out into a fan shape. They had just passed the summer houses before the first sleeping person turned over and lifted a tired head. Their cries entered the dreams of small children... who lifted their arms to pull down and hold the sweet little woolly lambs of sleep. Then the first person appeared outside. It was the Burnells' cat Florrie, sitting on the gatepost, much too early as always, looking out for their milk girl. When she saw the old sheepdog she jumped up quickly, curved her back, pulled in her striped head, and seemed to give a small disgusted shiver. "How unpleasant! What a rough, disgusting creature!" said Florrie. But the old sheepdog, not looking up, moved past with his legs swinging out to the sides. Only one of his ears moved slightly to show that he had seen her, and thought she was a foolish young female.

The morning breeze moved through the bush, and the smell of leaves and wet dark earth mixed with the sharp smell of the sea. Thousands of birds were singing. A goldfinch flew over the shepherd's head and, sitting on the very top of a branch, turned towards the sun, ruffling the small feathers on its chest. Now they had passed the fisherman's hut, passed the dark little house where Leila the milk girl lived with her old grandmother. The sheep moved out over a yellow swamp and Wag, the sheepdog, padded after them, gathered them together and directed them towards the steeper, narrower rocky path that led out of Crescent Bay and towards Daylight Cove. "Baa! Baa!" The sound came faintly as they moved along the quickly drying road. The shepherd put his pipe away, dropping it into his chest pocket so that the small bowl hung over the edge. And right away the soft, light whistling began again. Wag ran out along a ledge of rock after something that smelled interesting, and came back looking disgusted. Then, pushing and hurrying, the sheep went around the bend and the shepherd followed them out of sight.
