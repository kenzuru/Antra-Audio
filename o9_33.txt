
  An hour later, the "Henrietta" passed the lighthouse at the entrance of the Hudson, went around the point of Sandy Hook, and headed out to sea. During the day she sailed along Long Island, passed Fire Island, and headed quickly eastward.

At noon the next day, a man climbed up to the bridge to check the ship's position. You might think this was Captain Speedy. Not at all. It was Phileas Fogg himself. As for Captain Speedy, he was locked in his cabin, shouting loudly — angry in a way that was understandable, if a little extreme.

What had happened was quite simple. Phileas Fogg wanted to go to Liverpool, but the captain refused to take him there. So Phileas Fogg had paid for a passage to Bordeaux, and during the thirty hours he had been on board, had worked so cleverly with his bank notes that the sailors and engine workers — who were only a temporary crew and were not on the best terms with the captain — came over to his side completely. This was why Phileas Fogg was now in command instead of Captain Speedy, why the captain was a prisoner in his cabin, and why the "Henrietta" was now heading towards Liverpool. It was very clear, from the way Mr. Fogg handled the ship, that he had once been a sailor.

How the story ended will be seen soon. Aouda was worried, though she said nothing. As for Passepartout, he thought Mr. Fogg's move was simply brilliant. The captain had said "between eleven and twelve knots," and the "Henrietta" was proving him right.

If then — and there were still many "ifs" — the sea did not become too rough, if the wind did not turn to the east, and if nothing went wrong with the ship or its engines, the "Henrietta" might cross the three thousand miles from New York to Liverpool in nine days, between the 12th and the 21st of December. It was true, however, that once arrived, the events on board the "Henrietta," added to the Bank of England matter, might cause more problems for Mr. Fogg than he imagined or wanted.

During the first days, everything went smoothly enough. The sea was not too rough, the wind seemed steady from the north-east, the sails were raised, and the "Henrietta" moved through the waves like a real Atlantic steamer.

Passepartout was delighted. His master's latest adventure — the consequences of which he did not fully understand — filled him with admiration. No one had ever seen such a cheerful and skilful fellow on a ship. He made good friends with the sailors, and amazed them with his physical tricks. He felt they managed the ship like true gentlemen, and that the engine workers fired the furnaces like heroes. His cheerful and talkative personality affected everyone around him. He had forgotten the past — its worries and delays. He thought only of the goal, which was so nearly reached. Sometimes he became almost too excited to contain himself, as if heated by the furnaces of the "Henrietta." Often, too, the honest fellow walked around Fix, looking at him with sharp, suspicious eyes, but he did not speak to him, for their old friendship no longer existed.

Fix, it must be said, understood nothing of what was happening. The taking over of the "Henrietta," the bribing of the crew, Fogg handling the ship like an experienced sailor — all of this confused and surprised him completely. He did not know what to think. After all, a man who had begun by stealing fifty-five thousand pounds might end by stealing a ship. Fix could not help concluding that the "Henrietta," under Fogg's command, was not going to Liverpool at all, but to some part of the world where the criminal, turned into a pirate, would quietly hide himself away. This idea was at least possible, and the detective began to seriously regret that he had got involved in this affair.

As for Captain Speedy, he continued to shout and complain in his cabin. Passepartout, whose job it was to bring him his meals, was brave but very careful.  Mr. Fogg did not even seem to know there was a captain on board.

On the 13th they passed the edge of the Banks of Newfoundland, a dangerous area. In winter especially, there are frequent thick fogs and strong storms. Since the evening before, the barometer had suddenly dropped, warning of a coming change in the weather. During the night the temperature changed, the cold became sharper, and the wind turned to the south-east.

This was bad luck. Mr. Fogg, not wanting to go off course, brought down his sails and used more engine power. But the ship slowed down because of the rough sea — the long waves hit hard against the back of the ship. She moved up and down violently, and this slowed her progress. The wind gradually grew into a storm, and there was a real fear that the "Henrietta" might not be able to stay upright on the waves.

Passepartout's face became as dark as the sky, and for two days the poor fellow was constantly frightened. But Phileas Fogg was a brave sailor who knew how to keep a ship moving forward against a rough sea. He kept on his course without even reducing engine power. The "Henrietta," when she could not rise over the waves, went straight through them, flooding her deck with water, but passing safely. Sometimes the propeller came out of the water, spinning uselessly when a huge wave lifted the back of the ship above the waves. But the ship always kept going straight ahead.

The wind, however, did not grow as strong as had been feared. It was not one of those terrible storms that arrive suddenly and blow at ninety miles an hour. It stayed strong but, unfortunately, it remained stubbornly in the south-east, making the sails useless.

The 16th of December was the seventy-fifth day since Phileas Fogg's departure from London, and the "Henrietta" had not yet suffered any serious delay. Almost half the voyage was done, and the most dangerous areas had been passed. In summer, success would have been nearly certain. In winter, they were at the mercy of bad weather. Passepartout said nothing, but held onto hope quietly and comforted himself with the thought that if the wind let them down, they could still count on the engine.

On that day the engineer came up on deck, went to Mr. Fogg, and began to speak to him seriously. Without knowing why — perhaps it was just a feeling — Passepartout became vaguely worried. He would have given one ear to hear what the engineer was saying with the other. He finally managed to catch a few words, and was sure he heard his master say: "You are certain of what you tell me?"

"Certain, sir," replied the engineer. "You must remember that since we left, we have kept all our furnaces running at full heat. We had enough coal to go at reduced engine power from New York to Bordeaux, but we do not have enough to go at full engine power from New York to Liverpool."

"I will think about it," replied Mr. Fogg.

Passepartout understood everything. He was filled with terrible worry. The coal was running out! "Ah, if my master can get past this," he muttered to himself, "he will truly be a remarkable man!" He could not help telling Fix what he had heard.

"So you really believe we are going to Liverpool?"

"Of course."

"Idiot!" replied the detective, shrugging his shoulders and walking away.

Passepartout was about to respond angrily to that word, which he could not begin to understand. But he thought that poor Fix was probably very disappointed and embarrassed after having so clumsily followed the wrong person all around the world — and he held back.

And now, what would Phileas Fogg do? It was hard to imagine. But he seemed to have already decided, because that evening he called for the engineer and said: "Keep all the furnaces going until the coal is completely used up."

A short time later, the chimney of the "Henrietta" was pouring out thick clouds of smoke. The ship continued at full power. But on the 18th, the engineer announced, just as he had predicted, that the coal would run out before the end of the day.

"Do not let the fires go out," replied Mr. Fogg. "Keep them burning to the very last. Fill the valves."

Around noon, Phileas Fogg, after checking their position, called Passepartout and ordered him to go and release Captain Speedy. It was as if the honest fellow had been told to unchain a wild animal. He went to the back of the ship, saying to himself: "He will be like a madman!"

A few moments later, with shouts and curses, an explosion of a man appeared at the back of the ship. The explosion was Captain Speedy. He looked as if he was about to burst with anger.

"Where are we?" were the first words his anger let him speak. If the poor man had been someone with a heart condition, he could never have survived such a burst of rage.

"Where are we?" he repeated, his face purple.

"Seven hundred and seven miles from Liverpool," replied Mr. Fogg, with perfect calmness.

"Pirate!" shouted Captain Speedy.

"I have called for you, sir—"

"Criminal!"

"— sir," continued Mr. Fogg, "to ask you to sell me your ship."

"No! By all the devils, no!"

"But I will have to burn it."

"Burn the 'Henrietta'!"

"Yes, at least the wooden upper part. The coal has run out."

"Burn my ship!" cried Captain Speedy, who could hardly get the words out. "A ship worth fifty thousand dollars!"

"Here is sixty thousand," replied Phileas Fogg, handing the captain a roll of bank notes.

This had an amazing effect on Andrew Speedy. An American can rarely stay calm at the sight of sixty thousand dollars. The captain forgot in an instant all his anger, his imprisonment, and all his complaints about his passenger. The "Henrietta" was twenty years old — it was a great deal. The explosion would not happen after all. Mr. Fogg had removed the fuse.

"And I will still have the iron hull," said the captain, in a calmer voice.

"The iron hull and the engine. Is it agreed?"

"Agreed."

And Andrew Speedy took the bank notes, counted them, and put them in his pocket.

During this conversation, Passepartout had gone completely white, and Fix looked as if he was about to faint. Nearly twenty thousand pounds had been spent, and Fogg was leaving the hull and engine to the captain — that is, almost the full value of the ship! It was true, however, that fifty-five thousand pounds had been stolen from the Bank.

When Andrew Speedy had put the money safely away, Mr. Fogg said to him: "Do not be surprised, sir. You should know that I will lose twenty thousand pounds unless I arrive in London by a quarter before nine on the evening of the 21st of December. I missed the steamer at New York, and since you refused to take me to Liverpool—"

"And I was right!" cried Andrew Speedy. "Because I have gained at least forty thousand dollars from it!" Then, in a calmer tone, he added: "Do you know something, Captain—"

"Fogg."

"Captain Fogg, you have something of the American spirit about you."

And, having given his passenger what he thought was a great compliment, he turned to go. But Mr. Fogg said: "The ship now belongs to me?"

"Certainly, from the bottom to the top of the masts — all the wood, that is."

"Very well. Have the inside seats, beds, and wooden frames pulled out and burned."

They needed dry wood to keep the steam at the right pressure, and that day the back deck, cabins, beds, and spare deck were used as fuel. The next day, the 19th of December, the masts, spare timber, and poles were burned. The crew worked hard to keep the furnaces going. Passepartout cut, chopped, and worked with all his strength. Everyone seemed possessed by a desire to take apart the ship.

The railings, fittings, most of the deck and upper sides disappeared on the 20th, and the "Henrietta" was now nothing more than a flat hull. But on that same day they spotted the Irish coast and Fastnet lighthouse. By ten in the evening they were passing Queenstown. Phileas Fogg had only twenty-four more hours to get to London. That amount of time was exactly what was needed to reach Liverpool — if the engine kept going at full power. And the steam was about to run out completely!

"Sir," said Captain Speedy, who was now very interested in Mr. Fogg's situation, "I truly feel sorry for you. Everything is working against you. We are only opposite Queenstown."

"Ah," said Mr. Fogg. "Is that place where we can see the lights Queenstown?"

"Yes."

"Can we enter the harbour?"

"Not for three hours. Only at high tide."

"Wait," replied Mr. Fogg calmly, without showing in his face that he was about to try, through a sudden brilliant idea, to overcome bad luck one more time.

Queenstown is the Irish port where Atlantic steamers stop to drop off the mail. These letters are taken to Dublin by express trains that are always ready to leave. From Dublin they are sent on to Liverpool by the fastest boats, saving twelve hours compared to the Atlantic steamers.

Phileas Fogg planned to gain those same twelve hours. Instead of arriving in Liverpool the next evening on the "Henrietta," he would be there by midday, and would therefore have time to reach London before a quarter before nine in the evening.

The "Henrietta" entered Queenstown harbour at one o'clock in the morning, at high tide. Phileas Fogg, after shaking hands warmly with Captain Speedy, left that gentleman on the flattened hull of his ship — which was still worth half of what he had sold it for.

The group went ashore immediately. Fix was greatly tempted to arrest Mr. Fogg on the spot, but he did not. Why? What was happening inside him? Had he changed his mind about his suspect? Did he finally understand that he had made a serious mistake? He did not, however, leave Mr. Fogg's side. They all got onto the train, which was just about to leave, at half past one. At daybreak they were in Dublin, and they lost no time getting on a steamer which, instead of rising over the waves, cut straight through them.

Phileas Fogg finally arrived on the Liverpool quay at twenty minutes before twelve on the 21st of December. He was only six hours away from London.

But at that moment Fix came up, put his hand on Mr. Fogg's shoulder, and, showing his official document, said: "You are really Phileas Fogg?"

"I am."

"I arrest you in the Queen's name!"
