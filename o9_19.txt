

Hong Kong is an island that came into English possession through the Treaty of Nankin, after the war of 1842. The English talent for building had created on it an important city and an excellent harbour. The island is situated at the mouth of the Canton River, and is separated by about sixty miles from the Portuguese town of Macao on the opposite coast. Hong Kong had beaten Macao in the competition for Chinese trade, and now most of the transportation of Chinese goods passes through it. Docks, hospitals, wharves, a Gothic cathedral, a government building, and paved streets give Hong Kong the look of a town in Kent or Surrey that has been magically moved to the other side of the world.

Passepartout walked with his hands in his pockets towards the Victoria port, looking as he went at the unusual carriages and other forms of transport, and at the groups of Chinese, Japanese, and Europeans passing back and forth in the streets. Hong Kong seemed to him not unlike Bombay, Calcutta, and Singapore, since it too showed clearly everywhere the signs of English power. At the Victoria port he found a disorganised mix of ships of all nations — English, French, American, and Dutch warships and trading vessels, Japanese and Chinese boats, and flower boats, which made up many floating gardens. Among the crowd Passepartout noticed a number of very old men dressed in yellow. Going into a barber's shop to be shaved, he learned that these old men were all at least eighty years old, at which age they were allowed to wear yellow, which is the royal colour. Passepartout, without quite knowing why, found this very amusing.

When he reached the dock where they were to board the "Carnatic," he was not surprised to find Fix walking up and down. The detective looked very troubled and disappointed.

"That's bad news for the gentlemen of the Reform Club!" Passepartout said quietly to himself. He went up to Fix with a cheerful smile, as though he had not noticed the man's frustration. The detective did indeed have good reasons to complain about his bad luck. The warrant had still not come! It was certainly on its way, but it could not now reach Hong Kong for several more days. Since this was the last English territory on Mr. Fogg's route, the man he was chasing would escape, unless he could find a way to delay him.

"Well, Mr. Fix," said Passepartout, "have you decided to come with us as far as America?"

"Yes," replied Fix, speaking through his teeth.

"Good!" exclaimed Passepartout, laughing heartily. "I knew you could not bring yourself to leave us. Come and reserve your cabin."

They entered the shipping office and booked cabins for four people. The clerk, as he gave them the tickets, told them that the repairs on the "Carnatic" were finished, and the steamer would leave that very evening — not the next morning as had been announced.

"That will suit my master even better," said Passepartout. "I will go and let him know."

Fix now decided to be bold. He resolved to tell Passepartout everything. It seemed to be the only possible way of keeping Phileas Fogg in Hong Kong for a few more days. He therefore invited his companion into a drinking house that caught his eye on the dock. When they entered, they found themselves in a large, well-decorated room, at the far end of which was a large bed covered with cushions. Several people were lying on this bed in a deep sleep. At the small tables arranged around the room, about thirty customers were drinking English beer, dark beer, gin, and brandy, while smoking long red clay pipes filled with small balls of opium mixed with rose oil. From time to time one of the smokers, overcome by the drug, would slide under the table, whereupon the waiters would take him by the head and feet and carry him to lay on the bed. The bed already held twenty such senseless people.

Fix and Passepartout saw that they were in a smoking den, visited by those sad, hollow-faced, blank-eyed people to whom English merchants sell every year the miserable drug called opium — worth more than one million four hundred thousand pounds — tens of thousands spent on one of the most shameful habits that trouble humanity. The Chinese government had tried without success to deal with the problem through strict laws. The habit gradually spread from the rich, for whom it was at first exclusively intended, to the lower classes, and from that point its spread could not be stopped. Opium is smoked everywhere, at all hours of the day, by men and women across China. Once people become used to it, they cannot give it up without suffering terrible physical pain. A heavy smoker can smoke as many as eight pipes a day, but dies within five years. It was in one of these places that Fix and Passepartout, looking for a friendly drink, now found themselves. Passepartout had no money, but he happily accepted Fix's invitation, hoping to return the kindness at some point in the future.

They ordered two bottles of port wine, which the Frenchman drank freely while Fix watched him closely. They talked about the journey, and Passepartout was especially cheerful at the idea that Fix was going to continue it with them. When the bottles were empty, however, he stood up to go and tell his master about the change in the "Carnatic's" departure time.

Fix caught him by the arm and said, "Wait a moment."

"What for, Mr. Fix?"

"I need to have a serious talk with you."

"A serious talk!" cried Passepartout, finishing the last drop of wine in his glass. "Well, we can talk about it tomorrow. I have no time now."

"Stay! What I have to say concerns your master."

At this, Passepartout looked carefully at his companion. Fix's face seemed to show an unusual expression. He sat down again.

"What is it you want to say?"

Fix placed his hand on Passepartout's arm, lowered his voice, and said, "You have guessed who I am?"

"Of course!" said Passepartout, smiling.

"Then I'm going to tell you everything—"

"Now that I already know everything, my friend! That's very kind. But go on. First, though, let me tell you that those gentlemen have spent their money for nothing."

"For nothing!" said Fix. "You speak with great confidence. It's clear you don't know how large the sum is."

"Of course I do," returned Passepartout. "Twenty thousand pounds."

"Fifty-five thousand!" answered Fix, pressing his companion's hand.

"What!" cried the Frenchman. "Has Mr. Fogg dared — fifty-five thousand pounds! Well, that's all the more reason not to waste a single moment," he continued, jumping up quickly.

Fix pushed Passepartout back into his chair and went on: "Fifty-five thousand pounds. And if I succeed, I get two thousand pounds. If you help me, I'll give you five hundred of them."

"Help you?" cried Passepartout, his eyes wide open.

"Yes, help me keep Mr. Fogg here for two or three days."

"Why, what are you saying? Those gentlemen are not satisfied with following my master and doubting his honour, but now they want to put obstacles in his way! I am ashamed of them!"

"What do you mean?"

"I mean that it is a disgraceful trick. They might as well rob Mr. Fogg on the road and take his money!"

"That is exactly what we intend to do."

"A real conspiracy!" cried Passepartout, who was becoming more and more worked up as the wine went to his head — for he had been drinking without noticing it. "And from gentlemen, too! How terrible!"

Fix was beginning to be confused.

"Members of the Reform Club!" continued Passepartout. "You should know, Mr. Fix, that my master is an honest man and that, when he makes a bet, he tries to win it fairly!"

"But who do you think I am?" asked Fix, looking at him carefully.

"Of course! An agent of the Reform Club members, sent out here to interfere with my master's journey. But although I worked this out some time ago, I have been careful not to mention it to Mr. Fogg."

"He knows nothing, then?"

"Nothing," replied Passepartout, emptying his glass again.

The detective passed his hand across his forehead, thinking before he spoke again. What should he do? Passepartout's mistake seemed genuine, but it made his plan more difficult. It was clear that the servant was not the master's partner in crime, as Fix had been inclined to suspect.

"Well," the detective said to himself, "since he is not an accomplice, he will help me."

He had no time to lose. Fogg had to be kept in Hong Kong, so he decided to be completely honest.

"Listen to me," said Fix directly. "I am not, as you think, an agent of the Reform Club members—"

"Come now!" replied Passepartout, with a mocking expression.

"I am a police detective, sent here by the London office."

"You, a detective?"

"I will prove it. Here is my identification."

Passepartout was speechless with amazement when Fix showed him this document, whose genuineness could not be questioned.

"Mr. Fogg's wager," Fix continued, "is only an excuse, by which you and the gentlemen of the Reform have been fooled. He had a reason for securing your innocent involvement."

"But why?"

"Listen. On September 28th last, a robbery of fifty-five thousand pounds was carried out at the Bank of England by a person whose description was fortunately obtained. Here is that description. It matches exactly that of Mr. Phileas Fogg."

"What nonsense!" cried Passepartout, hitting the table with his fist. "My master is the most honourable of men!"

"How can you say that? You know almost nothing about him. You entered his service the very day he left, and he left without any luggage, carrying a large amount in banknotes. And yet you are brave enough to claim he is an honest man!"

"Yes, yes," repeated the poor fellow, automatically.

"Would you like to be arrested as his partner in crime?"

Passepartout, shaken by what he had heard, held his head in his hands and did not dare to look at the detective. Phileas Fogg, the rescuer of Aouda, that brave and generous man — a criminal! And yet how much evidence seemed to point against him! Passepartout tried to push away the suspicions forcing themselves into his mind. He did not want to believe that his master was guilty.

"Well, what do you want of me?" he said at last, with great effort.

"Look here," replied Fix. "I have followed Mr. Fogg here, but I have not yet received the warrant of arrest that I sent to London for. You must help me keep him here in Hong Kong—"

"I! But—"

"I will share with you the two thousand pounds reward offered by the Bank of England."

"Never!" replied Passepartout, who tried to stand up but fell back, worn out in mind and body.

"Mr. Fix," he said with difficulty, "even if what you say is true — if my master is really the criminal you are looking for — which I deny — I have been, and still am, in his service. I have seen his generosity and kindness, and I will never betray him — not for all the gold in the world. I come from a village where people don't do that sort of thing!"

"You refuse?"

"I refuse."

"Consider that I have said nothing," said Fix. "And let us drink."

"Yes, let us drink!"

Passepartout felt himself falling more and more under the effects of the alcohol. Fix, knowing that he absolutely had to separate Passepartout from his master, wanted to completely overcome him. Some pipes of opium lay on the table. Fix slipped one into Passepartout's hand. He took it, put it between his lips, lit it, drew several puffs, and his head, growing heavy under the effects of the drug, fell forward on the table.

"At last!" said Fix, seeing Passepartout senseless. "Mr. Fogg will not be told about the 'Carnatic's' early departure, and even if he finds out, he will have to leave without this troublesome Frenchman!"

After paying the bill, Fix left the drinking house.
