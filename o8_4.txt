

  "Wait for me, Isa-bel! Kezia, wait for me!"

There was poor little Lottie, left behind again, because she found it terribly hard to climb over the fence by herself. When she stood on the first step her knees started to shake and she held on to the post. Then you had to put one leg over. But which leg? She could never decide. And when she finally put one leg over with a kind of desperate stamp—the feeling was awful. She was half in the field and half in the rough grass. She held on to the post desperately and called out loudly. "Wait for me!"

"No, don't wait for her, Kezia!" said Isabel. "She's such a little fool. She's always making a fuss. Come on!" And she pulled Kezia's top. "You can use my bucket if you come with me," she said kindly. "It's bigger than yours." But Kezia couldn't leave Lottie there alone. She ran back to her. By this time Lottie's face was very red and she was breathing hard.

"Here, put your other foot over," said Kezia.

"Where?"

Lottie looked down at Kezia as if from a great height.

"Here where my hand is." Kezia touched the spot.

"Oh, there do you mean!" Lottie breathed out deeply and put the second foot over.

"Now—sort of turn around and sit down and slide," said Kezia.

"But there's nothing to sit down on, Kezia," said Lottie.

She managed it at last, and once it was done she shook herself and began to smile.

"I'm getting better at climbing over fences, aren't I, Kezia?"

Lottie was a very hopeful person.

The pink and the blue sun hat followed Isabel's bright red sun hat up the sliding, slipping hill. At the top they stopped to decide where to go and to look carefully at who was already there. Seen from behind, standing against the sky, making big movements with their spades, they looked like tiny confused explorers.

The whole Samuel Josephs family was there already with their helper, who sat on a folding chair and kept order with a whistle she wore around her neck, and a small stick with which she directed activities. The Samuel Josephs children never played by themselves or ran their own games. If they did, it ended with the boys pouring water down the girls' necks or the girls trying to put small black crabs into the boys' pockets. So Mrs. S. J. and the poor helper made what she called a "programme" every morning to keep them "amused and out of mischief." It was all competitions or races or group games. Everything started with a loud blast of the helper's whistle and ended with another. There were even prizes—large, rather dirty paper packages which the helper with a sour little smile took out of a full bag. The Samuel Josephs children fought hard for the prizes and cheated and pinched each other's arms—they were all expert at pinching. The only time the Burnell children ever played with them, Kezia had won a prize, and when she unwrapped three layers of paper she found a very small rusty button hook. She couldn't understand why they made such a fuss....

But they never played with the Samuel Josephs now or even went to their parties. The Samuel Josephs were always giving children's parties at the Bay and there was always the same food. A big washing bowl full of very dark fruit salad, bread rolls cut into four pieces and a large jug full of something the helper called "Lemonade." And you went away in the evening with half the decoration torn off your dress or something spilled all down the front of your pinafore, while the Samuel Josephs jumped like wild children on their lawn. No! They were too dreadful.

On the other side of the beach, close to the water, two little boys with their shorts rolled up moved quickly like spiders. One was digging, the other ran back and forth to the water, filling a small bucket. They were the Trout boys, Pip and Rags. But Pip was so busy digging and Rags was so busy helping that they didn't see their little cousins until they were very close.

"Look!" said Pip. "Look what I've found." And he showed them an old wet, squashed-looking boot. The three little girls stared.

"What are you going to do with it?" asked Kezia.

"Keep it, of course!" Pip was very dismissive. "It's a find—understand?"

Yes, Kezia understood that. All the same....

"There are lots of things buried in the sand," explained Pip. "They get washed up from shipwrecks. Treasure. You might find—"

"But why does Rags have to keep pouring water in?" asked Lottie.

"Oh, that's to make it wet," said Pip, "to make the digging a bit easier. Keep it up, Rags."

And good little Rags ran back and forth, pouring in the water that turned brown like cocoa.

"Here, shall I show you what I found yesterday?" said Pip in a mysterious voice, and he pushed his spade into the sand. "Promise not to tell."

They promised.

"Say, cross my heart, I really mean it."

The little girls said it.

Pip took something out of his pocket, rubbed it for a long time on the front of his top, then breathed on it and rubbed it again.

"Now turn around!" he said.

They turned around.

"All look the same way! Stay still! Now!"

And his hand opened. He held up to the light something that flashed, that winked, that was a most beautiful green.

"It's an emerald," said Pip seriously.

"Is it really, Pip?" Even Isabel was impressed.

The beautiful green thing seemed to dance in Pip's fingers. Aunt Beryl had an emerald in a ring, but it was a very small one. This one was as big as a star and much more beautiful.
