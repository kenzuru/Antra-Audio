
  The people living in Saville Row would have been very surprised the next day if someone had told them that Phileas Fogg had come home. His doors and windows were still shut, and nothing seemed to have changed.

After leaving the station, Mr. Fogg gave Passepartout instructions to buy some food, and quietly went home.

He faced his misfortune with his usual calm. Ruined! And because of the detective's mistake! After travelling so steadily along that long journey, overcoming a hundred obstacles, facing many dangers, and still finding time to do some good along the way — to fail so close to the end because of a sudden event he could not have seen coming and had no way to fight against — it was terrible! Only a few pounds remained from the large sum he had taken with him. All that was left of his fortune was the twenty thousand pounds deposited at Barings, and that amount he owed to his friends at the Reform Club. The cost of his trip had been so great that, even if he had won, it would not have made him richer. And it was likely that he had never been trying to get richer — he was the kind of man who made bets for the sake of honour rather than for the money. But this bet had completely ruined him.

Mr. Fogg's mind, however, was made up. He knew what he still had to do.

A room in the house in Saville Row was prepared for Aouda, who was deeply upset by her protector's misfortune. From the few words Mr. Fogg let drop, she could see that he was planning something serious.

Knowing that Englishmen driven by a fixed idea sometimes turn to the desperate act of taking their own life, Passepartout kept close watch over his master, though he was careful not to let it show.

First of all, the good fellow had gone up to his room and turned off the gas lamp, which had been burning for eighty days. He had found a bill from the gas company in the letter box, and thought it was well past time to stop this expense, which he had been forced to carry.

The night passed. Mr. Fogg went to bed, but did he sleep? Aouda did not close her eyes once. Passepartout kept watch all night, like a loyal dog, at his master's door.

Mr. Fogg called him in the morning and told him to prepare breakfast for Aouda, and a cup of tea and a piece of meat for himself. He asked Aouda to excuse him from both breakfast and dinner, as he would be busy all day putting his affairs in order. In the evening he would ask her permission to speak with her for a few minutes.

Passepartout, having received his orders, had nothing to do but follow them. He looked at his calm and steady master, and could hardly bring himself to leave him. His heart was heavy, and his conscience hurt with guilt — for he blamed himself now more than ever for being the cause of the complete disaster. Yes! If he had warned Mr. Fogg and had told him about Fix's plans, his master would certainly not have given the detective passage to Liverpool, and then—

Passepartout could hold back no longer.

"My master! Mr. Fogg!" he cried. "Why do you not blame me? It was my fault that—"

"I blame no one," replied Phileas Fogg, with complete calmness. "Go!"

Passepartout left the room and went to find Aouda, to whom he passed on his master's message.

"Madam," he added, "I can do nothing myself — nothing! I have no power over my master. But you, perhaps—"

"What power could I have?" replied Aouda. "Mr. Fogg is influenced by no one. Has he ever understood how deeply grateful I am to him? Has he ever seen what is in my heart? My friend, he must not be left alone for even a moment. You say he wants to speak with me this evening?"

"Yes, madam — probably to make arrangements for your safety and comfort in England."

"We shall see," replied Aouda, suddenly becoming thoughtful.

Throughout that day — Sunday — the house in Saville Row was as quiet as if no one lived there. Phileas Fogg, for the first time since he had lived in that house, did not go to his club when the Westminster clock struck half past eleven.

Why would he go to the Reform Club? His friends no longer expected him there. Since Phileas Fogg had not appeared in the saloon on the previous evening — Saturday, the 21st of December, at a quarter before nine — he had lost his bet. It was not even necessary to go to his bank for the twenty thousand pounds, since his opponents already had his signed cheque and only needed to complete it and send it to Barings for the money to be transferred to them.

Mr. Fogg, therefore, had no reason to go out, and so he stayed at home. He shut himself in his room and was busy putting his affairs in order. Passepartout kept going up and down the stairs. The hours seemed long to him. He listened at his master's door and looked through the keyhole, as if he had every right to do so, as if he feared that something terrible might happen at any moment. Sometimes he thought of Fix, but no longer with anger. Fix, like everyone else, had been wrong about Phileas Fogg and had only done his duty by following and arresting him. While he, Passepartout... This thought would not leave him, and he could not stop blaming himself for his terrible mistake.

Finding himself too unhappy to stay alone, he knocked at Aouda's door, went into her room, sat down without speaking in a corner, and looked sadly at the young woman. Aouda was still deep in thought.

At about half past seven in the evening, Mr. Fogg sent to ask if Aouda would receive him, and a few moments later he found himself alone with her.

Phileas Fogg took a chair and sat down near the fireplace, facing Aouda. No emotion could be seen on his face. The Fogg who had returned was exactly the same as the Fogg who had left — the same calmness, the same stillness.

He sat for several minutes without speaking. Then, looking directly at Aouda, he said: "Madam, will you forgive me for bringing you to England?"

"I, Mr. Fogg!" replied Aouda, trying to control her heartbeat.

"Please let me finish," returned Mr. Fogg. "When I decided to take you far away from the country that was so dangerous for you, I was rich, and I planned to put a part of my fortune at your disposal — so that your life would be free and comfortable. But now I am ruined."

"I know it, Mr. Fogg," replied Aouda. "And I ask you in my turn — will you forgive me for having followed you, and — who knows? — for having perhaps slowed you down and in that way helped to bring about your ruin?"

"Madam, you could not remain in India, and your safety could only be guaranteed by bringing you far enough away that your enemies could not reach you."

"So, Mr. Fogg," Aouda continued, "not content with saving me from a terrible death, you also felt it was your duty to make sure I would be comfortable in a foreign land?"

"Yes, madam. But things did not go as planned. Even so, I offer you the little I have left."

"But what will become of you, Mr. Fogg?"

"As for me, madam," replied the gentleman, in a cool voice, "I need nothing."

"But how do you feel about the future that awaits you?"

"As I always do."

"At least," said Aouda, "a man like you should not face poverty alone. Your friends—"

"I have no friends, madam."

"Your family—"

"I have no family left."

"Then I feel sorry for you, Mr. Fogg — because being alone is a sad thing, with no one to share your sorrows. People say, however, that even great misfortune, shared between two caring souls, can be faced with patience."

"People say so, madam."

"Mr. Fogg," said Aouda, standing up and taking his hand, "do you wish for both a friend and a companion at the same time? Will you have me as your wife?"

Mr. Fogg stood up as well. There was an unusual light in his eyes, and a slight movement in his lips. Aouda looked into his face. The sincerity, honesty, strength, and gentleness in the soft look of this noble woman — who could dare everything to save the man to whom she owed everything — first surprised him, then touched him deeply. He closed his eyes for a moment, as if to avoid her gaze. When he opened them again, "I love you!" he said simply. "Yes, by everything that is most precious, I love you, and I am completely yours!"

"Ah!" cried Aouda, pressing his hand to her heart.

Passepartout was called and appeared immediately. Mr. Fogg was still holding Aouda's hand. Passepartout understood at once, and his large, round face became as bright as the tropical sun at its highest point.

Mr. Fogg asked him if it was too late to contact the Reverend Samuel Wilson of Marylebone parish that evening.

Passepartout gave his warmest smile and said: "Never too late."

It was five minutes past eight.

"Will it be for tomorrow, Monday?"

"For tomorrow, Monday," said Mr. Fogg, turning to Aouda.

"Yes, for tomorrow, Monday," she replied.

Passepartout hurried off as fast as his legs could carry him.
