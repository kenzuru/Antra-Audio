
  Yes — Phileas Fogg himself.

The reader will remember that at five minutes past eight in the evening — about twenty-five hours after the travellers had arrived in London — Passepartout had been sent by his master to arrange the services of the Reverend Samuel Wilson for a wedding ceremony that was to take place the next day.

Passepartout went off on his task, full of joy. He soon reached the clergyman's house, but found him not at home. Passepartout waited a good twenty minutes, and when he finally left the reverend gentleman, it was thirty-five minutes past eight. But in what a state he was! With his hair messy and without his hat, he ran along the street faster than anyone had ever been seen to run before, bumping into people walking by, rushing along the pavement like a storm.

Within three minutes he was back in Saville Row and burst into Mr. Fogg's room.

He could not speak.

"What is the matter?" asked Mr. Fogg.

"My master!" gasped Passepartout — "marriage — impossible—"

"Impossible?"

"Impossible — for tomorrow."

"Why?"

"Because tomorrow — is Sunday!"

"Monday," replied Mr. Fogg.

"No — today is Saturday."

"Saturday? Impossible!"

"Yes, yes, yes, yes!" cried Passepartout. "You made a mistake of one day! We arrived twenty-four hours early! But there are only ten minutes left!"

Passepartout had grabbed his master by the collar and was pulling him forward with unstoppable force.

Phileas Fogg, carried off like this, without time to think, left his house, jumped into a carriage, promised a hundred pounds to the driver, and — after running over two dogs and knocking over five carriages — arrived at the Reform Club.

The clock showed a quarter before nine when he appeared in the main saloon.

Phileas Fogg had completed the journey around the world in eighty days!

Phileas Fogg had won his bet of twenty thousand pounds!

How could a man so careful and precise have made a mistake of a whole day? How did he come to think that he had arrived in London on Saturday, the twenty-first of December, when it was really Friday, the twentieth — only the seventy-ninth day since his departure?

The reason for the mistake is very simple.

Phileas Fogg had, without realising it, gained one day during his journey — simply because he had always been travelling eastward. He would, on the other hand, have lost a day if he had gone the other way — that is, westward.

By travelling eastward, he had been moving towards the sun, and as a result, the days had become slightly shorter for him — four minutes shorter for every degree he crossed in that direction. There are three hundred and sixty degrees in a full circle of the earth. Multiply three hundred and sixty by four minutes and you get exactly twenty-four hours — that is, the one whole day he had unknowingly gained. In other words, while Phileas Fogg, travelling eastward, had seen the sun cross the midpoint of the sky eighty times, his friends in London had only seen it do so seventy-nine times. That is why they had expected him at the Reform Club on Saturday, not Sunday, as Mr. Fogg himself believed.

And Passepartout's well-known family watch, which had always kept London time, would have shown this difference — if it had tracked the days as well as the hours and minutes!

Phileas Fogg had therefore won the twenty thousand pounds. But since he had spent nearly nineteen thousand along the way, the money gained was not large. His goal, however, had always been victory — not money. He divided the remaining one thousand pounds between Passepartout and the unfortunate Fix, against whom he held no bad feelings. He did, however, deduct from Passepartout's share the cost of the gas that had burned in his room for nineteen hundred and twenty hours — purely for the sake of order.

That evening, Mr. Fogg — as calm and steady as always — said to Aouda: "Is our marriage still what you wish?"

"Mr. Fogg," she replied, "it is for me to ask that question. You were ruined — but now you are rich again."

"Forgive me, madam — my fortune belongs to you. If you had not suggested our marriage, my servant would not have gone to the Reverend Samuel Wilson's, I would not have found out about my mistake, and—"

"Dear Mr. Fogg!" said the young woman.

"Dear Aouda!" replied Phileas Fogg.

It goes without saying that the wedding took place forty-eight hours later, and that Passepartout — shining with happiness — gave the bride away. Had he not saved her? Did he not deserve that honour?

The next day, as soon as it was light, Passepartout knocked loudly at his master's door. Mr. Fogg opened it and asked: "What is the matter, Passepartout?"

"What is it, sir? I have just this moment worked out—"

"What?"

"That we could have made the journey around the world in only seventy-eight days."

"No doubt," returned Mr. Fogg, "by not crossing India. But if I had not crossed India, I would not have saved Aouda. She would not have become my wife, and—"

Mr. Fogg quietly closed the door.

Phileas Fogg had won his bet and had made the journey around the world in eighty days. To do this, he had used every possible way of travelling — steamers, trains, carriages, yachts, cargo ships, sledges, elephants. This unusual gentleman had shown throughout his remarkable qualities of calmness and precision. But what then? What had he truly gained from all this effort? What had he brought back from this long and tiring journey?

Nothing, you say? Perhaps. Nothing but a wonderful woman, who — strange as it may seem — made him the happiest man in the world!

Truly, would you not travel around the world for less than that?
