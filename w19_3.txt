The group that gathered on the bank was indeed an odd sight. The birds had wet, clinging feathers, and the animals’ fur was sticking to their bodies. Every single one of them was dripping wet, cross, and generally miserable.

The most urgent problem was, of course, how to get dry again quickly. They held a quick discussion about this. After a few minutes of talking, it seemed completely natural to Alice that she was speaking so familiarly with a mouse, a duck, and several birds, as if she had known them all her life. She even had a rather long argument with the Lory (a type of parrot), which eventually became sulky and would only repeat, “I am older than you, and therefore I must know better.” Alice refused to accept this without knowing exactly how old the Lory was. Since the Lory absolutely refused to tell its age, the argument had to end there.

Finally, the Mouse, who seemed to be the most respected figure in the group, called out, “Sit down, all of you, and listen closely to me! I will make you perfectly dry very soon!”

They all immediately sat down in a large circle, with the Mouse sitting importantly in the middle. Alice watched the Mouse anxiously, certain that she would catch a very bad cold if she didn't get dry in a hurry.

“Ahem!” said the Mouse with a look of great importance. “Are you all prepared? This is the driest thing I know of. Complete silence, if you please! ‘William the Conqueror, whose cause was favored by the Pope, was quickly submitted to by the English, who desperately wanted leaders, and had lately grown very accustomed to being taken over and conquered. Edwin and Morcar, the Earls of Mercia and Northumbria—’”

“Ugh!” said the Lory, interrupting with a noticeable shiver.

“I beg your pardon!” said the Mouse, frowning a little but maintaining its politeness. “Did you say something?”

“Not me!” said the Lory quickly.

“I was certain I heard something,” the Mouse replied. “—I will continue. ‘Edwin and Morcar, the Earls of Mercia and Northumbria, declared their support for him: and even Stigand, the dedicated patriotic archbishop of Canterbury, found it advisable—’”

“Found what?” interrupted the Duck.

“Found it,” the Mouse replied, sounding quite annoyed. “Surely you know what the word ‘it’ means, in that context.”

“I know what ‘it’ means perfectly well when I find a thing,” said the Duck, shaking its head. “It’s usually a frog or a worm. The actual question is, what did the archbishop find?”

The Mouse ignored the Duck’s question completely and hurried on with its history lesson: “‘—found it advisable to go with Edgar Atheling to meet William and offer him the crown. William’s behavior at first was mild. But the rude arrogance of his Normans—’ How are you doing now, my dear?” the Mouse asked, turning to Alice as it spoke.

“I am just as wet as I was before,” said Alice in a sad voice. “Your story doesn’t seem to be drying me at all.”

“In that case,” said the Dodo with great seriousness, standing up on its legs, “I propose that the meeting immediately stop, so we can adopt more energetic and active methods—”

“Speak English!” said the Eaglet loudly. “I don’t understand the meaning of half those long words, and what’s more, I don’t believe you do either!” The Eaglet bent its head down to hide a smile, and some of the other birds giggled quietly.

“What I was attempting to say,” said the Dodo, sounding deeply offended, “was that the most effective thing to get us dry would be a Caucus-race.”

“What is a Caucus-race?” asked Alice. She didn't particularly care to know, but the Dodo had paused, clearly waiting for someone to speak, and no one else looked like they were going to say anything.

“Why,” said the Dodo, “the easiest way to explain it is to simply do it.” (And just in case you might want to try this yourself on a cold winter day, here is how the Dodo organized it.)

First, the Dodo marked out a racing area in a kind of rough circle. (“The exact shape does not matter,” it declared.) Then, all the members of the party were placed randomly along the course. There was no official start like “One, two, three, and go!” Instead, they began running whenever they felt like it and stopped whenever they felt like it, which made it impossible to know when the race was officially finished. However, when they had all been running for about half an hour and were completely dry again, the Dodo suddenly shouted, “The race is over!” They all rushed around it, breathing heavily, and asked, “But who is the winner?”

The Dodo could not answer this question without a very long period of deep thought. It sat for a long time with one finger pressed against its forehead (the classic thoughtful pose you see in pictures of Shakespeare), while everyone else waited silently. At last, the Dodo announced, “Everyone has won, and all of you must have prizes.”

“But who is going to provide the prizes?” a large group of voices asked all at once.

“Why, she is, of course,” said the Dodo, pointing a finger directly at Alice. The whole party instantly crowded around her, shouting in a loud, confused chorus, “Prizes! Prizes!”

Alice had no idea what she was supposed to do, and in complete desperation, she reached her hand into her pocket. She pulled out a small box of tiny candy drops, or comfits (luckily, the salt water had not managed to get into the box), and handed them out as prizes. There was exactly one piece of candy for every single creature in the group.

“But she must have a prize for herself, you know,” said the Mouse.

“Of course,” the Dodo replied very seriously. “What else do you have in your pocket?” he went on, turning back to Alice.

“Only a thimble,” said Alice sadly.

“Hand it over here,” said the Dodo.

They all gathered around her once more, watching as the Dodo formally presented the small thimble to her, saying, “We humbly request your acceptance of this elegant thimble.” When he finished this short, formal speech, all the animals and birds cheered loudly for her.

Alice thought the entire ceremony was utterly absurd and silly, but the creatures all looked so very serious that she didn't dare to laugh. Since she couldn't think of anything appropriate to say, she simply bowed her head and took the thimble, trying to look as solemn and serious as she possibly could.

The next event was eating the candies. This caused some loud noise and confusion because the larger birds complained that they couldn't even taste their tiny comfit, and the smaller ones began to choke and had to be patted hard on the back. Finally, the eating was over, and they all sat down in a circle again and begged the Mouse to tell them more.

“You promised you would tell me your life story, you remember,” said Alice, “and why it is that you hate—C and D,” she added in a very low whisper, half afraid that mentioning the names would offend the Mouse again.

“Mine is a very long and very sad tale!” said the Mouse, turning toward Alice and sighing deeply.

“It is a long tail, certainly,” said Alice, looking down in wonder at the Mouse’s incredibly long tail trailing behind him. “But why do you call it sad?” She kept puzzling over the words and the way they were used while the Mouse began to speak, so her understanding of the Mouse’s story was all mixed up with the shape of its tail, and the poem she heard went something like this strange layout:

(The long, thin shape of the lines below represents the long, coiling shape of the Mouse’s tale/tail.) “Fury said to a mouse, That he met in the house, ‘Let us both go to law: I will prosecute you.—Come, I’ll take no denial; We must have a trial: For really this morning I’ve nothing to do.’ Said the mouse to the cur, ‘Such a trial, dear sir, With no jury or judge, would be wasting our breath.’ ‘I’ll be judge, I’ll be jury,’ Said cunning old Fury: ‘I’ll try the whole cause, and condemn you to death.’”

“You are not paying attention!” the Mouse said to Alice severely. “What exactly are you thinking about?”

“I beg your pardon, sir,” said Alice very humbly. “You had just reached the fifth turn in the story, I think?”

“I had not!” cried the Mouse, sharply and with great anger.

“A knot!” said Alice, always wanting to be helpful, and looking around anxiously at the Mouse’s tail. “Oh, please, let me help you untangle it!”

“I shall do nothing of the kind,” said the Mouse, standing up and preparing to walk away. “You are insulting me by talking such utter nonsense!”

“I honestly didn’t mean to!” pleaded poor Alice. “But you are so very easily offended, you know!”

The Mouse only responded with a low growl.

“Please come back and finish your story!” Alice called after it. The others all joined in, calling out in a chorus, “Yes, please do!” But the Mouse only shook its head impatiently and walked away a little faster.

“What a pity it wouldn’t stay!” sighed the Lory, as soon as the Mouse was completely out of sight. An old Crab took the chance to give advice to her daughter: “Ah, my dear! Let this be a lesson to you that you must never, ever lose your temper!”

“Hold your tongue, Mother!” said the young Crab, speaking a little rudely. “You are enough to test the patience of even a quiet oyster!”

“I wish I had our Dinah here right now, I truly do!” said Alice out loud, speaking to no one in particular. “She would quickly bring that Mouse back!”

“And who is this Dinah, if I may be so bold as to ask?” said the Lory.

Alice answered excitedly, because she was always thrilled to talk about her pet: “Dinah is our cat. And she is such a wonderful hunter for catching mice, you wouldn’t believe it! And oh, I wish you could see her when she goes after birds! Why, she will eat a little bird as soon as she looks at it!”

This statement caused a remarkable reaction among the entire party. Several of the birds immediately flew off. One old Magpie started wrapping itself up carefully, remarking, “I really must be getting home; the cool night-air is bad for my throat!” And a Canary called out in a shaking voice to its children, “Come away, my dears! It is high time you were all tucked up in bed!” Using these and other weak excuses, they all moved away quickly, and Alice was very soon left all alone again.

“I really wish I hadn’t even mentioned Dinah!” she said to herself in a sad voice. “Nobody seems to like her down here, and yet I know she’s the best cat in the entire world! Oh, my dear Dinah! I wonder if I will ever see you again?” At this point, poor Alice began to cry once more, because she felt very lonely and deeply sad. In a little while, however, she again heard the quiet pattering of small footsteps in the distance, and she looked up eagerly, half hoping that the Mouse had changed its mind and was returning to finish its story.
