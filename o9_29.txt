
  The train continued its journey that evening without interruption, passing Fort Saunders, crossing Cheyne Pass, and reaching Evans Pass. The track here reached its highest point of the journey — eight thousand and ninety-two feet above sea level. The travellers now only had to descend towards the Atlantic across endless flat lands, made level by nature. A branch of the "main line" turned south towards Denver, the capital of Colorado. The land around it is rich in gold and silver, and more than fifty thousand people already lived there.

One thousand three hundred and eighty-two miles had been covered from San Francisco in three days and three nights. Four more days and nights would probably bring them to New York. Phileas Fogg was still not behind schedule.

During the night, Camp Walbach was passed on the left. Lodge Pole Creek ran next to the road, marking the boundary between the territories of Wyoming and Colorado. They entered Nebraska at eleven o'clock, passed near Sedgwick, and stopped at Julesburg, on the southern branch of the Platte River.

It was here that the Union Pacific Railroad was officially opened on the 23rd of October, 1867, by the chief engineer, General Dodge. Two powerful locomotives, carrying nine carriages of invited guests — including Thomas C. Durant, vice-president of the railway — stopped at this point. Cheers were given, the Sioux and Pawnees performed an imitation Native American battle, fireworks were set off, and the first copy of the Railway Pioneer was printed by a press brought on the train. This was how they celebrated the opening of this great railway — a powerful tool of progress and civilisation, built across the desert, intended to link together cities and towns that did not yet exist. The whistle of the locomotive — more powerful than a legendary lyre — was about to call them up from American soil.

Fort McPherson was left behind at eight in the morning, and three hundred and fifty-seven miles remained before reaching Omaha. The road followed the winding southern branch of the Platte River along its left bank. At nine the train stopped at the important town of North Platte, built between the two arms of the river, which join together around it and form a single wide stream — a large branch whose waters flow into the Missouri a little north of Omaha.

The one hundred and first line of longitude was passed.

Mr. Fogg and his card partners had gone back to their game. No one — not even the dummy player — was complaining about the length of the trip. Fix had started by winning several coins, which he now seemed likely to lose. But he showed himself to be no less eager a whist player than Mr. Fogg. During the morning, luck clearly favoured that gentleman. The best cards seemed to keep coming to his hands.

Once, having decided on a brave move, he was about to play a spade when a voice behind him said, "I would play a diamond."

Mr. Fogg, Aouda, and Fix looked up and saw Colonel Proctor.

Stamp Proctor and Phileas Fogg recognised each other immediately.

"Ah! It's you, is it, Englishman?" cried the colonel. "It's you who are going to play a spade!"

"And who plays it," replied Phileas Fogg calmly, putting down the ten of spades.

"Well, I prefer diamonds," replied Colonel Proctor in a rude tone.

He made a move as if to take the card that had just been played, adding, "You don't know anything about whist."

"Perhaps I do, as well as anyone," said Phileas Fogg, standing up.

"You only have to try, son of England," replied the colonel.

Aouda turned pale and her blood ran cold. She took Mr. Fogg's arm and gently pulled him back. Passepartout was ready to jump on the American, who was staring at his opponent with a disrespectful look. But Fix got up, and going to Colonel Proctor, said, "You are forgetting that it is I with whom you have to deal, sir. It was I whom you not only insulted, but hit!"

"Mr. Fix," said Mr. Fogg, "please excuse me, but this matter is mine and mine alone. By insisting that I should not play a spade, the colonel has insulted me again, and he must answer for it."

"When and where you want," replied the American, "and with whatever weapon you choose."

Aouda tried without success to hold Mr. Fogg back. The detective also tried without success to take over the argument. Passepartout wanted to throw the colonel out of the window, but a sign from his master stopped him. Phileas Fogg left the carriage, and the American followed him onto the platform.

"Sir," said Mr. Fogg to his opponent, "I am in a great hurry to get back to Europe, and any delay whatsoever would be very harmful to my plans."

"Well, what does that matter to me?" replied Colonel Proctor.

"Sir," said Mr. Fogg, very politely, "after our meeting in San Francisco, I intended to return to America and find you as soon as I had finished the business that called me to England."

"Really!"

"Will you agree to meet in six months?"

"Why not in ten years?"

"I said six months," replied Phileas Fogg, "and I will be at the meeting place on time."

"All this is just avoiding the issue," cried Stamp Proctor. "Now or never!"

"Very well. Are you going to New York?"

"No."

"To Chicago?"

"No."

"To Omaha?"

"What does it matter to you? Do you know Plum Creek?"

"No," replied Mr. Fogg.

"It's the next station. The train will be there in an hour and will stop for ten minutes. In ten minutes several revolver shots could be exchanged."

"Very well," said Mr. Fogg. "I will stop at Plum Creek."

"And I expect you'll stay there too," added the American in a rude tone.

"Who knows?" replied Mr. Fogg, returning to the carriage as calmly as usual. He began to comfort Aouda, telling her that people who make big threats are never to be feared. He then asked Fix to be his second in the coming fight — a request the detective could not refuse. Mr. Fogg went back to his card game with complete calm.

At eleven o'clock the engine's whistle announced that they were approaching Plum Creek station. Mr. Fogg stood up and, followed by Fix, went out onto the platform. Passepartout came with him, carrying two revolvers. Aouda stayed in the carriage, as white as a sheet.

The door of the next carriage opened, and Colonel Proctor appeared on the platform, attended by an American of the same type as his second. But just as the two men were about to step off the train, the conductor came running up and shouted, "You cannot get off, gentlemen!"

"Why not?" asked the colonel.

"We are twenty minutes late, and we will not be stopping."

"But I am going to fight a duel with this gentleman."

"I am sorry," said the conductor, "but we will be moving at once. There's the bell ringing now."

The train started.

"I'm truly sorry, gentlemen," said the conductor. "Under any other circumstances I would have been happy to help you. But, after all, since you have not had time to fight here, why not fight as we go along?"

"That might not be convenient for this gentleman," said the colonel, in a mocking tone.

"It would be perfectly convenient," replied Phileas Fogg.

"Well, we really are in America," thought Passepartout. "And the conductor is a gentleman of the first order!"

Saying this to himself, he followed his master.

The two men, their seconds, and the conductor passed through the carriages to the back of the train. The last carriage had only about a dozen passengers, whom the conductor politely asked if they would not mind leaving for a few moments, as two gentlemen had a matter of honour to settle. The passengers agreed with great willingness and immediately went out onto the platform.

The carriage, which was about fifty feet long, was very suitable for their purpose. The two opponents could walk towards each other down the aisle and fire at their ease. Never had a duel been more easily set up. Mr. Fogg and Colonel Proctor, each with two six-barrelled revolvers, entered the carriage. The seconds, staying outside, closed them in. At the first whistle of the locomotive they were to start firing. After a gap of two minutes, whatever was left of the two gentlemen would be removed from the carriage.

Nothing could be simpler. In fact it was all so simple that Fix and Passepartout felt their hearts beating as if they would burst. They were waiting for the agreed whistle, when suddenly wild shouts were heard in the air, together with gunshots that clearly did not come from the carriage where the duelists were. The shots continued from the front and along the whole length of the train. Cries of terror came from inside the carriages.

Colonel Proctor and Mr. Fogg, revolvers in hand, quickly left their space and rushed to where the noise was loudest. They then saw that the train was being attacked by a group of Sioux warriors.

This was not the first time these brave Native Americans had tried this kind of attack. More than once they had stopped trains on the road. A hundred of them had, in their usual way, jumped onto the steps without stopping the train, as easily as a circus performer jumping onto a moving horse.

The Sioux were armed with guns, and their shots were answered by revolver fire from the passengers, who were almost all armed.

The warriors had first climbed onto the engine and half knocked out the engineer and the stoker with blows from their guns. A Sioux chief, wanting to stop the train but not knowing how to use the controls, had opened the steam valve fully instead of closing it, and the engine was rushing forward at a terrifying speed.

The Sioux had at the same time entered the carriages, moving quickly over the rooftops, pushing open the doors, and fighting hand to hand with the passengers. They broke into the luggage carriage and robbed it, throwing trunks out of the train. The shouts and shots were constant. The travellers defended themselves bravely. Some of the carriages were blocked off and held against attack, like moving forts being carried along at a hundred miles per hour.

Aouda was brave from the very start. She defended herself like a true fighter with a revolver, firing through the broken windows whenever a warrior appeared. Twenty Sioux had been shot and fell dying to the ground, and the wheels crushed those who fell on the rails as if they were insects. Several passengers, shot or knocked out, lay on the seats.

The fight had to be stopped. It had been going on for ten minutes and would end in victory for the Sioux if the train was not stopped. Fort Kearney station, where there was a military unit, was only two miles away. But once that was passed, the Sioux would be in control of the train between Fort Kearney and the next station.

The conductor was fighting beside Mr. Fogg when he was shot and fell. At the same moment he cried, "Unless the train is stopped in five minutes, we are lost!"

"It will be stopped," said Phileas Fogg, getting ready to rush from the carriage.

"Stay, sir," cried Passepartout. "I will go."

Mr. Fogg had no time to stop the brave man, who, opening a door unseen by the warriors, managed to slip under the carriage. While the fighting continued and bullets flew over his head, he used his old experience as an acrobat and with amazing skill made his way under the carriages, holding onto the chains, using the brakes and edges of the window frames, moving from one carriage to another with wonderful ability, and in this way reached the front of the train.

There, hanging by one hand between the luggage carriage and the coal tender, with the other hand he undid the safety chains. But because of the pulling force, he would never have managed to unscrew the connecting bar if a violent crash had not shaken this bar loose. The train, now separated from the engine, stayed slightly behind, while the engine rushed forward with even greater speed.

Carried forward by the force it had already built up, the train still moved for several minutes. But the brakes were applied, and at last it stopped — less than one hundred feet from Kearney station.

The soldiers from the fort, attracted by the shots, hurried up. The Sioux had not expected them and left as a group before the train had fully stopped.

But when the passengers counted each other on the station platform, several were found to be missing — among them the brave Frenchman whose selflessness had just saved them.
