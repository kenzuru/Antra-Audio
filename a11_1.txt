People have used hot water and steam for cleaning and rest for thousands of years. Ancient cultures in Europe and Asia used heat to relax the body and clear the skin. Today, scientists study saunas to learn how heat changes the body and the heart.

A large study in Finland followed over 2,000 men for many years. The results showed an important pattern. Men who used the sauna four to seven times each week had better health than those who went less. Their blood pressure stayed lower. Their cholesterol levels improved. Their risk of heart disease and early death decreased. Inside the sauna, the temperature rises to about 80 to 90 degrees Celsius. This heat makes the heart beat faster. Blood vessels open wide. The body releases sweat. These changes look similar to light physical exercise. Over time, this regular stress may train the heart to work in a stronger and safer way.

Saunas also help the body recover after physical effort. After running or lifting weights, muscles get small injuries. Warm heat improves blood flow to these areas. More oxygen and nutrients reach the muscles. Swelling goes down. Pain becomes weaker. The heat also increases special proteins in the body. These proteins help repair damaged cells and protect them from future stress.

Mental health may also improve with sauna use. Many people feel calm and relaxed after a hot session. Stress hormones drop. Sleep often becomes deeper. Some studies link regular sauna use with lower levels of anxiety and better mood.

Even with these benefits, saunas do not suit everyone. People with heart problems, low blood pressure, or certain skin conditions must be careful. Staying too long in high heat leads to dehydration, dizziness, or fainting. Drinking water before and after is important. Scientists still continue research to learn the full limits and long term effects of sauna use.
