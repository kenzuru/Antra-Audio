
  The train, leaving Great Salt Lake at Ogden, headed north for an hour as far as Weber River, having completed nearly nine hundred miles from San Francisco. From that point it turned east towards the rough Wahsatch Mountains. It was in the section between this range and the Rocky Mountains that the American engineers faced the greatest difficulties in building the road. Because of this, the government gave a payment of forty-eight thousand dollars per mile, instead of the sixteen thousand allowed for the work done on the flat lands. But the engineers, instead of fighting against nature, avoided its difficulties by going around the rocks rather than through them. Only one tunnel, fourteen thousand feet long, was cut in order to reach the great basin.

The track up to this point had reached its highest point at the Great Salt Lake. From there it followed a long curve, going down towards Bitter Creek Valley, and then rising again to the high point where the waters divide between those flowing to the Atlantic and those flowing to the Pacific. There were many streams in this mountainous area, and it was necessary to cross Muddy Creek, Green Creek, and others on small bridges.

Passepartout became more and more impatient as they went on, while Fix wanted more than anything to get out of this difficult area. He was more anxious than Phileas Fogg himself to be past the danger of delays and problems, and to set foot on English soil.

At ten o'clock at night the train stopped at Fort Bridger station, and twenty minutes later entered Wyoming Territory, following the valley of Bitter Creek along its length. The next day, the 7th of December, they stopped for fifteen minutes at Green River station. Snow had fallen heavily during the night. Mixed with rain, it had half melted and did not block their progress. The bad weather, however, worried Passepartout, since the build-up of snow, if it blocked the wheels of the carriages, would certainly ruin Mr. Fogg's journey.

"What an idea!" he said to himself. "Why did my master make this journey in winter? Couldn't he have waited for the good season to improve his chances?"

While the good Frenchman was worried about the sky and the cold temperature, Aouda was frightened for a completely different reason.

Several passengers had got off at Green River and were walking up and down the platforms. Among them, Aouda recognised Colonel Stamp Proctor — the same man who had so rudely insulted Phileas Fogg at the San Francisco meeting. Not wanting to be seen, the young woman moved away from the window, feeling very alarmed at her discovery. She cared deeply for the man who, however calmly, showed her every day his complete devotion. She did not fully understand, perhaps, the depth of feeling her protector inspired in her — what she called gratitude, but which, though she was not aware of it, was really something more. Her heart sank when she recognised the man whom Mr. Fogg intended, sooner or later, to call to account for his behaviour. It was clearly just by chance that Colonel Proctor was on this train. But he was there, and it was necessary, whatever happened, to prevent Phileas Fogg from seeing his enemy.

Aouda found a moment when Mr. Fogg was asleep to tell Fix and Passepartout who she had seen.

"That Proctor is on this train!" cried Fix. "Well, do not worry, madam. Before he settles things with Mr. Fogg, he has to deal with me! It seems to me that I was the more insulted of the two."

"And besides," added Passepartout, "I'll take care of him, colonel or not."

"Mr. Fix," continued Aouda, "Mr. Fogg will not allow anyone to get revenge for him. He said he would come back to America to find this man. If he sees Colonel Proctor, we could not prevent a fight that might have terrible results. He must not see him."

"You are right, madam," replied Fix. "A meeting between them could ruin everything. Whether he won or lost, Mr. Fogg would be delayed, and—"

"And," added Passepartout, "that would be good news for the gentlemen of the Reform Club. In four days we'll be in New York. Well, if my master does not leave this carriage during those four days, we can hope that chance will not bring him face to face with this dangerous American. We must, if possible, stop him from going out."

The conversation ended. Mr. Fogg had just woken up and was looking out of the window. Soon after, Passepartout, without being heard by his master or Aouda, whispered to the detective, "Would you really fight for him?"

"I would do anything," replied Fix, in a tone that showed strong determination, "to get him back alive to Europe!"

Passepartout felt something like a shiver go through his body, but his faith in his master remained unshaken.

Was there any way to keep Mr. Fogg in the carriage, to prevent a meeting between him and the colonel? It should not be a difficult task, since that gentleman naturally preferred to sit and had little interest in moving around. The detective, at least, seemed to have found a way. After a few moments, he said to Mr. Fogg, "These are long and slow hours, sir, that we are passing on the railway."

"Yes," replied Mr. Fogg, "but they pass."

"You used to play whist," continued Fix, "on the steamers."

"Yes, but it would be difficult to do so here. I have neither cards nor partners."

"Oh, but we can easily buy some cards, as they are sold on all American trains. And as for partners, if madam plays—"

"Certainly, sir," Aouda quickly replied. "I understand whist. It is part of an English education."

"I myself believe I play a fairly good game. Well, here are three of us, and a dummy—"

"As you wish, sir," replied Phileas Fogg, very happy to go back to his favourite card game, even on a train.

Passepartout was sent to find the train attendant, and soon came back with two packs of cards, some pins, counters, and a cloth-covered board.

The game began. Aouda understood whist well enough, and even received some compliments on her playing from Mr. Fogg. As for the detective, he was an expert, and was a worthy opponent for his current player.

"Now," thought Passepartout, "we've got him. He won't move."

At eleven in the morning the train had reached the high point of the route at Bridger Pass, seven thousand five hundred and twenty-four feet above sea level — one of the highest points reached by the track in crossing the Rocky Mountains. After about two hundred miles, the travellers finally found themselves on one of those vast flat plains that stretch towards the Atlantic, and which nature has made so ideal for building a railway.

On the slope towards the Atlantic, the first streams — branches of the North Platte River — already appeared. The whole northern and eastern horizon was bounded by the huge half-circle formed by the southern part of the Rocky Mountains, the highest of which was Laramie Peak. Between this and the railway extended wide flat lands, well watered. On the right rose the lower parts of the mountain range that goes south towards the beginnings of the Arkansas River, one of the great branches of the Missouri.

At half past twelve the travellers saw Fort Halleck for a moment, which watches over that area. A few more hours and the Rocky Mountains were behind them. There was reason to hope that no accident would mark the journey through this difficult region. The snow had stopped falling, and the air had become crisp and cold. Large birds, frightened by the train, rose from the ground and flew away into the distance. No wild animals appeared on the flat land. It was a desert in its great emptiness.

After a comfortable breakfast served in the carriage, Mr. Fogg and his card partners had just gone back to their game when a loud whistle was heard and the train stopped. Passepartout put his head out of the door but saw nothing to explain the delay. No station was in sight.

Aouda and Fix feared that Mr. Fogg might decide to get out. But that gentleman was satisfied with saying to his servant, "Find out what is happening."

Passepartout hurried out of the carriage. Thirty or forty passengers had already got off, among them Colonel Stamp Proctor.

The train had stopped in front of a red signal blocking the way. The engineer and conductor were talking excitedly with a signalman whom the station master at Medicine Bow, the next stop, had sent ahead. The passengers gathered around and joined the discussion, in which Colonel Proctor, with his rude manner, was very noticeable.

Passepartout, joining the group, heard the signalman say, "No! You cannot pass. The bridge at Medicine Bow is weak and would not hold the weight of the train."

This was a suspension bridge built over some fast-moving water, about a mile from where they were. According to the signalman, it was in very bad condition. Several of its iron cables had broken, and it was impossible to risk crossing it. He was not exaggerating the state of the bridge. It may be taken as certain that, as brave as Americans usually are, when they are careful, there is good reason for it.

Passepartout, not daring to tell his master what he had heard, listened with clenched teeth, still as a statue.

"Right!" cried Colonel Proctor. "But we are not going to stay here and grow roots in the snow, I imagine!"

"Colonel," replied the conductor, "we have sent a message to Omaha for a train, but it is unlikely to reach Medicine Bow in less than six hours."

"Six hours!" cried Passepartout.

"Certainly," returned the conductor. "Besides, it will take us that long to walk to Medicine Bow."

"But it is only a mile from here," said one of the passengers.

"Yes, but it's on the other side of the river."

"And can't we cross in a boat?" asked the colonel.

"That's impossible. The stream is flooded from the rain. It is a rapid, and we would need to travel ten miles to the north to find a point where we can cross."

The colonel let out a stream of angry words, blaming the railway company and the conductor. Passepartout, who was furious, was not against joining him. Here was a problem that all his master's money could not solve.

There was general disappointment among the passengers, who, not even counting the delay, found themselves forced to walk fifteen miles through snow-covered flat land. They complained loudly and would certainly have attracted Phileas Fogg's attention if he had not been completely absorbed in his card game.

Passepartout found that he could not avoid telling his master what had happened. With his head down, he was turning towards the carriage when the engineer — a true American, named Forster — called out, "Gentlemen, perhaps there is a way, after all, to get over."

"On the bridge?" asked a passenger.

"On the bridge."

"With our train?"

"With our train."

Passepartout stopped and eagerly listened to the engineer.

"But the bridge is not safe," said the conductor.

"No matter," replied Forster. "I believe that if we go at the very highest speed we might have a chance of getting over."

"Good heavens!" muttered Passepartout.

But many of the passengers were immediately attracted by the engineer's suggestion, and Colonel Proctor was especially pleased. He found the plan very workable and told stories of engineers who had driven their trains across rivers without bridges by going at full speed. Many of those present agreed with the engineer.

"We have fifty chances out of a hundred of making it," said one.

"Eighty! Ninety!"

Passepartout was amazed, and though he was ready to try anything to get across Medicine Creek, he thought the suggested plan a little too American in its recklessness. "Besides," he thought, "there is a simpler way, and it does not even occur to any of these people! Sir," he said out loud to one of the passengers, "the engineer's plan seems to me a little dangerous, but—"

"Eighty chances!" replied the passenger, turning his back on him.

"I know it," said Passepartout, turning to another passenger, "but a simple idea—"

"Ideas are no use," replied the American, shrugging his shoulders, "since the engineer says we can make it."

"No doubt," said Passepartout, "we can make it, but perhaps it would be more careful—"

"What! Careful!" cried Colonel Proctor, for whom that word seemed to be very annoying. "At full speed, don't you see, at full speed!"

"I know — I see," repeated Passepartout. "But it would be, if not more careful — since that word bothers you — at least more natural—"

"Who! What! What's wrong with this fellow?" cried several people.

The poor man did not know who to talk to.

"Are you afraid?" asked Colonel Proctor.

"Me, afraid? Very well. I will show these people that a Frenchman can be as bold as any American!"

"All aboard!" cried the conductor.

"Yes, all aboard!" repeated Passepartout, and immediately added quietly: "But they can't stop me thinking that it would be more sensible to cross the bridge on foot and let the train come after!"

But no one heard this wise suggestion, nor would anyone have accepted it. The passengers returned to their seats. Passepartout sat down without telling what had happened. The card players were completely absorbed in their game.

The engine gave a strong whistle. The engineer, reversing the steam, backed the train nearly a mile — moving back, like a long-jumper, to get a longer run. Then, with another whistle, he started forward. The speed of the train increased, and soon it was moving at a terrifying pace. A long screech came from the engine. The pistons were working at twenty strokes per second. They all felt that the entire train, rushing forward at one hundred miles per hour, was barely touching the rails at all.

And they crossed! It was like a flash. No one saw the bridge. The train seemed to leap from one bank to the other, and the engineer could not stop it until it had gone five miles past the station. But almost as soon as the train had passed the river, the bridge — completely destroyed — fell with a crash into the fast waters of Medicine Bow.
