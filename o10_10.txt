

   As spring came on, a new set of activities became popular, and the longer days gave long afternoons for work and play of all kinds. The garden had to be put in order, and each sister had a quarter of the little plot to do what she liked with. Hannah used to say, "I'd know which each of them gardens belonged to, if I saw them in China," and so she might, because the girls' tastes differed as much as their characters. Meg's had roses and heliotrope, myrtle, and a little orange tree in it. Jo's bed was never the same two seasons in a row, because she was always trying experiments. This year it was to be a plantation of sunflowers, the seeds of which were to feed Aunt Cockle-top and her family of chicks. Beth had old-fashioned fragrant flowers in her garden, sweet peas and mignonette, larkspur, pinks, pansies, and southernwood, with chickweed for the birds and catnip for the cats. Amy had a small corner garden, rather small and with some insects, but very pretty to look at, with honeysuckle and morning glories hanging their colored flowers in graceful climbing patterns all over it, tall white lilies, delicate ferns, and as many bright, beautiful plants as would agree to bloom there.

Gardening, walks, boat trips on the river, and flower hunting occupied the fine days, and for rainy ones, they had indoor activities, some old, some new, all more or less original. One of these was the 'P.C.', because as secret clubs were fashionable, it was thought right to have one, and as all of the girls admired Dickens, they called themselves the Pickwick Club. With a few interruptions, they had kept this up for a year, and met every Saturday evening in the big attic, on which occasions the ceremonies were as follows: Three chairs were arranged in a row before a table on which was a lamp, also four white badges, with a big 'P.C.' in different colors on each, and the weekly newspaper called, The Pickwick Portfolio, to which all contributed something, while Jo, who loved pens and ink, was the editor. At seven o'clock, the four members went up to the clubroom, tied their badges round their heads, and took their seats with great seriousness. Meg, as the eldest, was Samuel Pickwick, Jo, being of a literary nature, Augustus Snodgrass, Beth, because she was round and rosy, Tracy Tupman, and Amy, who was always trying to do what she couldn't, was Nathaniel Winkle. Pickwick, the president, read the paper, which was filled with original stories, poetry, local news, funny advertisements, and hints, in which they good-naturedly reminded each other of their faults and shortcomings. On one occasion, Mr. Pickwick put on a pair of glasses without any lenses, rapped on the table, cleared his throat, and having stared hard at Mr. Snodgrass, who was leaning back in his chair, until he sat properly, began to read:


"THE PICKWICK PORTFOLIO"

MAY 20, 18—

POET'S CORNER

ANNIVERSARY ODE

Again we meet to celebrate
    With badge and solemn rite,
Our fifty-second anniversary,
    In Pickwick Hall, tonight.

We all are here in perfect health,
    None gone from our small band:
Again we see each well-known face,
    And press each friendly hand.

Our Pickwick, always at his post,
    With reverence we greet,
As, spectacles on nose, he reads
    Our well-filled weekly sheet.

Although he suffers from a cold,
    We joy to hear him speak,
For words of wisdom from him fall,
    In spite of croak or squeak.

Old six-foot Snodgrass looms on high,
    With elephantine grace,
And beams upon the company,
    With brown and jovial face.

Poetic fire lights up his eye,
    He struggles 'gainst his lot.
Behold ambition on his brow,
    And on his nose, a blot.

Next our peaceful Tupman comes,
    So rosy, plump, and sweet,
Who chokes with laughter at the puns,
    And tumbles off his seat.

Prim little Winkle too is here,
    With every hair in place,
A model of propriety,
    Though he hates to wash his face.

The year is gone, we still unite
    To joke and laugh and read,
And tread the path of literature
    That doth to glory lead.

Long may our paper prosper well,
    Our club unbroken be,
And coming years their blessings pour
    On the useful, gay 'P. C.'.

A. SNODGRASS

THE MASKED MARRIAGE
(A Story of Venice)

Gondola after gondola arrived at the marble steps, and left its elegant passengers to join the brilliant crowd that filled the grand halls of Count Adelon. Knights and ladies, elves and pages, monks and flower girls, all mingled cheerfully in the dance. Sweet voices and rich music filled the air, and so with merriment and music the masquerade went on. "Has your Highness seen the Lady Viola tonight?" asked a gallant troubadour of the fairy queen who floated down the hall on his arm.

"Yes, is she not lovely, though so sad! Her costume is well chosen, too, because in a week she is to marry Count Antonio, whom she deeply hates."

"I envy him. There he comes, dressed like a bridegroom, except for the black mask. When that is removed we shall see how he regards the fair maid whose heart he cannot win, though her harsh father gives her hand," returned the troubadour.

"It is whispered that she loves the young English artist who follows her steps, and is rejected by the old Count," said the lady, as they joined the dance. The celebration was at its height when a priest appeared, and taking the young pair to a private area hung with purple curtains, he signaled them to kneel. Immediate silence fell on the cheerful crowd, and not a sound, but the splash of fountains or the rustle of orange trees sleeping in the moonlight, broke the quiet, as Count de Adelon spoke:

"My lords and ladies, pardon the trick by which I have gathered you here to witness the marriage of my daughter. Father, we await your services." All eyes turned toward the wedding pair, and a murmur of amazement went through the crowd, because neither bride nor groom removed their masks. Curiosity and wonder filled all hearts, but respect kept all tongues quiet until the ceremony was over. Then the eager guests gathered around the count, asking for an explanation.

"Gladly would I give it if I could, but I only know that it was the wish of my shy Viola, and I agreed to it. Now, my children, let the pretense end. Unmask and receive my blessing."

But neither knelt, because the young bridegroom replied in a tone that surprised all listeners as the mask fell, revealing the noble face of Ferdinand Devereux, the artist lover, and leaning on his chest where now shone the star of an English earl was the lovely Viola, radiant with joy and beauty.

"My lord, you scornfully told me to claim your daughter when I could claim as high a name and as great a fortune as Count Antonio. I can do more, because even your ambitious soul cannot refuse the Earl of Devereux and De Vere, when he gives his ancient name and boundless wealth in exchange for the beloved hand of this fair lady, now my wife."

The count stood like someone turned to stone, and turning to the confused crowd, Ferdinand added, with a cheerful smile of triumph, "To you, my brave friends, I can only wish that your courting may go as well as mine has done, and that you may all win as fine a bride as I have by this masked marriage."

THE HISTORY OF A SQUASH

Once upon a time a farmer planted a little seed in his garden, and after a while it sprouted and became a vine and produced many squashes. One day in October, when they were ripe, he picked one and took it to market. A shopkeeper bought it and put it in his shop. That same morning, a little girl in a brown hat and blue dress, with a round face and small nose, went and bought it for her mother. She carried it home, cut it up, and boiled it in the big pot, mashed some of it with salt and butter, for dinner. And to the rest she added a cup of milk, two eggs, four spoons of sugar, nutmeg, and some crackers, put it in a deep dish, and baked it until it was brown and nice, and the next day it was eaten by a family named March.

A SAD ACCIDENT

On Friday last, we were startled by a violent crash in our basement, followed by cries of distress. On rushing together to the cellar, we found our beloved President flat on the floor, having tripped and fallen while getting wood for domestic purposes. A scene of complete chaos met our eyes, because in his fall Mr. Pickwick had plunged his head and shoulders into a tub of water, knocked over a container of soft soap onto his fine clothes, and torn his garments badly. On being removed from this dangerous situation, it was found that he had suffered no injury but several bruises, and we are happy to say, is now doing well.

ED.

THE PUBLIC SADNESS

It is our painful duty to record the sudden and mysterious disappearance of our cherished friend, Mrs. Snowball Pat Paw. This lovely and beloved cat was the pet of a large circle of warm and admiring friends; because her beauty attracted all eyes, her graces and virtues endeared her to all hearts, and her loss is deeply felt by the whole community.
    When last seen, she was sitting at the gate, watching the butcher's cart, and it is feared that some villain, attracted by her charms, basely took her. Weeks have passed, but no trace of her has been discovered, and we give up all hope, tie a black ribbon to her basket, put aside her dish, and grieve for her as one lost to us forever.

A sympathizing friend sends the following poem:

A LAMENT
FOR S. B. PAT PAW

We mourn the loss of our little pet,
    And sigh over her sad fate,
For never more by the fire she'll sit,
    Nor play by the old green gate.

The little grave where her kitten sleeps
    Is beneath the chestnut tree.
But over her grave we may not weep,
    We know not where it may be.

Her empty bed, her idle ball,
    Will never see her more;
No gentle tap, no loving purr
    Is heard at the parlor door.

Another cat comes after her mice,
    A cat with a dirty face,
But she does not hunt as our darling did,
    Nor play with her graceful ease.

Her quiet paws walk the very hall
    Where Snowball used to play,
But she only hisses at the dogs our pet
    So bravely drove away.

She is useful and gentle, and does her best,
    But she is not fair to see,
And we cannot give her your place dear,
    Nor love her as we love thee.

A.S.

As the President finished reading the paper (which I ask leave to say is a true copy of one written by real girls once upon a time), there was applause, and then Mr. Snodgrass stood up to make a suggestion.

"Mr. President and gentlemen," he began, taking on a formal manner and tone, "I wish to suggest the admission of a new member—one who fully deserves the honor, would be deeply grateful for it, and would add greatly to the spirit of the club, the literary quality of the paper, and would be very fun and nice. I suggest Mr. Theodore Laurence as an honorary member of the P. C. Come now, do have him."

Jo's sudden change of tone made the girls laugh, but all looked rather uncertain, and no one said a word as Snodgrass sat down.

"We'll put it to a vote," said the President. "All in favor of this motion please show it by saying, 'Aye'."

A loud response from Snodgrass, followed, to everybody's surprise, by a timid one from Beth.

"Those against say, 'No'."

Meg and Amy were against it, and Mr. Winkle stood up to say with great formality, "We don't want any boys, they only joke and make noise. This is a ladies' club, and we wish to be private and proper."

"I'm afraid he'll laugh at our paper, and make fun of us afterward," said Pickwick, pulling the little curl on her forehead, as she always did when uncertain.

Up stood Snodgrass, very seriously. "Sir, I give you my word as a gentleman, Laurie won't do anything of the sort. He likes to write, and he'll bring a higher quality to our contributions and keep us from being overly sentimental, don't you see? We can do so little for him, and he does so much for us, I think the least we can do is to offer him a place here, and make him welcome if he comes."

This clever reference to kindness received brought Tupman to his feet, looking as if he had made up his mind completely.

"Yes, we ought to do it, even if we are unsure. I say he may come, and his grandpa, too, if he likes."

This enthusiastic outburst from Beth surprised the club, and Jo left her seat to shake hands approvingly. "Now then, vote again. Everybody remember it's our Laurie, and say, 'Aye!'" cried Snodgrass excitedly.

"Aye! Aye! Aye!" replied three voices at once.

"Good! Wonderful! Now, since there's nothing like acting quickly, as Winkle wisely says, allow me to present the new member." And, to the shock of the rest of the club, Jo threw open the door of the closet, and revealed Laurie sitting on a rag bag, flushed and bursting with suppressed laughter.

"You rogue! You traitor! Jo, how could you?" cried the three girls, as Snodgrass led her friend triumphantly out, and quickly producing both a chair and a badge, settled him in right away.

"The boldness of you two troublemakers is amazing," began Mr. Pickwick, trying to put on a stern frown and only managing a friendly smile. But the new member was equal to the occasion, and standing, with a grateful bow to the Chair, said in the most charming manner, "Mr. President and ladies—I beg pardon, gentlemen—allow me to introduce myself as Sam Weller, the very humble servant of the club."

"Good! Good!" cried Jo, banging with the handle of the old warming pan on which she leaned.

"My faithful friend and noble supporter," continued Laurie with a wave of the hand, "who has so flatteringly presented me, is not to be blamed for the sneaky plan of tonight. I thought it up, and she only agreed after a lot of persuading."

"Come now, don't put it all on yourself. You know I suggested the closet," interrupted Snodgrass, who was enjoying the joke tremendously.

"Never mind what she says. I'm the one who did it, sir," said the new member, with a nod to Mr. Pickwick. "But on my honor, I never will do so again, and from now on I will devote myself to the interests of this wonderful club."

"Hear! Hear!" cried Jo, banging the lid of the warming pan like a cymbal.

"Go on, go on!" added Winkle and Tupman, while the President bowed graciously.

"I merely wish to say, that as a small token of my gratitude for the honor done me, and as a way of encouraging friendly relations between neighboring nations, I have set up a post office in the hedge in the lower corner of the garden, a fine, spacious construction with locks on the doors and every convenience for the mail, as well as for the females, if I may use the expression. It's the old martin house, but I've blocked up the door and made the roof open, so it will hold all kinds of things, and save our precious time. Letters, stories, books, and bundles can be passed through there, and as each nation has a key, it will be wonderfully convenient, I think. Allow me to present the club key, and with many thanks for your acceptance of me, I take my seat."

Great applause as Mr. Weller put a little key on the table and sat down, the warming pan banged and waved wildly, and it was some time before order could be restored. A long discussion followed, and everyone surprised the others, because everyone did her best. So it was an unusually lively meeting, and did not end until a late hour, when it broke up with three loud cheers for the new member.

No one ever regretted admitting Sam Weller, because a more dedicated, well-behaved, and cheerful member no club could have had. He certainly did add 'spirit' to the meetings, and 'a higher quality' to the paper, because his speeches made everyone laugh and his contributions were excellent, being patriotic, classical, funny, or dramatic, but never overly sentimental. Jo considered them worthy of Bacon, Milton, or Shakespeare, and improved her own work with good results, she thought.

The Post Office was a wonderful little institution, and flourished greatly, because nearly as many strange things passed through it as through the real post office. Stories and scarves, poetry and pickles, garden seeds and long letters, music and gingerbread, rubber boots, invitations, scoldings, and puppies. The old gentleman liked the fun, and entertained himself by sending odd packages, mysterious messages, and funny notes, and his gardener, who had fallen in love with Hannah's charms, actually sent a love letter through Jo's care. How they laughed when the secret came out, never imagining how many love letters that little post office would hold in the years to come.
